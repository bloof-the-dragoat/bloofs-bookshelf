
## Chapter 5: That Dangerous Kind of Quiet

> [!warning] Content note Aftermath of violence, alcohol impairment, drinking against medical instructions, intimacy while intoxicated, traumatic recollections, and emerging possessiveness.

Getting out of the car required more planning than getting into it.

I unfastened the belt, turned toward the door, and discovered that my knee had developed an opinion about the whole arrangement. Voeren waited outside with her jacket over one arm and the tequila in her hand.

“Need a minute?”

“I’m negotiating.”

“With?”

I pointed down.

“Let me know who wins.”

I laughed, which hurt, which made me laugh again because apparently that was what I did now. Voeren watched with a smile she wasn’t doing much to hide.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Your nothing face is terrible.”

“Been a difficult evening for it.”

She set the bottle on the roof long enough to offer her arm. I took it, got my feet underneath me, and stood without doing anything too embarrassing. Then the curb turned out to be farther away than I expected, and her hand steadied my elbow.

“I knew it was there,” I said.

“Of course.”

“Hostile architecture.”

She laughed and retrieved the bottle. We reached the door slowly. A small light beside the frame changed when she unlocked it, and she tapped a panel just inside. The security system gave a quiet confirmation, and warm light reached across the entryway.

“Come in,” she said.

I did.

A pair of climbing shoes sat under a narrow bench, one tipped on its side, and a bag beside them carried a chalky handprint across the flap. Farther in, a drum practice pad occupied one end of a table, sticks laid across it as if she’d meant to come straight back.

I liked the house immediately. That was inconvenient. I’d been hoping to distribute my enthusiasm more evenly.

Voeren hung her jacket on a hook by the door and caught me looking at the sticks.

“No demonstrations tonight.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You looked interested.”

“I am interested.”

Her hand stayed on the jacket a moment longer than it needed to. Then she smiled and tipped her head toward the living room.

“Come on. Shoes off if you want. Couch is through there.”

The couch looked as if someone actually used it. One cushion sat lower than the others, and a folded blanket lay along an arm beside a remote and a second pair of drumsticks.

“Do you put those down everywhere?” I asked.

“Only places I intend to sit.”

“That explains the car.”

“Exactly.”

I bent toward my boots, felt my back object, and straightened again. Voeren looked at me.

“I can—”

“I know,” I said. “Wasn’t the question.”

Her grin spread. “Learning.”

She waited while I sat on the bench, then helped loosen the laces when my fingers stopped cooperating. I pulled the boots off myself and set them together under the edge. It was such an ordinary thing to do in someone’s house that I stared at them for a second.

“Spade?”

I looked up. “Yeah.”

“You’ve gone somewhere.”

She wasn’t smiling now, but she wasn’t pressing either.

“Just tired.”

“All right.”

She set the tequila on a side table and went to get water. I listened to cupboard doors, the tap, a glass touching the counter. No shouting. No furniture breaking. Somewhere beyond the entryway, something in the house clicked and settled.

Voeren returned with two glasses. I took mine in both hands.

“Food’s still on the way,” she said. “And I’ve got clothes, if you want to get out of those.”

I looked down. The cream tee had dried stiff in places.

“Yeah,” I said. “Please.”

---

She brought me a black T-shirt and a pair of sweats, showed me the bathroom, and left me to it.

Removing the knife took longer than changing the trousers. I unfastened the sheath from my belt and put it down beside my folded overshirt, and my hand stayed on it for a moment. There was blood at the mouth of the sheath. I turned it away.

The sweats were the easy part. I tightened the drawstring twice before trusting it, and the legs still gathered around my ankles. Nobody needed to know how long it took my knee to bend enough to step into them.

The cream tee was a different problem.

I took the hem in both hands and my right palm objected immediately. The cuts across my fingers pulled open a little under the pressure. I tried to lift with my forearms instead, got the fabric to my ribs, and felt the whole left side of my torso tighten around a bruise I had been politely ignoring since the car. I stopped with the tee bunched under my arms and tried the other side. That pulled my back. My lower back seized again, the way it had in the bar, and I stood hunched over the sink with my elbows trapped and my breath held, looking at myself in the mirror.

The dried blood had stiffened the hem. It refused to travel anywhere I wanted it to go.

_You are twenty-four years old._

I tried a third time. The collar caught under my chin and dragged against my throat, and I made a noise I would have denied under oath. I let go. The tee dropped back into place.

My neck looked worse than I wanted it to. Fresh color had spread over the faded marks, and none of it was going to be improved by pulling a shirt across it. I stood there for a while.

Then I opened the door a few inches.

“Voeren?”

Her footsteps arrived faster than I had expected.

“Everything all right?”

“Yes.” I looked at the floor. “Mostly.”

“Mostly?”

“I can’t get my shirt off.”

There was a pause on the other side of the door. When she spoke again, her voice was suspiciously even.

“Your ribs?”

“And my back. And my hands.”

“Okay.” The word was gentle. “Can I come in?”

“Yes. Please. Before I make it worse.”

She pushed the door open and took in all of it at once: me, the sink, the half-lifted hem, the way I was holding my arm. Her mouth pressed together very hard.

“Don’t,” I said.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’re being loud with your face.”

Her laugh escaped. She turned it into a cough and moved in front of me.

“Okay. Hold still.”

“I am holding still.”

“You’re vibrating a little.”

“That’s the back.”

“Sure it is.” She raised her hands, then stopped short of the hem. “Can I?”

The question was ordinary. It shouldn’t have done anything to my stomach.

“Yes.”

Her fingers found the stiff edge of the tee, careful of the blood, and began to lift. Cool air touched my stomach first, then my ribs. She had lifted this shirt before. I knew that, and she knew it, and we both seemed to know the other knew it, because neither of us looked up.

The swelling under my ribs was darker than it had been in the car. Her fingers paused beside it.

“Tell me when it pulls.”

“It’s pulling.”

“Right now?”

“A little.”

She waited. “Arms up. Slowly. Only as far as you can.”

I raised my left arm. It stopped at about shoulder height with a hot line along my side, and I breathed through it. Voeren lifted the hem under my arm instead, taking the weight of the fabric herself. The shirt came up in careful increments, over my ribs and past my chest. Her knuckles brushed my skin, and a moment later they did it again, and I was nearly certain the second time was on purpose. I looked at the tiles very hard.

“There’s the arm,” she said. “Other one.”

I got it out slowly. She eased the collar over my head with both hands, careful around my throat, and it came away with a soft tug and a sting I couldn’t stop from showing in my face. She stopped immediately.

“Okay?”

“Yeah.”

“You made a face.”

“The face is a separate issue.”

She set the ruined tee on the counter. When she turned back, I was standing there in borrowed sweats with my arms crossed in front of me at a speed that implied I had practiced. The bruises ran along my ribs in dark, mottled bands, and the ones on my neck were darker still. I hadn’t looked at myself properly since the hospital. I had been busy being fine.

Voeren looked at them for a long moment. Then she looked at my face.

“You’re red.”

“I’m injured.”

“Across your whole face?”

“It’s a very thorough injury.”

Her smile started at one corner of her mouth and kept going. “All the way to your ears.”

“Stop looking at my ears.”

“Hard not to. They’re working so hard.”

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out. I tried again.

“You’re enjoying this.”

“Very much.”

The honesty hit harder than the teasing. I turned toward the mirror frame and saw color going down my throat.

She picked up the black shirt and shook it out. Then, more quietly: “Can you turn? I want to look at your back before this goes on.”

That was reasonable. A medical observation. I turned, and felt the bathroom light settle on my shoulders, and then her gaze, and I became aware of the exact distance between us in inches.

“You’re holding yourself tight here.”

Her fingertip hovered above the muscle low on my back. It didn’t touch. I could feel the warmth of her hand anyway.

“I told you.”

“You did.” Her voice had dropped. “Could’ve said it was a nice back, too.”

My brain stalled. “What?”

“Good shoulders. Clean line.” She sounded perfectly composed, which was somehow worse. “Shame the ribs are interfering.”

“That is not a thing people say.”

“I just did.”

“About a person who is currently—” I gestured vaguely at all of myself. “Colored like this.”

“The bruises aren’t what I’m looking at.”

I did not know what to do with my hands. I had a lifetime of practice with retorts, and all of them had been replaced by a loud white hum.

“Thank you,” I said, much too formally.

Behind me, Voeren made a sound of pure joy. “You’re welcome.”

“Don’t laugh.”

“I’m not laughing.”

“You are. With your entire body.”

“Yeah.” She didn’t deny it. “Sorry. I can’t help it. You’re so—”

She stopped. I turned my head, not quite enough to see her fully.

“I’m so what?”

“Cute.”

My whole body heated.

“I’m not cute. I’m competent.”

“You can be both.”

“I’m armed.”

“Adorably.”

I made a noise. It wasn’t a word, and it wasn’t dignified, and it came out of me entirely without permission.

Voeren pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth, shoulders shaking. I covered my face with one hand and thought seriously about the structural integrity of the bathroom floor.

“Sorry,” she managed eventually. “Arms.”

I lowered them slowly, ribs tight. She worked the black shirt over my head with exaggerated care, guiding the collar past my throat and my arms through the sleeves one at a time. The fabric dropped past my hips and settled, soft and enormous. It smelled of clean laundry and, faintly, of her.

I looked down at myself. “This is mostly shirt.”

“Yeah.”

“I might get lost.”

“I’ll send a search party.”

When I looked up, she was close, and her hand was still resting against the fabric at my side. She seemed to notice at the same moment I did. She didn’t take it away. Her eyes dropped once toward the sweats gathered at my ankles, then returned to my face with a speed that suggested she had told them to. She smoothed the fabric lightly, near the bruise, and something warm went through me again, uninvited and entirely unrelated to the pain.

_Not now._

“There,” she said. “Better?”

“Yes.”

“Still red.”

“I’m aware.”

“I like it.”

I stared at the sink. “Don’t say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you’re keeping it.”

Her smile softened without leaving. “Can’t promise.”

I folded my original clothes around the small things from my pockets, including the discharge instructions, because my hands needed a job. When I carried the bundle out with the sheathed knife resting on top, Voeren followed a step behind, still smiling. She cleared a place on a chair near the couch. I set everything down together, knife and sheath tucked against the folded trousers, and lowered myself onto a cushion.

Voeren sat beside me. Not at the other end.

I looked at the space between us, then at her.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“Is my *nothing* face terrible?”

“Mine’s worse.”

“Getting an excellent view of it.”

I laughed softly. She handed me the water, then held up a cushion. “Behind your back?”

I nodded and leaned forward enough to let her settle it, and when I sat again the relief was immediate.

“Oh.”

“Better?”

“Absolutely.”

“Good.”

The answer came quickly. I looked at her, and she smiled into her glass.

---

The house had the particular, dangerous quiet of somewhere nobody was expected to interrupt.

I took a drink of water and let my gaze wander, because it was easier than letting it stay where it wanted to. The drum pad. The climbing bag with its chalky handprint. One mug on the counter beyond the passage. One toothbrush in the cup by the bathroom sink, which I had noticed and told myself I hadn’t. None of it belonged to anyone else.

I looked at her. Voeren had rested an arm along the back of the couch, close enough that her fingers nearly touched my shoulder.

“You’re doing it again,” she said.

“What?”

“Thinking loudly.”

“I’m not.”

“Spade.”

“I’m trying to work something out.”

“Out loud would be more efficient.”

I turned the glass in my hands.

“How are you not taken?”

Her eyebrows rose. “Excuse me?”

“I’m not asking about tonight.” I felt my face start to warm and pressed on anyway, because I had already started. “You’re funny. You have a house with climbing shoes in it. You pick movies specifically so you can argue about them. It doesn’t add up.”

She laughed softly. “You sound offended on my behalf.”

“A little.”

“Should I be flattered?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Go on, then. Be offended. I’m interested.”

“No. You’ll make it difficult.”

“I haven’t done anything.”

“You’re looking at me.”

“Very unreasonable.”

“It is when you do it like that.”

She didn’t look away. I was beginning to suspect she enjoyed watching me try to survive her attention.

“Relationships get complicated,” she said after a moment.

“Scheduling?”

“Among other things.”

I waited. She turned her own glass between her hands.

“Sometimes people like the idea better.”

“Of you?”

She shrugged, small and almost casual. I looked at the practice pad and the climbing bag near the door, then back at her.

“That seems like a disappointing use of an actual person.”

Her hands stopped. I hadn’t meant to say it quite like that, and I started to explain, but she shook her head.

“No. Keep going.”

I rubbed my thumb against the glass.

“You’re funny. You listen. You’ve got things you care about.” I met her eyes. “And you’re beautiful.”

Voeren forgot to move. The word stayed in the room. I felt my face heat, but I didn’t take it back.

After a moment she set her glass down.

“Spade.”

“That one was deliberate.”

Her mouth opened, then closed again. I smiled before I could lose my nerve.

“There. You do it too.”

She laughed, quieter this time, and looked down at her hands. “Yeah. Apparently.”

The delivery notification sounded before either of us found another sentence.

---

The food arrived in paper bags that made the whole room smell of ginger and fried dumplings. Voeren put the containers on the low table and handed me the noodles I’d finally admitted to wanting in the car. She took her own, stole a dumpling before sitting, and pointed at the display.

“Movie?”

“Something you like.”

“That’s a dangerous amount of trust.”

“I’ve been warned.”

She found _The Last Star of Avel_. The cover showed a woman holding a sword beneath an enormous moon while a man beside her looked concerned about something outside the picture.

“Fantasy,” I said.

“Technically.”

“Why technically?”

“You’ll see.”

We got through the opening battle before I put my fork down.

“Why did they defend that?”

“The bridge?”

“There’s nothing behind it.”

“Pride.”

“There isn’t even a road.”

“Very proud bridge.”

I looked at her. She was already grinning.

“Oh, you picked this on purpose.”

“You said something I like.”

“You like arguing about it.”

“Beginning to understand me.”

We ate closer together than the arrangement of the couch required. Her knee touched mine, and neither of us moved it. On the display, the concerned man announced that he would cross the kingdom for the sword woman.

“He’s been standing beside her for ten minutes,” I said.

“Still getting his bearings.”

“He needs a map.”

“He needs your harbor master.”

I laughed hard enough to regret it. Voeren set down her container and waited until I’d settled.

“All right?”

“Yes. Stop being funny.”

“You started.”

She picked up her food again without turning the wince into the end of the conversation. I liked that. I liked far too many things.

---

After we’d eaten most of the food, Voeren gathered the containers.

“Leaving those,” she said when I started to rise.

“I can help.”

“Tomorrow you can criticize my organizational system.”

“You have one?”

“Everything’s somewhere.”

“Strong foundation.”

She took the leftovers to the kitchen, returned with fresh water, and tugged at the hem of her tank. “Going to change. Need anything?”

I looked up at her. “Come back.”

Her hand stopped against the fabric. Then she smiled, without teasing me for how quickly I’d said it.

“Yeah. Can do that.”

I watched the film until I heard her footsteps again. She returned in a faded T-shirt and gray sweats. That should have made things easier, and it did not. The shirt sat comfortably across her shoulders, and her hair had been pushed back badly enough that one section had already escaped. There was nothing deliberate about the way she stood in the doorway. I liked it so much I forgot to be discreet.

“What?” she asked.

“Just looking.”

“I noticed.”

“You said I could.”

Her smile arrived slowly. “So I did.”

She sat beside me, nearer than before, and this time I let my shoulder rest against hers. On the display, the sword woman was confessing her love in a speech that appeared to have several endings. Voeren listened for a while.

“You’d write something better.”

“You haven’t read anything I’ve written.”

“Then I guess you’ll have to fix that.”

“You’re collecting a lot of things I apparently have to do.”

“Looking forward to every one.”

I glanced over. She wasn’t watching the film. Neither was I.

---

My back got tired before the characters finished discussing the prophecy. I shifted once, then again.

“Cushion not doing it?” Voeren asked.

“It’s doing its best.”

“Want to stretch out?”

I considered the available couch. Then her. “Can I?”

She followed my gaze. “Oh.”

I nearly changed my mind. Then she moved the remote, settled back, and patted her thigh.

“Yeah.”

Getting there wasn’t elegant. I turned carefully, dragged the blanket out from under my heel, and lowered my head onto her lap with more planning than the gesture deserved. Voeren went still.

“This all right?”

Her laugh came out on a breath. “Very.”

I settled with my knees slightly bent and the cushion supporting my back, and the room stopped asking me to hold myself upright.

“Oh, that’s unfair.”

“What is?”

“You’re comfortable.”

“Sorry.”

“No, you’re not.”

“Not even slightly.”

I smiled up at her. Her hand settled near my shoulder, and I leaned into it before she could second-guess the gesture. The film continued without us. Someone betrayed someone else, and I’d missed enough dialogue that I wasn’t sure whether I was meant to be surprised.

Voeren touched the cuff of the borrowed shirt. “Looks good on you.”

“It’s mostly shirt.”

“I like it.”

“Your shirt?”

“*On you*.”

My face heated. I turned toward the display, smiling. After a while her fingers moved lightly over my sleeve, and every so often they stopped, and I looked up to find her watching me.

“You keep doing that,” I said.

“What?”

“Checking I’m still here.”

Her hand rested against my shoulder. “I know you’re here.”

“Then what?”

For a moment she didn’t answer.

“I’m trying not to get used to it.”

I smiled. She didn’t. Then her thumb moved once against the fabric, and the film supplied a sudden burst of music. I looked back at the screen, but the sentence stayed with me.

---

Voeren reached for the tequila during a scene in which the concerned man finally acquired a horse. I made a protesting sound.

She looked down. “What?”

“I wanted some.”

“You’re horizontal.”

“Not a moral failing.”

“No. Quite fond of it.”

I looked at her. She lifted the bottle.

“Well then. Take it from me.”

She winked and took a small drink. I thought she meant the bottle, for perhaps half a second. Then I pushed myself upright, caught her chin lightly, and kissed her before I had time to assemble a reason not to.

It was brief and clumsy. When I drew back, she hadn’t moved. A drop of tequila ran down the side of the bottle onto her hand.

“I win,” I said, and giggled.

Then the rest of my brain arrived.

“Oh my God.”

Voeren blinked.

“Oh my God. I—”

I got my feet onto the floor too quickly. My knee protested, my back followed, and I stood anyway, hands suddenly impossible to put anywhere.

“I didn’t think. I didn’t mean—”

Her mouth started to curve, which made it worse.

“I mean, I did mean— Not like—”

“Spade.”

“I’m so sorry. I’ll just—”

“Hey.”

I stopped. She set the bottle down and leaned forward from the couch. Her smile was still there.

“Do you want to leave?”

“No.”

The answer came out immediately. Her fingers moved then, hooking two of them into a fold of the sweatpants at my hip, so lightly that the fabric barely shifted.

_God. I’d just kissed her, and somehow this was worse. Two fingers in my sweatpants, and I wanted her to leave them there._

_Do not look at her hand again._

I looked at her hand again. Then I looked at her. The fabric barely pulled, and I could have stepped back. I didn’t. Her grip was the gentlest thing in the room, loose enough that the slightest movement would have freed me, and she was watching my face as if she wanted me to notice how easy it would be.

“Voeren.”

“Yeah?”

“You’re holding my pants.”

“I’m holding the _fabric_. Very different.”

“Is it?”

“Pretty sure.” Her thumb moved once, a small, absent motion against the hem. “You can leave,” she said. “I’d rather you stayed.”

I swallowed. The embarrassment hadn’t gone anywhere, but something steadier had moved in underneath it.

“I don’t want to leave.”

“Good.”

She released the fabric, and the absence of her fingers was louder than I’d have liked. Then she offered the same hand, palm up. I took it and sat beside her, still trying to work out how one body could contain this much embarrassment without assistance.

“I was hoping you’d kiss me,” she said. “About an hour ago, actually. Didn’t expect you to turn it into a competition.”

“You were?”

“Yes.”

“Oh.”

“Yes, oh.”

I let out a breath that almost became another laugh. “I’m sorry I surprised you.” Her smile faltered, and I added quickly, “Not that I kissed you. Those are different things.”

Her eyes returned to mine. “Good. Because I liked the second one.”

I looked down at our hands. Hers was warm around mine, her grip loose enough that I could move. I turned my hand properly into hers.

“I wanted to.”

“I noticed.”

“No. Before. At the bar, in the car. I kept thinking about it.”

She stared at me long enough that I started to wonder whether I’d finally said too much.

Then she laughed, quiet and disbelieving. “You could’ve told me.”

“I thought I was being obvious.”

“You were making me hope.”

The answer caught me. I looked at her face, at the smile she hadn’t quite recovered.

“I wanted you to.”

She set her free hand on the cushion between us. “Can I kiss you back?”

“Yes.”

There was nothing clever in the answer, and no attempt to make it smaller. She leaned toward me, and I met her halfway. For a few seconds I forgot the film, the room, and the effort I’d been putting into appearing like someone who knew what they were doing.

When we drew apart, her forehead rested lightly against mine. She went quiet.

“Please,” she said. “Tell me you’re not sorry.”

I opened my eyes. There was no joke waiting beneath it.

Too many answers arrived at once. I was sorry I hadn’t asked. Sorry I’d startled her. Sorry my shirt was on a chair with blood on it and I still didn’t know how to think about the man on the floor. I wasn’t sorry I’d wanted to kiss her. I had wanted that before the tequila, and I knew that much, even if it didn’t settle everything else.

“No,” I said. “_God_, no. I’m not sorry I wanted to.”

She closed her eyes for a moment. The relief on her face was so immediate that it made my chest ache. I squeezed her hand.

“Was that clear enough?”

“Yeah.” She smiled. “I just liked hearing you say it.”

On the display, the sword woman finally interrupted the speech. Voeren reached for the remote without looking and paused the film. Neither of us complained.

---

“You’ve stopped talking,” she said.

“Busy.”

“Doing what?”

I looked at her mouth, then back at her eyes. She laughed softly.

“Oh.”

I smiled, and it felt different when I didn’t immediately try to hide it. This time I leaned toward her first. She met me with a hand resting lightly against my cheek, and the kiss ended only because the angle pulled at my ribs. I drew back with a sharp breath.

Voeren stopped. “Shit. Your side?”

“Still attached.”

“Not what I asked.”

“Still dramatic.”

She looked at the awkward way I’d folded myself toward her. “Come here.”

I started to shift closer. She slipped an arm behind my back, then paused.

“Can I lift you?”

I looked at her. Her grin spread.

“That face.”

“What face?”

“That one.”

“I’m considering the logistics.”

“I’ve got those.”

I nodded. She stood with me in her arms as if the movement required no particular thought. I caught her shoulders, startled into a laugh, and tightened my hold when the room shifted.

_God, she’s strong._

The thought had become a recurring problem.

“You all right?” Voeren asked.

“Yes.”

“You’re very quiet.”

“I’m trying not to say something embarrassing.”

“Terrible waste of an opportunity.”

I laughed and let my head rest briefly against her shoulder. “Where are we going?”

“Somewhere you can lie down properly.”

“Good plan.”

She carried me past the paused display and into the passage. I watched her face instead of where we were going.

“You’re staring again.”

“You’re carrying me. I’m enjoying the view.”

Her next step slowed. She looked down, smiling so openly that I nearly forgot what I’d said.

“Keep talking like that.”

“Thought I was meant to rest.”

“Can make exceptions.”

---

Her bedroom was dim until she nudged a switch with her elbow. A bedside lamp came on, revealing a shirt over the back of a chair, a book facedown beside the bed, and a glass on the bedside table.

Voeren lowered me onto the mattress carefully, letting my feet touch the floor before easing the rest of my weight down. She kept an arm behind me until I was settled. The sudden absence of movement made me realize how tired I was.

“Better?” she asked.

I nodded. She looked at the sweats gathered around my ankles.

“Still winning against the drawstring?”

“Against considerable odds.”

“Proud of them.”

I smiled. She went to get water and came back with both glasses, setting mine within reach. I drank slowly while she moved the old glass aside, and when I looked up she was standing beside the bed, looking at me.

“What?”

She shook her head, smiling. “You’re here.”

“That’s the observation?”

“Apparently it’s taking me a minute.”

I put the water down and held out my hand. She took it and sat beside me, and I leaned into her before she had time to wonder whether I wanted that too. Her shoulder settled against mine, and she let out a long breath.

I studied the scar through her brow, softer in the lamplight than it had looked in the bar. Her hair was still refusing to stay where she’d pushed it.

“You don’t have to impress me anymore tonight.”

“Bad news. Had several demonstrations planned.”

“I’m starting a list.”

She laughed, and I smiled before I’d finished the joke. Then she turned toward me more fully.

“Spade. You can tell me you want me close.”

My face heated. “I thought I was making that obvious.”

“You are.” Her thumb moved over my knuckles. “I still want to hear it.”

I looked at our hands. There were several ways to answer that safely. I could make a joke. I could point out that she was already close. Instead I looked up.

“I want you close.”

Voeren’s expression changed before she could do anything about it.

“Good,” she said.

“And I want to kiss you again.”

Her smile returned slowly. “That one too.”

I touched her cheek first, partly because I wanted to and partly because I liked discovering that she would go quiet when I did. She leaned into my hand. When I kissed her, she smiled against me, and we drew apart before I had to find another uncomfortable angle.

“I’m not very good at this,” I admitted.

“Could’ve fooled me.”

“I don’t mean the—”

“I know.”

I laughed softly and rested my forehead against her shoulder. Her hand settled at the back of my head, light and still. For once neither of us rushed to fill the quiet.

---

We negotiated the pillows badly. I insisted I could arrange them, and Voeren watched me put one in a place that immediately made my back worse, then waited while I reconsidered my position on assistance.

“Not a word,” I said.

She held up both hands.

I laughed and moved too quickly, and the pain took the rest of the sound away. Voeren helped me settle without making a joke. When I could breathe comfortably again, she moved the pillow a little lower and waited. I nodded.

“Yeah. That.”

She sat beside me, and the room went quiet. I looked toward the doorway.

For an instant I wasn’t looking at the doorway. I was back on the floor of the bar, his weight across my chest and his face against my shoulder.

Then the knife, slick and shaking in my hand.

I blinked. The doorway came back.

Voeren noticed. “Hey.”

“I’m here.”

“I know.”

She didn’t ask where I’d gone, and I wasn’t sure I could have explained. The mattress dipped as she shifted nearer, but she left the space between us open. I looked at her hand resting on the blanket.

“Can you—”

She waited. I reached for her, and she took my hand without asking me to finish. I let out a breath.

“I don’t want to think about it yet.”

“All right.”

“It’s still there.”

“I know.”

I looked at her. There was nothing in her face suggesting she could make it disappear, and I liked that she didn’t try.

After a while I moved over, carefully enough not to undo the work with the pillows, and lifted the edge of the blanket.

“Here?” Voeren asked.

“Unless you’re planning to make me ask again.”

Her smile returned. “Could.”

“Cruel.”

“Tempting.”

But she settled beside me, leaving room until I closed it myself. My head found her shoulder. Her arm rested around me without pressing against my ribs, and I put my hand over hers. I could feel her breathing, and it helped.

---

After a while, she said, “I like having you here.”

“I noticed.”

“I mean here. Not just tonight.”

I lifted my head. She was looking at the ceiling. There was too much in that sentence to answer without knowing which part she meant. Then Voeren turned toward me and smiled, almost casually.

“You make good company.”

“You’ve only had the introductory offer.”

“Keeping it.”

I smiled back, but the earlier sentence stayed with me.

_Not just tonight._

I wanted to ask what she was imagining. I wanted, more immediately, to remain in the place I’d found beside her.

“I want more time with you,” I said.

Her attention returned to my face. “Yeah?”

“Yes.” I tightened my hand around hers. “I just don’t know what all of this means yet.”

For a moment her expression didn’t change. Then her thumb moved against my fingers.

“All right.”

“Can we work out the rest tomorrow?”

She looked down at our hands, and I waited.

“Yeah,” she said at last.

I touched her cheek, and her eyes came back to mine. “That wasn’t me asking you to stop wanting me here.”

Her breath left her in a small laugh. “Good.”

I kissed her once, gently, and settled against her shoulder again. She held me a fraction closer.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

I couldn’t tell whether she was agreeing or reminding herself.

---

The house made small sounds around us. A pipe clicked somewhere beyond the wall. Outside, a vehicle passed and was gone. There was no film playing now, no conversation to keep ahead of what had happened. My knee still ached, my palms throbbed when I moved my fingers, and the room felt unsteady if I lifted my head too quickly.

I was still drunk.

“Comfortable?” Voeren asked, looking down at me.

“Unfairly.”

“Good.”

I closed my eyes, then opened them again.

“Voeren?”

“Yeah?”

“I’m glad I came.”

Her hand stopped against my shoulder. Then she drew me a little closer, carefully enough that nothing hurt.

“Me too.”

I believed her.

There was still blood on the clothes I’d left in the other room. There were questions waiting for morning, and Monday waiting after that. None of it had gone away. But her hand rested over mine, and when I moved closer, she made room.

I was beginning to understand that the difficult part might not be getting her to want me there.

It might be how much I wanted to stay.
