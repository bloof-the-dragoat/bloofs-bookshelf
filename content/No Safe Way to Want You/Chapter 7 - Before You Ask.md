
# Chapter 7: Before You Ask

Sunday morning, I woke before my alarm and lay still while I took inventory.

My ribs ached, but the ache had a shape I recognized. My knee only complained when I bent it. The bruising at my throat had faded to a dull soreness when I swallowed. My palms stung when I flexed them. Still, I'd slept through the night, the first time in a week I could say that.

Voeren's shirt was twisted around my waist. I untangled it and lay there a moment longer than necessary. Then my phone lit on the nightstand.

> _Leaving in 20. Hungry?_

I read it twice before I finished smiling.

> _Always_

I got up.

I took my time. I washed my face and changed the dressing on my palm, then stood in front of the wardrobe with the door open. The sensible choice was easy: black trousers and a plain shirt that didn't pull when I raised my arms. I laid them on the bed, looked at them, and put the shirt back.

What I pulled out instead was a fitted white top with a faded silver print, cut low enough at the neck to show every bruise. I held it up and put it back too. Then I took out the black zip hoodie I'd worn thin at the cuffs. It was heavy enough to hide the dressing and sit loose over my ribs, and I could close it as high as my throat if I needed to. I laid the white top beneath it.

The trousers came next: wide, black, with straps and buckles at the thighs. I'd bought them for the pockets, and I told myself that was totally the reason I grabbed them. I looped the chain through my belt and let it hang, then dug through the dish on the shelf for the silver pieces I only wore on days I felt like myself. Two necklaces of different lengths. A ring for my left hand. A black headband to keep my hair off my face. My knee made its opinion known when I sat to lace the heavy boots, so I did them loosely, to spare my ribs.

I checked the mirror. It was comfortable, easy to move in, and it covered most of the damage. It was also very much what I'd wear on a day when I was thinking about someone in particular.

I took the headband off. Then I stood there with it in my hand and put it back on.

"This is not about her," I told the mirror.

The mirror looked at me with the expression of someone who had changed their shirt twice. I turned off the light before it could say anything else.

Her shirt still sat folded on the chair. I looked at it for a while before leaving it where it was. It didn't seem like something to bring along, and I wasn't ready to hand it back either.

My phone buzzed while I was lacing the second boot.

> _Downstairs_

I took longer than I should have on the stairs, pretending my ribs were responsible. When I came out, she was leaning against the car with two paper cups, the passenger door already open. She looked up. It was a very small pause. Her gaze went from my face to the silver at my throat, the chain at my hip, and the boots, and then back up again. The corner of her mouth moved before she smoothed it.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning."

"Nice outfit."

"It's what I had."

"Mm." She held out a cup. "Looks good on you. The chains especially."

I took it so I'd have something to do with my hands. The cup was still hot.

"Decaf," she added. "I checked."

"You checked."

"Medical instructions are medical instructions."

"That's the second time you've been responsible at me."

"Don't get used to it."

I got into the car. The belt caught my ribs on the way across, and I eased it into place while she watched the chain swing against my hip.

"What?" I asked.

"Nothing."

"Don't."

"Haven't said a word."

She started the car. The corner of her mouth had not smoothed out.

Breakfast was quick and unremarkable: sandwiches eaten in the car with the radio low. By the time she parked, I'd stopped noticing my ribs for several minutes at a time.

---

I'd kept my eyes on the road for most of the drive. That had mostly worked until she got out and walked around the front of the car.

She had on a burgundy tee with the sleeves cut off at the shoulder, the armholes hanging low enough to be intentional. Black fishnet sleeves ran from her wrists to just above her elbows. Two silver chains lay against her collarbone, one with a small dark pendant. Her trousers were black and strapped at the thighs, with a row of steel rings along one hip and a chain that swung loosely when she moved. Her boots laced almost to the knee.

I opened my door slowly, using the movement to give myself time.

"You're staring," Voeren said.

"I'm assessing."

"Right. And?"

"The boots have more laces than I've had hot dinners."

She laughed, pleased. "Took me ten minutes."

"It shows."

"Good." She stopped beside me with her hands in her pockets, close enough that I could smell the faint, clean scent of soap on her skin. She waited.

"The sleeves," I said.

"Mm?"

"I like them."

I hadn't planned on saying it. It came out in the same tone I'd use for a bridge design, flat and factual, and I felt my ears heat immediately.

Voeren's grin spread slowly. "Do you."

"They're well made."

"They're mesh, Spade."

"Well-structured mesh."

"You like my arms."

"I like the arrangement."

"The arrangement of my arms." 

"I'm— not answering that."

She clapped a hand over her mouth, but her eyes gave her away. When she'd recovered, she tilted her head and looked me over, slowly and deliberately, from the headband down to the chain at my hip and the heavy boots, and then back up again.

I felt it all the way down.

"My turn," she said.

"You already commented."

"Briefly. I was being polite." She stepped a little closer. "Can I do it properly?"

"There's a proper version?"

"Oh, absolutely."

I braced myself. "Go on."

"The hoodie." She nodded at it. "Oversized, black, a little worn at the cuffs. Looks like you've lived in it for years."

"I have."

"The silver." Her finger hovered near the necklaces, not touching. "Two lengths. Not matching on purpose."

"On purpose," I admitted.

"The ring."

I looked down at my left hand. "It's just a ring."

"Mm-hm." She took a breath, apparently deciding something. "And the headband."

"What about the headband?"

"I love the headband."

"It's a headband."

"It makes your face look like..." She stopped and visibly reconsidered. "Like you're about to say something sarcastic and then do something kind."

I hadn't expected that. I stood there with the car door still half open in my hand.

"That's very specific."

"I've had a lot of time to study it."

"Friday doesn't count as a lot of time."

"I've been efficient."

I couldn't think of a single reply. I closed the car door instead, with more force than it needed, and Voeren's shoulders shook.

"You went red," she said.

"I did *not*."

"The tips of your ears."

"They're cold."

"Of course."

"It's cool out."

"It's the middle of the day."

I turned toward the shop before I could say something worse. Behind me, her boots fell into step beside mine, along with a soft laugh she didn't bother to hide.

"Spade."

"Yes?"

"You wore the chain."

I looked straight ahead. "I wear it often."

"I believe you."

"I do."

"I said I believe you."

She was grinning so openly that I gave up. I let myself smile too, still not looking at her, and when we reached the shop she put a hand against the door and held it for me.

"After you," she said. "Wouldn't want to ruin the arrangement."

---

The music shop had a wall of guitars I wanted to look at and a woman beside me who had clearly anticipated the problem.

"Back room first," Voeren said.

"I was only looking."

"That's how it starts."

"You brought me here."

"And I'd like to reach the drums before closing."

I followed her past a row of amplifiers and looked back once. Then twice. She caught the second one.

"We can come back."

"I'm holding you to that."

She grinned and went to the counter. While she arranged the practice room, I wandered over to a display of picks. I didn't need any. I had dozens at home, scattered so that I could never find one while holding a guitar. I picked up a purple one anyway.

Voeren touched my elbow. "Ready?"

"Yes."

She looked at the pick, then at me.

"Not a word," I said.

"Haven't said anything."

"You're getting good at that."

---

The practice room was just big enough for a drum kit, a stool, and two chairs against the wall. Dark panels covered the walls, and a small window in the door showed a slice of the shop. Voeren hung her jacket on one chair. I lowered myself into the other. There was still a point where my lower back objected, but I knew where it was now, and I could sit without turning it into an event.

She took a pair of sticks from the holder and handed me a set of ear defenders from the shelf. Then she sat behind the kit and adjusted the stool, tested a pedal with her foot, and moved something beside the hi-hat by a fraction. I watched her work, and I didn't pretend I wasn't.

"What?" she asked.

"I like watching you get ready."

She paused with one stick halfway to the snare. It had come out more easily than I'd meant it to, and I decided not to take it back.

"It's the part nobody sees," I said. "You do it like it matters."

"It does matter." Her mouth curved. "Nobody's ever said they like watching it."

"Then you've been playing for the wrong audience."

"Maybe."

She put the defenders on and began.

The first bars were spare enough that I could follow them: a steady pulse, the snare returning sharp and clean, her right hand keeping time above it. Then she changed something underneath without disturbing anything, and my attention caught. Her shoulders stayed loose. She didn't hit everything as hard as she could, and some notes barely happened at all, tucked between the ones I was waiting for. When she crossed the toms, the movement was quick and deliberate and ended exactly where the beat needed her.

She was having a wonderful time. I could see it in the lift of her chin before a change, and in the grin when she caught my heel keeping time. I stopped trying to hide it. I tapped along openly, and her grin widened. Then she did something with her feet that made me lose the beat completely, and I laughed out loud.

When she finished, the last cymbal hung in the room until she silenced it. She lifted one side of the defenders.

"Well?"

I took mine off. "I knew you'd be good. I didn't know you'd look like that while you did it."

"Like what?"

"Like you've never been happier to be anywhere."

She went still for a second. Then she looked down at the sticks, and I saw her pleasure before she hid it.

"Okay," she said. "That's a good review."

"It's an honest one."

"Don't stop."

"You'll get a big head."

"Already got one." She pointed a stick at me. "Your turn."

"My turn to what?"

"Tell me something. You've watched me for ten minutes." She propped her feet on the kick pedal. "What do you play when nobody's around?"

I looked at the seam of my trousers. A week ago I'd have said _a bit of everything_ and let her stop there. But she was looking at me with real interest, the way she looked at things she liked, and I found I wanted to give her something.

"Slow things, mostly," I said. "I like having room to hear what the guitar's doing. I learned on a cheap one with a crack in the body. The strings sat so high off the neck that my fingers bled for a month."

"And you kept going."

"I was stubborn."

"I'd never have guessed."

"I also write my own songs. Badly. I sing over them, which is worse."

Voeren's whole face changed. "You sing?"

"Not well."

"I want to hear it."

"That's what everyone says before they hear it."

"Spade." She leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "Nobody's ever sung to me. I'm not going to criticize."

I hadn't known I'd been waiting for permission until I felt it. I looked at the guitars through the window in the door, the shop's lights shining off the finish.

"Maybe at my place," I said. "Not here."

"Is that an invitation?"

"It's a maybe."

"I'll take a maybe." She tried not to look pleased and failed. "I'm very good at maybes."

"Very persistent ones."

"Part of the training."

I laughed, and she looked at me as if it was the best thing she'd heard all day. I felt it land somewhere under my ribs, not in the bruised place.

"Come here," she said.

I looked at the stool. "I'm medically discouraged from discovering new ways to hurt myself."

"Then don't hit anything hard. Sit. I'll show you the grip."

That sounded manageable.

We traded places. The stool was warm from her, and the kit felt much larger with me behind it. Voeren handed me the sticks one at a time. I held them stiffly, as if they might go off.

"You expecting them to escape?"

"I don't know their intentions."

"Loosen up."

I did.

"One of them might escape now."

"We'll risk it."

She crouched beside the kit so her eyes were level with my hands. "Can I?"

"Yes."

Her fingers settled lightly over mine. She turned the stick a little, adjusted my thumb, and slid it back along my palm to the place where it balanced.

"Not a fist. Give it somewhere to move."

I tried the motion in the air. "Like that?"

"Yeah. That's it." She stood and pointed at the snare. "One light tap. Let it come back up."

I put the defenders on, and so did she. The first tap barely made a sound. Voeren lifted a hand, thumb and forefinger a little apart. _Slightly more._ The next tap rang properly, and I felt the stick lift on its own against my fingers.

"There you go."

I did it again. And again. 

Voeren moved behind me. I felt her there before I understood why: a faint warmth along my back, the shift of air. Then she leaned over my right shoulder to watch my hands. Her arm was nearly against mine, and her jaw was close enough that I could have turned my head and felt her breath. I kept my eyes on the snare, because looking anywhere else would have been a mistake.

The next tap came out uneven. The one after that landed far too hard and made me flinch.

She went quiet. Then, close to my ear, in a voice that was warm and thoroughly entertained:

"You nervous?"

I stopped playing. I could have lied, but there wasn't much point.

"Well," I said, "there's a cute person staring at me play drums for, like, the first time. So."

Voeren didn't answer at once. I turned my head slightly, as far as my neck allowed, and found her already watching me. She was close enough that I could see where her smile had gone soft at the edges. She wasn't teasing anymore, though she was trying to look as if she was.

"*Adorable*," she said.

"Don't make me say it twice. Please." The "please" came out far quieter than I meant it. 

"I wasn't going to ask for it twice." 

"That's a lie."

"A little." Her gaze dropped briefly to my mouth, then returned. "Keep going."

"With the drums?"

"Yeah." She didn't move away. "Or— whatever you want."

I looked at the snare because I had to. "*Whatever you want*" rung in my head a bit longer than it should have. It took me two tries to find the grip again, and she let me, steady and patient behind me, one hand resting beside mine on the edge of the drum. I could hear her breathing under the defenders. It was slightly faster than when she'd been playing.

I tapped out a small, slow rhythm and kept it even.

"That's good," she said quietly.

"Don't be nice."

"I'm not. It's good."

I tried my left hand, and the rhythm fell apart immediately. Voeren's laugh shook against my shoulder. I stopped and lifted one side of the defenders.

"Excellent teaching method."

"You looked so offended."

"My hands have worked together before. This is a recent disagreement."

"Slower."

"I was barely moving."

"Then you've got room."

I managed a few even taps before one hand rushed to meet the other. When I felt my palm start to ache, I stopped before I started gripping harder. Voeren noticed me flexing my fingers and straightened up, a little too slowly, I thought. The space where she'd been felt cooler.

"Enough?"

"Of my remarkable debut? Probably."

I offered the sticks. She took them without trying to persuade me into one more round, and her fingers stayed over mine a second longer than the handoff needed.

"Good grip by the end," she said.

"Very diplomatic."

"Terrible timing."

"There she is."

The half hour ended sooner than I wanted. I gathered the defenders while she put the sticks away, and neither of us was in a hurry to open the door.

In the shop, we moved along the guitars at my pace, and I found myself explaining a bridge design I disliked without checking whether she wanted to hear it. Halfway through, I noticed I was gesturing with my free hand and nearly stopped. Voeren was still listening. She watched my hands the way I'd watched hers.

"You get different when you talk about this," she said.

"Different how?"

"Louder." She smiled. "Happier. I like it."

I didn't have a clever answer, so I gave her the true one. "I like that you do."

She looked at me for a moment and then looked at the guitars, which gave me time to find my footing again. I paid for the purple pick. Nothing larger followed us out.

Outside, the air was cool after the practice room. I put the pick in my pocket and waited while she pulled on her jacket.

"That was good," I said. "You playing. Me playing, if you can call it that. All of it."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. I'd like to do it again."

Her smile came quickly and stayed. She finished settling her collar and held out her hand. I took it, and this time I laced my fingers through hers instead of letting them rest loosely. She noticed. She didn't say anything, but her thumb moved once across my knuckle.

"Somewhere to sit for a bit?" she asked.

"Please."

We started walking. After a few steps, I glanced at her.

"I do have another idea."

"Should I be worried?"

"Only about your professional reputation."

Voeren looked delighted. "Go on."

---

The arcade sat two streets over, wedged between a closed tailor's shop and a noodle place with a queue out the door.

Inside, it was all carpet in a pattern designed to hide spills and screens turned up to a pitch that made my ribs feel louder. Voeren stopped just past the door and turned a slow circle.

"This is the idea?"

"You asked if you should be worried."

"I did."

"There's a game at the back." I nodded toward it. "It has sticks."

She followed my gaze. Two padded drums were bolted to a platform under a screen of falling colored notes, the sticks tethered by cables. Voeren looked at it the way she'd looked at the kit in the shop, except there was less reverence in it and a great deal more suspicion.

"That's not drums."

"It's drums with consequences."

"I'll destroy it."

"There's a leaderboard. Be gentle."

She grinned, shrugged out of her jacket, and handed it to me. I hung it over my arm and found a spot against the railing where I could see her face.

The first round went badly. She hit the lit pads a fraction late, then a fraction early. The screen informed her in a cheerful font that her combo was broken. By the end of the first song she was glaring at it with her jaw set, and a pair of kids waiting behind her had started to whisper.

"Timing's off," she said.

"Or you are."

"I'm not _off_. I'm calibrated for actual drums."

"The screen disagrees."

"The screen is a liar." She cracked her neck and hit start again.

By the third song she'd found the lag and begun to compensate. A girl of about twelve counted her combos out loud. Two of her friends joined in, and then a man waiting for the claw machine. By the final chorus a small crowd had gathered, not staring but cheering, and Voeren's whole face had lit up. She finished with a flourish that nearly dropped a stick, and the screen flashed a number that put her third on the board.

She turned to me, breathing hard and delighted. "Did you see that?"

"I saw all of it."

"Third."

"Out of how many?"

"Don't." She was already typing initials into the prompt, and when I leaned to read them I saw she'd entered SPD.

"That's not your name."

"You counted the beat for me. That's worth credit."

"I barely said anything."

"You were _there_."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I said, "I'll pretend I earned it."

My own contribution was less dramatic. Across the aisle stood a row of ramps and bullseyes, and I took a ball and aimed it with the stillness that comes from years of sitting at a console. I rolled eight in a row into the forty ring. Voeren tried to match me and threw one so hard it cleared the bullseye entirely and bounced off the wall, much to the delight of the staff member sweeping nearby.

We pooled our tickets at the counter. The only thing worth having was a tiny plastic skull ring, black, with a small chip in one eye socket. Voeren looked at it, then at me.

"Can I?"

I held out my left hand. She slid it onto my thumb, where it fit well enough, and let her fingers rest there a moment longer than the ring needed.

"Terrible taste," she said.

"Not all the time." 

---

By the time we reached the food hall, my ribs had begun making themselves known again, a low ache that sharpened whenever I breathed too deeply.

I hadn't said anything. I didn't intend to. The noise in the hall was a living thing, a hundred conversations and clattering trays and a sound system fighting all of it, and I could feel my patience for the world thinning with every stall we passed. But I wanted to stay. I chose a noodle counter and ordered, and Voeren got dumplings from the stall beside it.

When we turned from the counters with our trays, her hand settled lightly at my elbow, and she started steering me toward a table near the far wall, tucked into a quiet corner. She'd noticed. Of course she had. It was a kind thing to do. It was also a decision she'd made without asking.

"I was going to sit by the window," I said.

"Yeah?" She stopped. "Is it quieter?"

"A bit. And the light's better."

"Then window."

She let go and followed me, and nothing in her face suggested she'd taken it as a correction. We took the table, and I let out a breath I hadn't noticed I was holding.

Voeren stole a noodle off my tray with the confidence of someone who had done it before. "Okay. Real question."

"Go on."

"What do you want?"

"From this?"

"From anything." She chewed. "Not like, a mission statement. Just what you'd pick if nobody was keeping score."

I considered the question while I ate. The honest answer was _I don't know_, and for a long time I would have said that and moved on. But the noise had thinned at our table, and she was looking at me as if she'd wait.

"I'd like to finish a story," I said. "One I've started, I mean. Properly. Not just the part I like."

"The one in the notebook?"

"Probably that one."

"What's it about?"

"A woman who argues with everyone." I turned a noodle with my fork. "And a man who never says what he wants."

"Sounds like someone I know."

"Which one?"

She grinned. "Both."

I laughed, and the ache in my ribs eased slightly.

"Your turn," I said.

"I want a room." She stirred her broth. "Soundproofed. Big enough for a kit and a couch. Somewhere nobody gets angry about the noise."

"That's a good answer."

"It's a small one."

"Small ones are the ones that happen."

Voeren looked at me for a moment, then looked at her bowl. When she glanced up again, her smile was a different kind, less bright and more private.

"Come on," I said, standing before I lost my nerve. "I'll show you my guitar."

Her eyebrows went up. "The maybe?"

"The maybe's a yes."

---

My apartment looked smaller with her in it.

Not in a bad way. Voeren stood just inside the door and took it in slowly: the lamp, the desk, the art supplies crowded along one wall, the frame hanging crooked above the shelf. She looked at the sketch I'd left propped against the wall and didn't touch it.

"Is this finished?"

"I haven't decided."

"Can I see?"

I took it down and held it so she could look. It was a figure half in shadow, the face unresolved, the hands done. She studied it with her head tilted.

"I like the hands."

"That's the only part I'm sure of."

"It shows."

I put it back and talked, which I hadn't planned on. I told her about the paints, why the lamp had to be warm, the drawer of gothic oddments I'd collected over the years. She asked a question about the PC, and then another about the way I'd set up the monitor, and I found myself explaining a cable-routing decision with more enthusiasm than anyone should have for cable routing. Voeren listened as if every word mattered.

Then she stopped beside the chair.

"That's mine."

Her shirt lay folded on the cushion, exactly where I'd left it that morning.

"I was going to return it."

"Uh-huh." She picked it up, turned it over, and smoothed a crease with her thumb. It had been slept in, and anyone who looked closely would know. "It's got a lot of character."

"It was a rough night."

"I'm sure." She held it a moment longer, then put it back on the chair rather than into my hands. "Keep it. Give it back when you're done with it."

"When do I stop being done with it?"

"I'll let you know. I liked seeing it on you." 

*Oh—*. I took the guitar down from its stand. It was far easier than responding.

My hands protested. The bandage on my right palm pulled against the neck, and my left fingertips had softened over days of not playing. I sat on the edge of the desk chair and tuned by ear, wincing at one string, while Voeren took the stool by the shelf and said nothing.

I played something slow. It wasn't good. The chord changes stuttered where my hand couldn't stretch, and I dropped a note I'd never missed before. I didn't stop. I hummed the melody through the second verse, then found the words and let them out, quiet and a little flat.

When I finished, the room felt very still.

"Well," I said. "That's what it sounds like."

"I don't have a complaint."

"You haven't heard it properly."

"I heard enough." Her voice was lower than usual. "I'd like to hear it properly."

"It's a long list of things you'd like."

"It's getting longer."

I set the guitar on its stand. My face was warm again, and I didn't try to hide it.

---

I went to the kitchenette for water. When I came back with two glasses, Voeren was standing at my desk with her head bowed. She had the notebook in both hands.

I stopped in the doorway.

I could see the page from where I stood. It was the last one I'd written, the kiss in the inn, the collar, the answer I'd crossed out and rewritten. She held it open with her thumb pressed against the margin, and she wasn't moving at all.

"Voeren."

She looked up. The color in her face went through three shades in about two seconds.

"I opened it," she said. "I just saw it and I read a line, and then I—" She stopped. "Didn't stop."

"No."

"I should have asked."

"Yes."

She didn't argue. She held the notebook out, open, and I made no move to take it.

The thing was, I did want her to read it. That was what unsettled me. Part of me wanted to snatch it away and part of me wanted to watch her turn the page, and the two feelings sat side by side in my chest with no sign of resolving. I'd written those pages for no one. And now, standing with water in my hands, I was surprised by how much I wanted her to like them.

"Can I— keep reading?" Voeren asked.

I crossed the room and set the glasses down. "You're asking now."

"I know. It's late."

"It's very late."

"But I'm asking."

I looked at her hands on the notebook, then at her face. She wasn't teasing. She was bracing herself for the answer.

"Yes," I said. "You can keep reading."

She nodded once, and her shoulders eased. She read in silence for a while. I sat on the arm of the chair and tried to look occupied by the glass in my hand. Whenever she turned a page, I felt it somewhere under my sternum.

"It's good," she said eventually.

"You don't have to say that."

"I'm not just saying it." She tapped the margin. "The argument at the bridge is good. She's so angry at him for being careful, and he can't see it."

"He's not a very observant man."

"He's terrible." She turned another page. "I like her."

"She's difficult."

"That's why."

She reached the last page and went quiet. I watched her read the line I'd crossed out and the one I'd written in its place, and I watched her mouth curve.

"_Are you going to make me ask?_" she said.

*Oh my god.*

My ears burned. "I planned that scene before any of this."

"I believe you."

*Kill me.*

"I swear I did."

"I said I believe you." She read the next few lines, and the curve in her mouth deepened. "Oh, she _smiles against his mouth_."

Okay. That was enough. I stood up too fast, meaning to take the notebook and put it somewhere safer. Meaning, honestly, to put myself somewhere safer. My boot caught the edge of the rug, and my bad knee gave out under me.

I didn't hit the floor.

Voeren had the notebook on the desk and her arm around my back before I'd finished falling. She'd caught me high, below the shoulder blades, careful of my ribs. When I came up against her, my right hand landed flat on her shoulder, bandage and all, and my left found the chain at her collarbone.

For a second, neither of us moved.

She was holding me close enough that I could feel her breathing, and I could see the small crease at the corner of her mouth, the scar over her right eye, and the exact color of her pale gray-green eyes, which I had somehow never noticed in the daylight. Her gaze dropped to my mouth. Then it came back up, and I realized that I'd done the same thing and that she'd seen it.

"Ribs?" she asked quietly.

"Fine." It came out thinner than I'd intended. "Knee."

"Still holding?"

"Mostly."

"I've got you."

I knew she did. That was the problem.

Her thumb moved once against my back, a small, unconscious motion. Then her mouth curved, and something mischievous came into her eyes.

"*Maybe you should write about this*."

I went completely, catastrophically red. I felt it climb my neck, across my cheeks, and all the way to the tips of my ears, and there was nowhere I could look that wasn't her. I didn't know if I wanted to die, or for her to pull me in closer.

Voeren didn't laugh. She didn't say anything else.

I waited for the second line, the one that would finish me off, and it didn't come. She just stood there with her arm around me, looking at my face with her head tilted slightly, as if she were memorizing it.

That was worse. Somehow that was *so* much worse.

"No teasing this time?" I managed.

"No." She said it so simply that my chest tightened. "Not about this."

"Why not?"

"Because I'd rather just look at you."

My heart did something unhelpful. I opened my mouth, and nothing useful came out, and she moved a fraction closer, close enough that I could feel the warmth of her breath. My hand curled against the chain at her throat. Her fingers spread across my back, and I stopped thinking about the notebook entirely.

The phone buzzed on the desk.

A second later, so did hers.

Neither of us moved. The buzzing went on for another half beat, and then Voeren breathed out slowly, not quite a laugh, and loosened her hold. She waited until she was sure my knee would take my weight before she let go. Even then, her hand lingered at my elbow.

"That'll be work," she said.

"It always is."

We both looked down at the desk.

> _Staff Sergeant Voeren. Sergeant Spade. Recovery element departs 0430 Monday. Briefing 0400. Report rested and fit for duty. Acknowledge receipt. Col. Stroud._

For a moment, neither of us moved. Then Voeren thumbed hers open, typed something, and set the phone down. "Acknowledged."

I read the message again. _Rested and fit for duty._ I was neither, and the doctor had said as much. Being on communications didn't change that.

I forcibly gathered myself. "I'll send Marlowe the hospital's paperwork tonight," I said. "So the restrictions are on file before the briefing."

"Good."

"They won't pull me. Comms doesn't need me on my feet."

"No." Voeren's voice was careful. "I'll be on your channel."

"I'll be on the other end."

"That's what I'm counting on."

I acknowledged the message. My thumb hovered over the screen a second longer than it needed to.

"Four in the morning," Voeren said.

"It's a terrible time."

"It's awful." She glanced at me. "We should both sleep."

"We should."

Neither of us made a move.

---

The light through the window had gone amber, and the room had the settling quality that comes when the day is nearly over. Voeren was still on the stool by the shelf, and I was still perched on the arm of the chair, her shirt folded on the cushion beside me.

She glanced at the time on her phone, then set it facedown on her knee.

"I should go."

I looked at the notebook on the desk. "I know."

Neither of us moved.

"I'd rather stay," she said.

I got up and crossed the room. When I held out my hand, she took it, and I drew her to her feet. She was close enough that I could feel the warmth of her through the fishnet at her wrists.

"I'd rather you did," I said. "But we have a four a.m. briefing, and I don't want to be something you fit in around it."

Her fingers tightened. "Me neither."

"I don't want it rushed."

"No."

"I'd like to have the whole evening. Not the part before we have to be somewhere."

"That's what I want too."

Voeren leaned her forehead against mine. For a while, we stood like that, breathing the same air.

I kissed her. It was longer than the one in the car. My hand found her jaw, and she turned into it, and for a moment I wasn't thinking about anything else. When we pulled apart, her eyes were bright and she was smiling in a way I hadn't seen before.

"Ask me again when we're back," I said.

"To stay?"

"Yeah."

Her smile came slowly. "I can do that."

She collected her jacket from the chair and shrugged into it. At the door, she turned and looked back at the room, at the lamp and the notebook and the guitar, and then at me.

"Hey, Spade."

"Yes?"

"Thanks for today."

"Thanks for coming."

She grinned, and it was the same grin from the shop. Then the door shut behind her, and her boots faded down the stairs.

I stood where I was for a while.

The apartment was quiet in the way I normally liked, but tonight the quiet felt wrong. It was the quiet of a room someone had just left, with the air still shifted from her being in it. I looked at the skull ring on my thumb, the shirt folded on the chair, and the notebook she'd set back on the desk. Everything was where it belonged. It didn't help.

_Four in the morning._

I thought about the briefing, which I could do from memory: the frequencies, the check-ins, the order the team would go in. Then I thought about the part I couldn't do from memory. Voeren would be at the front of that order, in a building I hadn't seen, while I sat in a room miles away with a headset and a screen. Her voice would come through the channel in pieces. And at some point, it might stop.

My hand had tightened on the back of the chair. I hadn't felt it happen.

The ache in my ribs sharpened as I breathed in, and I held that breath longer than I needed to. I could hear how quiet the room was. I could hear the small, stupid sound of my own pulse.

_Stop._

It wasn't a thought so much as an order, the kind I gave myself when a task started to run away from me. I let the breath out. I unclenched my hand and flexed my fingers until the bandage stopped pulling. Voeren was trained. She was good at this, better than I was at most things. Planning for the worst case was my job, which meant that was all I needed to do.

I picked up my phone and opened a message to Marlowe.

> _Discharged from the civilian hospital yesterday. Packet attached. Restrictions apply to Monday. Requesting guidance before 0400._

I read it once, fixed a comma, and sent it.

The thing about being useful was that it worked. By the time I'd set a reminder for the follow-up appointment and laid out my gear for the morning, the room had stopped feeling like an absence and started feeling like mine again.

Mostly.

I switched off the lamp and went to bed with her shirt still folded on the chair.
