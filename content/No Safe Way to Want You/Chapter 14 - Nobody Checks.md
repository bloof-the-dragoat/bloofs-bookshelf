
# Chapter 14 - Nobody Checks

>[!warning] Content Note
Trauma symptoms, a severe depressive episode, graphic recollection of violence, self-destructive thoughts, and grief.

**The hand on my chest was warm.**

That was the first thing wrong with it. In every version of this I'd ever had, the hands were cold. This one lay flat over my sternum, heavy and unhurried, and it pressed down with a slow, patient weight, the way you'd press a bruise to find out how much it still hurt. I tried to lift my arm. Nothing moved. I tried to turn my head, and nothing moved there either, and I understood with a lurch that it wasn't that I was weak. I was held. Something had a strap across my ribs and another across my wrists, and the leather was already creaking.

The ceiling above me was white and flat and shadowless. A fever was climbing out of the table into my spine, a slow, rolling heat, and my heart had begun to run without me, faster and faster, each beat hitting the strap like a fist. I knew this one. I knew what came next. I opened my mouth to say _wait,_ and the hand slid up and closed around my throat.

The room dropped. Rust, instead of white. A gantry rail across my spine, cold and wet, and the drop below me an open mouth. The hand tightened. It wasn't a question anymore. It was a decision, and I felt my windpipe fold under the thumb like paper, the air stopping dead in my chest as though somebody had turned a valve. I clawed at the wrist. It was like clawing at a pipe. My vision went to gray at the edges, then to gray in the middle, and the thing I'd learned on that rail came up out of me with no voice at all. _Not like this. Not again._

It changed. Concrete, and sticky, and the sour sweetness of a bar floor. A knee came down on my chest, and I felt my ribs take it, and the hand on my throat did not let go. Somewhere above me a crowd was laughing, or maybe it was only the ringing in my ears. The edges of the world went soft and far away. And through all of it, as if from the bottom of a well, I heard her.

_Spade._

It was her voice, and it was the worst sound I'd ever heard. It wasn't calm. It was raw, and ripped down the middle, and it kept calling my name from somewhere I couldn't reach. I tried to answer. My mouth worked on nothing. I couldn't breathe, I couldn't breathe, and she was screaming for me, and every time her voice broke, the hand pressed harder.

_There,_ said another voice, almost tender. _There._

The hand came away from my throat. For one wild moment, I thought it was over. Then I felt the cold thumb of a blade settle under my breastbone, a small, polite, patient pressure, and I looked down and saw it going in. It went in slowly. It went up. I watched my own shirt darken, a flower of it, spreading, and then the warmth poured out of me all at once, down my stomach, down my legs, soaking into the table. Something gave inside me with a wet, tearing sound I felt in my teeth. A fist came out of the dark and found my side, and my ribs folded one after another like a handful of dry sticks. I tried to scream. A sound came out, small and wet and wrong.

_One._

I started the count. I always started the count. It was the only thing I had left that was mine, and for a few seconds it held, clean and even and small against all that noise. _Two. Three. Four._ But the numbers had begun to slide, and the blood was filling my lungs, and I reached for the next one and found nothing there. I tried to start over. _One._ The strap bit into my wrists. The hand closed on my chest, warm, so terribly warm, and pressed, and I felt everything inside me begin to quiet, a long soft sinking, like a stone going down through dark water.

_Please._

She was still screaming my name, and I couldn't answer, and I couldn't move, and the hand pressed down.

My body woke before I did.

I didn't sit up. I was simply somewhere else, as if a seam had opened in the room and dropped me through it. One heartbeat I was on my back in the lamplight with her arm across me, and the next I was moving, the heat in my legs flaring like a struck match, the mattress dropping away under my shoulder blades and the floor coming up much too fast. I felt fingers close around my right wrist.

Not hard. I'd think about that later, over and over. She hadn't been hard about it at all. But a hand had closed on me in the dark, and the dream was still on my skin, and my body had never learned to tell the difference. I twisted the way I'd been taught, against the thumb, and put everything I had behind it.

The grip came apart. I felt her fingers open before the twist even landed, and I didn't know that yet.

The first step took me clear of the bed. I'd meant it to be the only one. The second came anyway, and the third, and the studio, which had always felt small, suddenly seemed far too short. My shoulder clipped the corner of the desk and sent the chair skidding across the floor. My hip hit something that rang, a long, hollow, discordant chord that went up through the room as the guitar toppled out of its corner and struck the wall, and then I was in the corner myself, wedged between the shelf and the wall with my back against the plaster and my knees drawn up. I couldn't get air. Every breath snagged short in my chest, thin and fast and useless, as though something were still pressing down on it, and the harder I pulled, the less seemed to arrive.

The sound of the guitar was still fading when I heard the other sound.

It came from the bed. It was a short, hard intake of breath, cut off at the top, the kind you make when something has hit a bruise that was already there. I looked up.

The lamp was still burning, low and gold, exactly as I'd left it. She was half off the mattress, one leg over the edge and one hand braced on the iron of the headboard, her other arm clamped across her ribs. In the dim light, the bruise along her side looked darker than it had before. She was staring at me, not at the guitar or the chair or the mess I'd made of the room, and her face did something I had never seen it do. It closed, and then it opened again too quickly, as if she'd wiped it.

"Spade," she said.

Her free hand was still out in front of her, fingers loose and open, where my wrist had been. She seemed to notice it at the same moment I did. She drew it back slowly and set it flat on the blanket.

I couldn't breathe properly. The count had gone, and the room was too bright and too small, and the corner pressed into my back as if it might give if I leaned hard enough. I'd gone across a room in a blink to get away from the one person I had spent the whole night wanting to be near.

_I flinched,_ I thought, and the thought was so quiet and so complete that it left no room for anything else. _I flinched from her._

---
Neither of us moved.

The guitar was still humming against the wall, the strings settling into a low, dissonant ring that went on far longer than it should have. The desk chair had come to rest against the shelf with one wheel slowly turning. I watched the wheel because it was something to watch. Across the room, Voeren sat at the edge of the bed with her bare feet flat on the floor and her hands lying open on her knees, in plain view, as though I were something she'd found in the road.FI

"Ranelle," she said.

She said it quietly. She didn't say it the way she had in bed, as though she were checking whether the word still fit. This was careful, steady, and pitched exactly to carry across the room and no farther. Hearing it made something in my throat clench. I looked at the floor.

_Count._

It was the only instruction I had left. I'd given it to myself so many times that it fired without a thought, and I reached for the first number and found it, and the second. _One._ It was clean. _Two._ I breathed in on the second, and the breath snagged. _Three._ I held on to it. My ribs were tight, and my fingers had begun to tingle, a cold, prickling numbness spreading up from the tips, and the number slid sideways. 

_Shit! One. Two._

I could hear her heart.

It was faint, and I wasn't supposed to be able to hear it from here, but the serum had left my ears sharper than they'd ever been, and in the quiet it came across the room as clear as a hand on a door. Fast. Faster than it should have been for someone who'd been asleep five minutes ago. I'd counted that pulse twenty times today, in a hallway and in a car and in the dim light of her bed, and every time it had held me up. I tried it now. _One. Two. Three. Four._ It kept going. It wouldn't slow down for me. I lost the number somewhere past nine and had to start over, and lost it again, and a thin sound came out of my chest, half laugh and half something much worse.

_You can keep a net up through a jammer,_ I thought. _You can hold a net for six hours with a dead repeater, and you can't hold a fucking number?_

My hands were shaking. I pressed them flat against my knees, and they shook there too. I was still wearing her shirt. It hung off my shoulders and over my knees like a tent, and I pulled the hem down over my feet as though that would help. It smelled of her. That was the worst part. I'd wanted this shirt for days, and now I was curled up inside it in a corner with my teeth chattering, and the room I'd been so proud of, the little lamp-lit studio with its guitar and its skulls and its lantern, looked like something I'd smashed.

I'd smashed it. I could see the guitar lying on its side with its neck angled against the wall. I could see the stylus that had rolled off the desk and lay on the floor. I could see, in the low gold light, the bruise along her ribs, darker than it had been when we'd gone to sleep.

"You're okay," Voeren said. "It's okay. It's just the room."

"I know."

It came out cracked. It didn't sound like anything I'd ever said.

"I know. I _know_ where I am, I know it's—" I stopped, because there was nothing at the end of the sentence. My breath was still coming in quick, shallow pulls that never seemed to reach the bottom. The wall at my back felt like the only solid thing in the room, and I leaned into it harder.

She didn't move. I noticed that, in the part of me that still noticed things. She had started to rise, her weight coming forward onto her toes, and then she had stopped. Her hands stayed open on her knees. She kept her eyes on me, and her face was very still. Her breathing was careful and slow, and I knew that careful. I'd heard it in a hospital doorway, one breath at a time, with a hand on the back of my head that shook.

"I'm not coming any closer," she said. "Not unless you want me to."

That nearly broke me. I couldn't have said why. It was such a small, reasonable thing to say.

"Aeirith," I said, and heard it crack. "I'm so sorry. I'm so— I didn't—" I couldn't get the words in the right order. They kept tumbling over each other. "I didn't mean to. I didn't know it was you. I mean I _knew_ it was you, but I didn't— my body didn't—"

"Hey."

"I'm _sorry,_" I said again, and my voice went up, thin and high and frightened. "Your ribs, I heard you, I heard it hit—"

"That was the headboard." Her voice stayed level. "I lunged. That was me, not you."

It was almost true. I could tell it was almost true by the way she said it, and by the arm still clamped across her side. It was a kind lie, or a kind half of one, and I couldn't stand it. A sergeant was supposed to be able to take care of the one person in the room. I was sitting on the floor in a stranger's shirt, shaking so hard that my heels drummed on the boards, and she was the one trying to make _me_ feel better about it.

_Pathetic,_ said a small, clear voice in the back of my head, and it didn't sound like anyone else. It sounded like me.

The shame came up behind it like a tide. It was worse than the fear. It was so much worse. Fear I knew how to carry. Fear I'd been carrying since I was a kid, I could do it with a straight face and a steady voice and a neat stack of tasks. This was something else. I had done everything right all day. I'd taken the hit and gone to the hospital and sat through Stroud, and I'd been fine, I'd been _fine,_ and now I was falling to pieces in front of the one person I'd wanted to look steady for.

"I'm sorry," I said, and I couldn't stop. "I'm sorry, *I'm sorry*, it's not normally this bad."

I heard it as it left me.

The room went very quiet. Even the guitar had stopped. I watched her face, because I couldn't look anywhere else, and I saw the word land. _Normally._ It didn't change her expression. It changed what was behind it. Something went still in her, and then she looked at me the way she had looked at my bare chest in the lamplight, with that quiet, careful attention, as though she'd just realized there was a whole room she hadn't been shown.

"Normally," she said, very softly.

And I understood exactly what I had just given her, and I couldn't take it back.

"I didn't mean to say that," I said. "That's— forget I said that. It's nothing. It doesn't usually— I can usually—"

I couldn't find the end of it. There was a place I kept things. Vaulted. *Away*. I'd kept things there since I was a kid, a shelf somewhere behind my ribs where everything went in neat and stayed put, and I reached for it now the way you'd reach for a light switch in your own house. It wasn't there. There was too much. It had been piling up all week and spilling over, and every time I put one thing in, three more fell out.

"I'm fine," I said. "I'm always fine. Ask anyone. Ask Marlowe. Ask— I'm _fine,_ I'm just—"

"Can I sit on the floor?"

It was so quiet and so ordinary that it stopped me for half a second. She'd moved off the bed while I was talking, slowly, one hand on her ribs, and was standing in the middle of the boards with her bare feet planted and her palms turned out. She wasn't coming closer. She was asking.

I nodded. I didn't know why. She lowered herself down where she was, two meters from me, with her back against the foot of the bed and her knees drawn up, and she didn't say anything else.

It should have helped. I could see that it should have. But the quiet she'd left was too big, and the thing in my chest came up to fill it.

"I counted," I said. "I don't know why. I counted them. Four times, I thought it was— that I was going to— and then five, if you count the ribs separate, I don't know if I should, I don't know if you count that, it was the same fight, it was the same _minute,_ but it was— I felt them. Both. Two different ways to die in the same minute."

"Hey—"

"The gantry. That **fucking** gantry. I couldn't breathe, I couldn't _breathe,_ and I kept thinking this is stupid, this is such a stupid way, I'm going to die on a catwalk because I couldn't— and then the bar. That was _last week,_ that was— and the table. He strapped me down. He said it would hurt. He didn't say it would feel like being— and I was thinking, I'm going to die on a table in a room with no windows, and nobody's going to know, and the only thing I could think—" My voice cracked in half. "And then the floor. The floor was—"

My hand had gone to my chest. I hadn't told it to. It was pressing flat over my sternum, over the place, and I could feel it, I could feel it _all,_ the blade going in, and the cold, and then that awful warm pour down my stomach, and I pressed harder as though I could push it back.

"It's closed," I said. "I know it's closed. I looked, you looked, there's nothing there, it's _closed._ But I can still feel it. I can still feel him _turn_ it."

"I know," Voeren said, very low. "I know."

"You *don't*."

It came out harsher than I'd meant. I saw it land. She didn't flinch from it. She just nodded once, as if I'd said something true, and stayed where she was.

I couldn't stop.

"And I lay there. I lay there and I _counted._ They thought I was dead. I made them think I was dead, I held my breath, I held it for— and my _ribs_ were— I could feel them going back, one at a time, click, click, like— like a _zipper,_ like someone was doing me up from the inside, and I couldn't make a sound. I couldn't make a _sound._ And you were— you were talking to him. You were keeping him— you knew. You knew I was—"

"I saw your foot move."

"Your _toes._ You flexed your—" A noise came out of me that wasn't a word. "You were bleeding. You were hanging there bleeding and you were _buying me time._"

"That's what I was doing." She said it simply. "It worked."

"I know it worked! That's not— that's not the—" I scrubbed my hands down my face. They were wet. I hadn't felt them get wet. "That's not the part I— I stood up. I stood up and I killed them. I killed them all, I killed four people in two minutes, and I didn't— I didn't feel _anything,_ I'm supposed to feel something, and I _didn't._ And then the last one—"

I stopped.

There was something wrong with my hands. I looked down at them. They were clean. They were pale and a little shaky and perfectly clean, but I could feel the blood. *I could see it.* It was drying in the creases of my knuckles, tacky between my fingers, dripping down my arms. I rubbed them against my thighs, hard, again and again, and the feeling didn't go anywhere.

"Ranelle."

I didn't hear it.

"I don't know how many times," I said. "I've tried to count. I keep trying to count and I can't get a number. Twenty. Thirty. He was dead. He was _dead,_ I knew he was dead, and I could tell, there's a way you can tell, it just—goes out of them, but my arm kept going. It kept going in and out and I could hear it. It sounded like—  like—" My teeth were chattering. "There was a voice. A tiny one. **Mine**. It said stop. It was so small. It said _stop, stop, this isn't you, this isn't what you do,_ and I could hear it the entire time, and I couldn't make my arm listen. **I didn't want to listen.**"

"That's—"

"**It felt good**."

The words hung in the room. I heard them go out of me and couldn't stop it, and I watched her face, and she didn't move.

"It felt like— like— _finally,_" I whispered. "That's the worst part. That's the part I can't— it wasn't a, a _thing that happened to me._ It was a thing I _wanted._ He said _again,_ like it was nothing, like it was a thing a person could just be asked for, like of course I'd just _take it,_ and I thought, I've been taking it my whole _life_, I've had to just **take** what happened, be okay with it. **Deal with it.**"

My breath had gone ragged, in and out, in and out, and the room was starting to tilt.

"And then you said _that's enough,_ and I stopped. I just stopped. Like a— like a dog. And I looked down and he wasn't a person anymore, he was just— **gone**. And I did that. I did that, and you _saw._ You watched me do it. You stood there with your wrists bleeding and you _watched—_"

"I watched you save my life."

"**I don't _care._**"

It came out in a scream. It cracked in the middle and tore.

"I don't care! It doesn't— I don't want it to be— I just want it to _stop!_ I can't turn it off! Haskins was alive. He was alive, he was right there, he was making a joke about my _radio,_ and I was _there,_ I was at the console, I was listening, and I couldn't— I said his name, I said _please,_ and nobody could hear me. And Stroud said _noted._ He said _continue._ Like I was a— like it was a _log,_ and every light on the board was _green,_ every single one, I did it _right,_ I did every single thing _right_—"

My voice broke. For a second, there was nothing in the room but my own breath, shuddering in and out.

"I can still hear you," I said. It was barely a voice. "In the hall. You were screaming my name. You were pulling on the— and I couldn't answer. I couldn't _answer_ you. I was on the floor and you were screaming and I couldn't make my mouth—"

"Spade, I'm here—"

"And it was so _tired._ That's the— it isn't just _tonight,_ I'm so tired, I've been tired since— it's like there's a—" I couldn't find it. "I do this. I get like this. Not— like *this*. Not this bad. But there's a place where I put it and it's not _there,_ there's too much, there's too _much,_ and I can't—"

My fingers had found a loose thread in the hem of her shirt, and I was winding it around my knuckle so tightly the tip had gone white.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I shouldn't be— I'm supposed to be— I'm not supposed to be like this in front of—"

"You don't have to be sorry—"

"And it's— it's _fine,_ it's okay, it's honestly— I think about it, and it's—" Something was rising in me, strange and almost light, a kind of terrible calm. I'd stopped crying at some point. I was staring at the lamp. "It's okay. It would've been okay. It's not— I keep thinking, if I'd just— if the table had— if it had gone the other way, it wouldn't have been— because it'd have been _for_ something, you know? At least I would've died trying to save you. At least it would've been for a **FUCKING REASON**."

"_SPADE._"

The word hit the room like a hand slapped flat on a table.

Everything in me stopped. It didn't slow down or wind down. It stopped, the way the engine of a truck stops when it hits a wall, and the thing I'd been about to say, whatever it was, went out like a lamp. I sat with my mouth open and my fingers wound in a thread and the terrible, strange calm that had been rising in me vanished so completely that I could no longer remember what it had felt like. I stared at her. I didn't know what else to do.

She was up on her knees.

I hadn't seen her move. She'd been sitting against the foot of the bed with her knees drawn up, and now she was a meter closer than she'd been, kneeling on the boards with one hand clamped to her ribs and the other flat on the floor like a person who has caught herself falling. Her face was gray under the bruises. Her good eye was wide and bright, and the other had swelled halfway shut again. Her jaw worked, once, before anything came out of it.

"Don't."

The shout was gone. Her voice had dropped so low that I had to strain to hear it, and every word came out slow and deliberate, set down one at a time like something that might break if she hurried. I'd heard her use that voice exactly once, in a steel frame, to a man who had called her a coward. It was so much worse than the shout.

"Don't you ever say that to me again."

I couldn't answer. I didn't think I could have found the words in any language.

"_For a reason,_" she said. She said it as if she were holding it up to the light and could not believe what she was looking at. "You think that's— you think that would have _been_ one? You'd be dead, and I'd be standing on the other side of it, and the reason would be _me._"

"That's not—"

"Let me finish."

I closed my mouth.

She breathed. It shook going in, and she held it, and I saw her gather herself the way she'd gather a lifted weight. When she spoke again, the level was back in her voice, and it was the false, careful level I'd heard in the hospital doorway, and under it, something was bleeding.

"I watched you die." It was nearly a whisper.

The room went very quiet.

"I was in that frame. I couldn't move, I couldn't _breathe,_ I pulled until my wrists came open, and I watched that man put a knife in you, and I watched you go down. I watched you not get up. I heard it, Spade. I heard your ribs." Her voice cracked at the end, and she shut her teeth on it. "I thought you were dead. For a _minute._ Almost a whole minute. I stood there and I thought, they're dead, they came for me and they're *dead*, and I did that."

"You didn't—"

"Then you stood up." She wasn't listening. "And I've been trying not to think about the minute ever since. And you sit there and tell me you'd have been _fine_ with it."

"I didn't mean—"

"You did." She said it gently, and that was the worst of it. "You meant every word. You were calm about it. You were _calm._"

I looked down at my hands. They had started to shake again. I hadn't known she could see them.

I *had* heard it. That was the thing I couldn't get around. I'd heard myself say it, and I'd said it as though I were reading a sentence off a page, and the only part that had surprised me was the profanity. It hadn't felt like a confession. It had felt like the first true, easy thing I'd said all night. It had felt like _relief._ And hearing her say it back to me, flat and stripped and held at arm's length, I understood for the first time that it hadn't sounded like relief at all.

_At least I would've died trying to save you._

I didn't know what to do with it. I didn't know where to put it. It sat in my chest next to everything else, and it was the heaviest thing there.

"I'm sorry," I said, and it came out so small that it barely counted.

"Stop apologizing."

"I don't—"

"I know you don't." She breathed out hard. She wasn't looking at me anymore. She was staring at the floor between us, at her own hand spread flat on the boards, and I saw her shoulders shake. A tear fell off the end of her nose and hit the wood. I don't think she knew. "I'm not mad at you. I'm— I _am,_ a little, I'm furious, I'm so— but it's not _at_ you. I don't know what it's at. It's at everything. It's at the _table._"

I went still.

"I heard you say table," she said. "I'm not asking. Not tonight. But I heard it, and I'm going to want to know." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. It left a pale streak through the bruise. "Later."

"Okay."

"Okay." She looked up. Her eyes found mine, and held. "Ranelle."

It wasn't the way she'd said it in bed. It wasn't the way she'd said it across the room, either, careful and calm. It was plain and unguarded, and it went through me like a nail.

"I need you to promise me something."

I knew what was coming. I could see it in the set of her mouth. A cold, tight thing closed around my chest, and for a moment, I thought I'd lose the floor again.

"Promise me you'll live."

She said it quietly. She said it as though it were the simplest thing in the world, and as though it were the only thing.

"No matter what. Not— not _for_ me. I don't care what you do it for. For spite. For the guitar. For the stupid story about the library. I don't care, I just— no matter what happens. No matter who's in the room, or what they do to me, or what you think is the reason. You _live._ You find a way. You live." Her voice caught. "*Promise me*."

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out.

I tried again. The word was right there. It was a small, easy word, one I'd said to a hundred people in a hundred rooms. _Yes. Okay. Sure._ I'd said them at the end of every briefing and into every comm check. They'd always come out smooth.

But I thought of the table. I thought of the truck, and Stroud's face, and the sound of my own voice saying _yes, sir_ to a question he hadn't even asked. I thought of the hall, and a woman screaming my name, and the thing that had come up in me as I crossed that floor. It hadn't felt like a choice. It had felt like something I'd been walking toward all my life.

_If it happened again,_ said a quiet voice in the back of my head. _You'd go._

I'd go. I'd go in a heartbeat. I knew it with a certainty that frightened me, and I had no idea what to do with a promise that was a lie.

"I—" I said.

Her eyes didn't leave my face.

"I'll try," I said. It sounded thin. It sounded like something a person says to end a conversation.

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

"Ranelle."

"I _know._" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. When I took them away, the room swam. "I can't promise you something I— if it was *you*. If it happened again, if you were in that room, and I—" I stopped. I couldn't say it. I could feel her listening to the hole where it should have been. "I'm not going to _want_ to die. I don't want that. I'm not—"

"Then say it."

I looked at her. She was waiting. She was kneeling on the floor of my apartment at three in the morning with her wrists bandaged and her ribs bruised, crying without knowing it, and she was asking me for one small thing.

"Okay," I said. "I promise."

It came out flat. It came out so flat and so smooth that I heard it from the outside, a clean, empty line that didn't belong to anyone, the kind of thing I said to a console at the end of a shift. _Copy. Acknowledged. Out._ There wasn't a single true thing in it, and it hung there in the lamplight between us like a piece of paper.

She heard it.

I watched her hear it. I watched it go into her, and I watched her decide, one muscle at a time, not to say what she was thinking. She didn't flinch. She didn't argue. She looked at me for a long moment, and then she nodded, slowly, as though I'd given her something real.

"Okay," she said.

Neither of us believed it.

She didn't move back. I noticed that. She stayed where she was, a meter and a half from me, with her palm still flat on the boards, and the lamp burned low and gold over the two of us, and somewhere far off a train sounded and went quiet.

She was looking at me. She wasn't looking at the guitar, or the overturned chair, or the dark bruise along her own ribs. She was looking into my eyes, steadily, in a way that made it impossible to look anywhere else, and her face was wet and open and wholly without defense.

"Ranelle, what do you need right now?"

It wasn't the question I'd braced for. It wasn't _are you okay_ or _do you want to talk about it_ or any of the careful, reasonable questions I had a careful, reasonable answer ready for. She asked it as if she actually wanted to know. As if she'd take whatever I said, and do it, and not ask me why.

I opened my mouth to say _nothing._ I'd said it so many times. _Nothing. I'm fine. I don't need anything._ It was right there on my tongue.

It didn't come.

What came instead was something small and cracked that I barely recognized as my own voice.

"Please," I whispered. "Please just be here."

It was the truest thing I'd said all night. It wasn't clean. It didn't have a shape. It was only a wish with no sentence around it, and the moment it left me, I wanted to take it back, because I could hear how it sounded. How _needy._ How small.

Her face broke.

It wasn't dramatic. Her mouth just went soft, and something behind her eyes let go, and she let out a breath that was nearly a sob. "I'm here," she breathed. "I'm right here." Her hand lifted off the floor, slowly, and hung in the air between us. "Can I? I want to hold you. Is that okay?"

I nodded. I couldn't have said it.

She crossed the distance on her knees. It cost her. I saw it, the tight flinch at her ribs that she swallowed before it reached her face, and then her arms were around me, and she pulled me out of the corner and into her chest as though I weighed nothing at all.

I held still.

I'd thought I was ready for this. I'd thought I could take it the way I took everything else, with my jaw shut and my spine straight and a careful, steady _thank you_ ready on my tongue. My arms hung at my sides. I didn't know where to put them. It was such a stupid thing to think, with her heartbeat against my cheek and her hand settling warm and wide at the back of my head, and I thought of her saying it a few hours ago, in the dark, _I don't know where to put my hands._ She had said it as a joke.

Something in my chest began to shake.

It was a small thing at first, a tremor low in my ribs. I clamped my mouth shut and bit down on the inside of my cheek until I tasted copper. I breathed through my nose. I counted, and there was no number. I told myself I could hold this, that I'd held worse, that I could stay upright for another minute, another thirty seconds. I pressed my face into her collarbone, as if I could push it back down through force.

She felt it. Of course she did. Her arm tightened a fraction, not enough to crush, just enough to say _I know,_ and her mouth came down against my hair.

"You said something to me tonight," she said. Her voice was very low, and it wasn't steady. "Before. When we were lying here. You said you were good at being not-known. That you'd spent your whole life being a reasonable person that nobody checks on."

I stopped breathing.

I hadn't thought she'd keep it. I'd said it into a ceiling, in a quiet that felt as though it would swallow whatever fell into it. She had answered _that tracks,_ and I had thought that was the end of it. I had been so sure I'd said it into the dark.

"I heard that," she said. "I haven't stopped hearing it."

"Aeirith—"

"*I'm checking*." Her voice cracked right down the middle, and she kept going. "I'm checking on you. Right now. And I'm going to keep checking. I'm not going to ask you if you're fine and take _yes_ for an answer. I'm not going to tell you it'll be okay, because I don't know that, and I'm not going to lie to you. I'm just not going to stop. I'm not going to get tired of it. I'm not going to leave when it's ugly."

It went in like a blade, and it didn't hurt at all.

I'd been braced for it all my life. The kind word that missed. _Oh, you worry too much. It'll be fine. You're stronger than you think._ Every kind, well-meant, useless one. I'd learned to smile through them and say thank you and let the door close a little more each time. I'd built whole years around the certainty that someone would either leave or tell me it was nothing. I had never once been told, _I'm going to stay and I'm not going to pretend._

"I'm f—" I said.

It was reflex. It was the oldest reflex I had.

"I know," she said softly. "Not with me. You don't have to be."

My chest caved.

The sound came out of me before I could stop it. It wasn't a sob yet. It was the sound a person makes when something heavy has been taken out of their hands, a low, broken, astonished noise, and it dragged the next one behind it, and the next. I curled into her. I couldn't help it. My hands found her back and fisted in the hoodie she'd put on, and I clung to it as though it were a rail. The bite on my cheek tore open. It didn't matter. Nothing I did could hold it back anymore.

It came up through me in great, ugly, wrenching sobs. They shook my whole frame. They tore out of my throat in pieces, and I could hear myself, and I hated it, and I couldn't stop. I cried the way you cry when you've been holding a breath for ten years, with my whole face pressed into her shoulder and my mouth open and no dignity left in me at all.

"I've got you," she said into my hair. Her voice was thick. "I've got you. I'm right here."

"I'm sorry—"

"No."

"I didn't want you to see—"

"I know. I know. I see you." Her arm tightened, and her hand moved slowly over the back of my head, again and again. "All of it. I'm not going anywhere."

I wept until my ribs hurt. I wept until there was nothing left in me but a long, hollow shiver, and then I wept some more. She held me through all of it. She never once told me to stop. She rocked me, very slightly, the way you'd rock something you were afraid to break, and her breathing was ragged against my temple, and somewhere in the middle of it, I understood that she was crying too.

She held me as though she had decided something. I felt it in the set of her arms, a fraction too tight, a fraction too sure. I should have noticed that. I should have wondered how she had kept every word I'd said in the dark, exactly as I'd said it. But I was so tired, and it was so warm, and for the first time in longer than I could remember, I let someone hold the weight.

---
I woke up on my side, with her arm around me.

It took me a second to understand where I was. The window was a pale gray rectangle with the blind half drawn, and the light that came through it lay across the blanket in thin, tired bars. The lamp was off. At some point, it had been turned off. My eyes felt as though they'd been sanded, and my mouth tasted of copper and sleep, and somewhere under the blankets, my hunger was already awake and complaining.

Then the rest of it came back.

It didn't arrive all at once. It came in pieces, each one slotting into place with a small, sick click. The corner. The guitar going over. My own voice saying a number I couldn't find. Her face when I'd said _normally._ I had said all of it out loud. I'd said things I hadn't said to anyone, not to Marlowe or my friends, not to the dark, and I'd said them in a broken, wet, screaming voice, to the one person I'd wanted to see me steady.

I went rigid.

The arm around my waist didn't tighten. It didn't slacken, either. It simply stayed, warm and heavy, and I felt a slow, even breath against the back of my neck.

"Morning," Voeren said, very quietly.

She'd been awake. I could tell by her voice. There was no sleep in it at all, only that low, careful, unhurried quality she'd had since the hospital, as though she were walking on ice she trusted.

"How long have you been up?"

"A while."

"You could have gotten up."

"I could have."

She didn't say anything else. She didn't have to. I lay there and understood, slowly, that she'd spent however many hours it had been lying very still with her arm over me, her ribs, sore now instead of broken, against my spine, so that she wouldn't wake me. She hadn't moved. I could feel it in the faint stiffness of her arm, and it wasn't from pain. It was from holding still.

It should have felt like too much. A part of me knew that. Somewhere in the back of my head, a small, clear voice said, _that's a lot of a person to have behind you._ But it didn't feel like too much. It felt like a wall at my back in a room I'd been standing in the middle of for years, and I was so tired, and it was so _warm,_ and I couldn't make myself mind.

Then the shame arrived.

It came up in a wave, hot and thick, and it went through me from my scalp to my heels. I pulled away. I didn't mean to. My shoulders curled in, and I put my hand over my face and pressed my fingers against my eyes, as though if I couldn't see the morning, it couldn't see me.

"I'm sorry about last night," I said into my palm. "I mean— I'm sorry you had to— I shouldn't have— you shouldn't have had to deal with that. That's not what you signed up for. It's, you came here for a, for a night, and I—"

"Ranelle."

"—I made a mess of your— of the whole—"

"Ranelle. Look at me."

I didn't want to. I looked.

She'd shifted onto her elbow. Her hair was flat on one side, and the swelling around her eye had gone down to a yellowing shadow, the scar over her brow back in plain view, and there was a faint line on her cheek where she'd lain against the seam of the pillow. She looked tired. She looked about as tired as I felt. And she was looking at me with the quiet, steady attention that I'd seen in the annex, as if I were something she was trying very hard to hold still enough to see.

"I'm not going to let you do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Be ashamed. Of last night." She said it slowly, one word at a time. "Not with me. You don't get to. That's the one place you don't."

"It was—" My throat closed. "It was ugly."

"It was true." Her hand came up, slow, and settled on the side of my face. "You told me the truth. For the first time, I think, in a long time. That's the opposite of something you have to be sorry for." Her thumb moved once across my cheekbone. "I'll take true over _fine_ every day of the week. Every day. I told you that. I meant it."

I had nothing to say. I put my forehead against her collarbone, because it was the only place left to put it, and I breathed. She curled her arm around me, and neither of us said anything for a while, and I let her hold the weight for a little longer.

It didn't make the shame go away. I want to be honest about that. It sat in me like a stone, and it was going to sit there for a while. But it had stopped being the only thing in the room.

---

The room had been put back.

I noticed it as I swung my legs off the bed. The desk chair stood at the desk, tucked in square. The stylus had been picked up off the floor and laid on the keyboard. In its corner, the guitar leaned upright against the wall, the neck unmarked, as though nothing had happened to it. She'd put it back without playing a note. I could tell, because the strings were still slightly out of tune from where it had fallen, and she hadn't touched them.

I looked at it for a long moment.

"You didn't—"

"I said I'd ask," she said, behind me. She was sitting up against the headboard with the blanket over her knees. "I just picked it up. I didn't want it lying there."

"Thank you."

"It's fine. It sounds like a dying cat, though. Just so you know. I think one of the strings is—" She stopped. "Sorry. Not the time."

It was almost funny. I felt the corner of my mouth move, a tiny crooked tug, and it surprised me so much that I didn't know what to do with it.

The kitchenette had survived. She'd gone through it sometime before I woke, and I found, on the little counter by the hot plate, a plate stacked with burnt toast, a pot of something that might once have been rice, and four packets of instant noodles lined up beside a single, lonely egg.

"I found your pantry," Voeren said, behind me. "It's not a pantry. It's a _cry for help._"

"It's a pantry."

"You have forty packets of ramen and one egg."

"It's a _strategic_ egg."

"I'm sure it is."

I ate everything. I didn't taste any of it, and it didn't matter. The hunger had a bottom to it this morning, somewhere beneath the ache, and I could feel myself reaching for it with every bite. She ate half a slice of toast and watched me work through the rest, and when I looked up from the plate, she was smiling at me, small and private and a little rueful, as if I'd said something she'd been waiting to hear.

The phone on the counter had one new message. It was from Major Ito, timestamped 0612, two lines: _No change on Doyle. Medical will call you Thursday. Rest._ I read it twice. I'd wanted to call the ward, and then I realized I couldn't make myself, and I set the phone face down on the counter.

"Later," she said quietly. "You'll call later."

"Yeah."

The pay, the notifications for Haskins and Hale and Okafor, the Thursday appointment, all of it was sitting there, folded small, waiting. I could feel it. I couldn't look at it yet. But I had a plate of eggs and a sunlit room and someone who'd made me burnt toast, and for the first time in a week, the thing waiting for me wasn't a briefing.

I took my plate to the sink and washed it. When I turned around, she was standing in the middle of the room in her bare feet and the borrowed hoodie, one hand resting against her ribs where they were still sore, and the gauze on her wrists had gone gray and fraying.

"You can take those off," I said. "They've been on since the hospital."

"I know."

"So take them off."

She didn't move. She looked at the gauze, and then at me, and I saw something go across her face that I hadn't seen since the doorway of the ward. It was quick and a little embarrassed. It was the look she got when she thought she'd said too much.

"I didn't want you to see them," she said.

I stopped.

"It's stupid. They're fine. They've been fine since yesterday. I kept them wrapped because—" She huffed a breath. "Because you'd look at them and you'd get that face. The one you get. And I didn't want that to be the first thing you saw this morning."

"What face?"

"The one you're making."

I wiped my expression flat. She laughed, once, a surprised, rough sound, and some of the tension went out of her shoulders.

"Sit," I said.

She sat on the edge of the bed. I knelt in front of her on the boards and took her left hand, and she went still. I unwound the old gauze slowly, turn by turn, and underneath it the skin was almost whole. There was no blood, no rawness. Where the cuffs had cut, a band of fresh, shiny pink ran around the wrist, with a thin ridge standing up along its center, like a seam sewn by someone who'd been in a hurry.

I didn't say anything.

It had been raw and red and bleeding the last time I'd looked, in the dim light of a hall. I'd been the one who'd been too late. I'd stood at a rack of levers with my hands slipping, and she'd hung there pulling until her own skin gave. Looking at it now, healed, I felt the weight of that go through me in a long, slow wave.

"It'll scar," she said quietly. "Probably. The serum does that, when it's bad. Fast, but not clean."

I turned her hand over and set my thumb very lightly against the ridge.

"Good," I said.

Her eyebrows went up. "_Good?_"

"I mean—" My face went hot. "Not good. I don't mean it's good that you— I mean it's _yours._ It's something you got out of there. It's proof that you—" I gave up. "I'm not saying this right."

"No," she said, very softly. "You are."

I did the other wrist. I checked it as carefully as the first, with the same slow thumb, and when I finished, I set both of her hands back on her knees. She was looking at me with her whole face, and I couldn't hold it for long.

"You don't need the gauze," I said. "Just keep them clean."

"You're good at this," she said.

"I only looked."

"That's what I meant."

I sat back on my heels. Her gaze slid past me, to my shoulder. I'd pulled on a clean shirt from the drawer, one of the loose black ones with a faded print I never explained, and the collar had slipped to one side.

I felt her go still.

"It's almost gone," she said.

The bite had faded overnight. What was left was a pale, greenish-yellow ghost on the line of my shoulder, with the faint curve of her teeth just visible in it, like something seen through water. I'd forgotten it. I'd been so busy with everything else that I hadn't thought about it once.

I wanted to keep it.

She reached out, slowly, and stopped before she touched it. "May I?"

I nodded.

Her fingertip rested very lightly at the edge of the mark, and the touch was so gentle that it might have been a breath. Her eyes flicked up to mine.

"That was a lot faster than it should've been," she said quietly. "Last night it was still dark."

"I ate."

"I know you ate." Her thumb moved a hair. "I've been thinking about it since I woke up. About how you were, with the hickeys. About the— where you were, in the hall." She swallowed. "You don't have to. You said later. I'm not— I'm not trying to push you."

I looked at her hand on my shoulder.

It would have been easy to say _not today._ It would have been the sensible answer, and she'd have accepted it. She'd have nodded and gone to make another pot of rice, and she'd have waited, patiently, for as long as I asked. I could feel that. I could feel how much she'd wait.

But I'd been holding it for two days, and it was heavy, and I had just spent an entire night learning what happened when I didn't put things down.

"I want to," I said. "I think I need to say it out loud. To someone who won't tell me it was okay."

Her hand didn't move.

"I won't," she said.

I took a breath.

"Okay," I said. "The table."
