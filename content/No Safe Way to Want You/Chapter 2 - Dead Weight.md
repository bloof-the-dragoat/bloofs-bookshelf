## Chapter 2: Dead Weight

> [!warning] Content note  
> Graphic violence, strangulation aftermath, injury, and blood.

The pressure eased.

Not enough. His hand was still around my throat, his knee still on my chest. I pulled at his wrist, and his fingers opened. I tried to breathe. Something caught in my throat, and the breath came apart.

Above me, his mouth opened. A wet grunt. Then a crack so sharp I felt it in my teeth.

His head turned farther than it should.

He fell sideways, dragging his knee off my ribs, and struck the concrete beside me. Voeren stood behind him. Then she swayed, and I reached for her before I remembered I couldn’t get up.

Air scraped into my lungs. I coughed, tried again, coughed harder. My throat hurt all the way down. Each breath felt too small, but I wanted the next one before I’d finished the last.

Voeren’s left arm hung close against her side, the scorched carrier dangling loose over a shirt dark with blood. Her jaw was tight. In the strobe light I could see the effort it took her to stay upright.

But she was upright, and she was looking at me. Her mouth lifted. Not the grin from the pump house. Barely a smile at all. I dropped my eyes a moment too late, and I’d already seen it.

“Hey.” Her voice shook. “You okay?”

I’d been about to ask her the same thing. “I’m—” A cough bent me forward, my back caught, and I stopped halfway while Voeren lowered herself beside me. “I’m alive,” I managed.

“Good.”

She braced one hand against the floor and put the other to her flank. Her shirt was soaked. At the burned edges of the wound the tissue looked raw and wet, drawn closer together than I remembered, and I couldn’t tell what that meant for whatever was damaged underneath. Her fingers trembled against it.

“You put my shooter down,” she said. “Dropped half the depot on his friend.”

“You saw that?”

“Heard enough.” One corner of her mouth moved. “Horn. Chain. Lot of screaming.”

A laugh scraped out of me and turned into another cough. “Careful,” she murmured, and I pressed a hand against my ribs.

A minute ago I’d thought I would never hear her speak again. Now she was beside me, making jokes badly enough that I wanted her to keep doing it.

“Most people can’t think with somebody trying to kill them,” she said. “You can.”

I looked at the man beside my leg. I could still feel his hand. Voeren followed my glance, then looked toward the doorway. “We need to move.”

“Yeah.”

I drew my left foot closer. Pain caught behind the knee and ran up my thigh, and the foot stopped where it was. Her gaze shifted toward the tool crib.

“There was a hatch,” she said. “Saw it before I went down.”

I kept looking at my boot.

“You could’ve left,” she said.

The alarm filled the space between us. I wiped my mouth, and blood came away on my fingers.

“You stayed.”

“I just—” Swallowing hurt. “Didn’t want you to die.”

I hadn’t meant to say it like that. There were other answers, better ones, things about the mission and not abandoning personnel and her being the person we needed to get out. None of them arrived in time.

Her hand settled on my shoulder, and I looked up. “I know.” Her fingers pressed once. “Thank you.”

I nodded. Then nodded again, because apparently that was all I had.

_Get up. You can feel strange about this somewhere else._

I braced my good hand against the concrete and pushed. The room tilted, and Voeren’s grip tightened. I stopped moving until the shelving settled back where it belonged.

“Knee,” I said. “Back. I can walk, but—”

“How well?”

I tested the left leg with most of my weight still on the right. It held. I wished it hadn’t hurt quite so much proving it. “Barely.”

Her eyes moved down to my knee.

“The canal,” I said. “We might get a better signal there. Cross the rail bridge by the old pump station, get farther from the jammer—”

She looked back at my face, and I lost the sentence. “What?”

“You’re doing it again.”

“Doing what?”

“Planning.”

I glanced toward the southern doorway. “Someone has to.”

“Yeah,” she said. “Someone does.”

There was no joke in it this time. My ears warmed. _You idiot._ “The handset might still work,” I added, although neither of us had asked me to continue.

Voeren pushed herself upright and caught the pillar halfway up. I watched her fingers spread against the concrete. “Your side—”

“Come on.”

She let go of the pillar. I reached for the shelving. “I can walk.”

“You said barely.”

She bent, slid one arm beneath my knees and the other behind my back, and lifted.

My hand closed on her shoulder. “What—Voeren!”

“Barely’s not fast enough.”

My back seized. I sucked a breath through my teeth, and she adjusted her hold. A stupid, painful little moan escaped me. At least I think it was just pain. I think.

_Shut up._

“Better?”

I nodded against her shoulder. It took me another second to understand where I was.

Eight months of trying not to stare at this woman, and now I was against her chest with a hand twisted in her shirt. _Okay. Cool. Normal. I am being carried by the most terrifying person in the Columbian Army, and my body has decided this is the moment to notice she smells good._

“Comfortable?” she asked.

“Fine.”

She glanced down. I looked at the light fitting above the doorway. Broken. Unremarkable. An excellent thing to look at.

“You’re blushing.”

_Oh god._

“Can we please move?”

Her laugh was quiet. I felt it beneath my palm. “Yeah,” she said. “We can move.”

She turned through the doorway, and the first few strides jarred my back badly enough to clear my head. I clenched my teeth and tried not to shift in her arms. Pipes passed overhead, and her shoulder cleared a narrow gap with very little room to spare. Water splashed under her boots.

I looked back. The doorway emptied behind us, and beyond it the strobes still flashed across the loading bay. No movement. I kept watching until the angle took it away.

“Anything?” Voeren asked.

“Nothing I can see.”

“Good.”

She was warm through the damp cloth and the bulk of my vest. There was blood on her collar. Close to her shoulder, I could smell sweat and gun oil and something cleaner beneath them.

My hand had found the back of her neck. I noticed. I considered moving it. _Tactical,_ I told myself. _Stability. Obviously._ It was not tactical. Her next stride jolted my back, and I held on.

The rail bridge rose ahead, old planks stretched over black water. Voeren’s pace changed, a short step and then a longer one to make up for it. I lifted my head. “Voeren?”

“I’m fine.”

She reached the bridge, and the planks drummed beneath her boots. Her arm trembled under my knees.

“You’re hurt.”

“Noticed.”

I looked at her side. The shirt was dark all the way to her belt. “Put me down.”

“We’re almost across.”

“I can walk.”

She breathed in through her nose and let it out too quickly. I knew that breath. I’d been making it every time I moved.

“Voeren. Stop.”

“No.”

The word came out rough.

She carried me off the bridge into a lane between the warehouse wall and the canal. Metal posts lined the water. Farther ahead, a railing caught the first gray light above the roofs. Her stride shortened again, and I tightened my hand on her shoulder.

“Please. Just let me—”

Her knee folded.

She twisted as we fell, drawing me against her. For a moment her arm held. Then my back struck the bricks.

I couldn’t hear the impact. There was only pain, then a high ringing and the sight of the warehouse wall turned sideways. I lay against the base of a metal post with my mouth open, and eventually I heard myself breathing.

“Voeren?”

She was several feet away, one arm outstretched. I waited for her to lift her head.

“Voeren.”

I tried to stand. My knee gave before I could straighten it, and I caught myself hard on both hands. She didn’t move, so I crawled. Brick scraped my palms, and each movement pulled at my back. I reached her shoulder and nearly fell across her.

Two fingers beneath her jaw. Nothing.

No. My hand was shaking. I moved it and pressed again.

_Come on._

There. Fast beneath my fingers.

“Oh, thank God.”

Her chest lifted. I watched until it happened again. “Okay.” My voice broke. “Okay.”

Blood seeped from her flank. I pulled the medical pouch from her belt and fought the fastening. My thumb slipped. I tried again, got it open, and tore the dressing packet apart. The gauze darkened under my hand, and she didn’t flinch. I looked at her face.

“Come on,” I whispered.

Nothing.

I kept pressure on the wound, then secured the dressing as well as I could and checked her breathing. Still there. I sat back on my heels.

_She should’ve put me down._

The thought came again before I’d finished thinking it.

_She should’ve stopped._

I looked toward the bridge. Empty. The lane ahead vanished between buildings. No one was coming from either direction.

The handset pressed against my thigh, so I pulled it out. Cracked casing, dead screen. Something I could work on. I set it beside her and released the battery. A broken corner of the housing had caught against a contact. I worked the fragment free, checked the terminals, and reached into my pouch for the spare pack. My fingers were steadier now.

I fitted the battery. A light flickered and went out.

“Come on.”

I eased the split casing apart. Pain caught across my back, and I stopped with the handset balanced against my knee and waited, breathing through my mouth. Inside, a connector had worked loose. I could see it. For the first time since she’d fallen, I knew exactly what to do next.

I pressed it home. The light returned, and stayed. I turned toward Voeren, and her chest rose.

“All right.”

I keyed the handset. “Kestrel Control, Kestrel Control, this is Echo Three-One. Do you copy, over?”

Static.

I shifted closer to the wall. “Kestrel Control. Echo Three-One. How copy?”

Nothing. Behind me, her breathing caught, and I turned so fast my back seized again. Her chest rose. Fell. I waited for the next breath before I faced the radio.

The antenna connection was damaged. I checked it, then looked at the railing running along the lane. Weathered steel, with bare metal where the paint had gone. Maybe.

I drew my knife. It took two attempts to catch what I needed at the damaged connection. On the third I braced my wrist against my knee and made myself slow down. The arrangement I managed against the railing was ugly. I had to hold the handset at an angle to keep contact, with the knife braced in place. It only had to work long enough.

“Kestrel Control. Echo Three-One.”

The hiss changed. I raised the handset a little.

“Any station on this net, this is Echo Three-One.”

Water lapped against the canal wall. I listened.

“I have a casualty. Staff Sergeant down. Request—”

My voice cracked. I released the key and swallowed. It hurt.

“Any station. Echo Three-One.”

The static broke.

“—Three-One—Kestrel—say again—”

I stopped breathing. Then I pressed the key.

“Kestrel, Echo Three-One. I read you weak. How do you read me?”

“—trying to raise you—sitrep—”

“Two survivors. Staff Sergeant Voeren and myself. She’s wounded. Unconscious, breathing. Flank wound.”

Too fast. I forced a breath.

“I’m injured but mobile. Request immediate MEDEVAC.”

“—Copy. Stand by.”

I lowered my forehead toward the railing without changing the handset’s position. Someone knew. We weren’t just two bodies beside a canal anymore. Someone beyond the warehouses knew where to start looking. My eyes burned, and I wiped them against my sleeve.

“Kestrel?”

No answer. I looked back at Voeren, at her hand lying open beside her.

“Kestrel, she’s—”

“Echo Three-One. Kestrel. Interference still heavy at your position. We can’t bring the aircraft in there. Can you move?”

I stared down the lane. “How far?”

“Pier Four. Old ferry slip. Southwest along the canal. Approximately five hundred meters.”

_No._

“Dustoff is airborne. Twenty-five minutes out. Ten-minute holding window after that.”

I looked at my knee, then at the dressing on her side.

“Say again the distance.”

“Five hundred meters. Follow the canal wall. Blue light on the pier.”

I shut my eyes.

“Echo, can you move?”

The answer was no. I could tell them that. One casualty unconscious, one unable to walk properly. Request another extraction point. Assistance on the ground. Anything. I had the words.

Voeren’s hair had caught against her mouth. I reached back and brushed it away, and she didn’t stir.

“Yeah.”

My voice startled me. I pressed the key harder.

“I’ll move her. Tell them to hold.”

I worked the knife free, wiped it against my trousers, and returned it to the sheath behind my right hip. Then I closed the handset as securely as I could and stowed it.

The drag sheet was strapped to her pack, which had come down with her. Gray polymer, webbing loops. I knew how to use one. I’d practiced on a clean floor with someone who helped when the instructor wasn’t looking. I spread it beside her.

Getting her onto it took nearly six minutes. Small movements. Stop when my back locked, start again when I could breathe. Keep her from rolling off. Check the dressing. Her shoulder struck the bricks.

“Sorry.”

She gave no sign of hearing. I moved her another few inches, and at last she lay on the sheet. I crouched beside her with my head hanging, sweat dropping from my nose onto the polymer.

Six minutes. I had spent six minutes before moving her a single meter toward the pier.

_Get up._

I gathered the webbing. My good leg straightened and the left followed badly. I leaned forward, the straps tightened, and nothing moved. I set my feet again and pulled, and the sheet scraped across the bricks. A foot. Another. I reached the next post and collapsed against it, breathing through my mouth. Behind me, a pale track marked the wet ground.

Two meters.

_Four hundred and ninety-eight._

I closed my eyes. I couldn’t do five hundred. Not like that. I looked at the next post. I could try for that.

I pulled. Step. Set the foot. Pull again. The sheet scraped behind me. When my knee flared, I shifted off it. When my back seized, I leaned against the railing until I could straighten. Then another post. The webbing rubbed against my palms, and I changed my grip, then changed it back.

Behind me, cloth shifted. I turned immediately. Voeren’s head had rolled to one side, and I watched her chest.

“Still here?” I whispered.

A breath.

“All right.”

Her fingers rested loose against the polymer. It was strange seeing that hand open. I’d watched it tear a door aside. Then it had settled on my shoulder so carefully I’d forgotten, for a second, about the dead man beside me.

_“Thank you.”_

My face warmed. Here, of all places. I looked away and took up the straps.

It didn’t work. Every time I checked the dressing, I saw the rest of her. The long line of her forearm, the torn sleeve showing the edge of black ink. The set of her shoulders, even slack. The thick cords of her thigh where her trousers had ridden up, and the hard curve of her calf under the strap. I knew exactly what I was doing. I’m not a person who likes war. I’ve spent my whole career fixing the things that break in it. But there was something about the way she was _built_, like someone had designed a weapon and then given it a face, and I had been dragging her through a canal lane for ten minutes thinking _battle goddess_ with my whole chest.

_Stop it. She’s unconscious. You’re a terrible person. Look at the post._

I looked at the post. Then at her again. Then at the post. My ears were on fire, and my knee had stopped screaming somewhere in the last forty meters, which was either a miracle or a very bad sign. I pulled harder, mostly so I’d have something to be angry at.

The sheet caught against a seam. I eased it free without letting myself stop for long.

Across the canal, headlights swept a warehouse wall. I went still. The beams slid toward the water, swung away, and vanished behind a building. An engine faded. I waited. No voices. No boots on our side.

I pulled again.

The lane opened onto a stretch of quay exposed to the wind. No doorway to lean into, no shelter. The sky above the roofs was paling. I looked back, trying to judge the distance by the buildings.

Two hundred meters. Perhaps.

My throat hurt too much to swallow. I had lost track of the minutes. Six getting her onto the sheet. The stops. The radio before that. I started pulling before I finished adding.

At the next rough patch, the sheet stopped. I leaned harder. Nothing. A broken edge of paving had folded beneath the front. I stared at it. One piece of brick. That was all.

I wanted to sit beside it. Not die. Not disappear. Just put the straps down and stop asking my body for something it didn’t have.

Behind me, Voeren breathed.

I let go of the webbing, went back, and freed the corner. Checked the dressing. Then I returned to the straps. My palms were wet, and I didn’t look.

Ahead, the canal wall bent. Pier Four should be beyond it. I still couldn’t see a blue light.

_To the bend._

My left leg shook when I set it down.

_Just to the bend._

I pulled. By the next stop, my breathing had become a harsh scraping sound. I sank onto my good knee with the straps still clenched in my fists. A minute. I needed—

“Hey.”

I lifted my head. Voeren’s eyes were open.

I stared at her. She blinked. Her gaze moved from my face to the webbing, then past me, following the scraped track along the bricks, and came back to my face.

“You—” She swallowed. “You dragged me?”

“Yeah.” My voice barely worked. I looked toward the bend. “Pier Four’s ahead. Hundred and twenty meters. Maybe a bit more.”

She didn’t look. “How far?”

“From where you—”

“How far did you come?”

I glanced back. “Three-seventy-ish.”

She went quiet. Wind lifted a strand of hair from her cheek. Under her attention I became aware of my mouth hanging open, my arms shaking, the way I was kneeling because I couldn’t stay upright.

“I wasn’t going to leave you,” I said.

She looked at me.

“I just—it took longer than I thought. You’re really heavy. Not—” Her eyebrows moved. “Not like that. I mean, you’ve got a lot of—”

_Ugh. You’re an idiot._

I closed my eyes. “I’m going to stop talking.”

A small sound escaped her. I looked again, and she was laughing. It caught at her side, and she pressed a hand against the dressing, winced, and tried to stop. That made it worse. I laughed too. It hurt my throat and my ribs, and I couldn’t get enough air for it. She looked at me and shook her head, still laughing, and for a few seconds I didn’t need anything beyond that.

Then she took a careful breath. “Almost four hundred meters.”

I looked down.

“On that knee.”

“It’s what anyone would—”

“No.” Her hand settled over mine. “Not anyone.”

I looked up. She wasn’t teasing. I was still gripping the strap so tightly her fingers couldn’t quite get around mine, and I made myself loosen them. Her hand closed.

“I’m not going to forget this,” she said. “Okay?”

I nodded.

“Not a damn minute of it.”

My mouth moved. Nothing came out. She didn’t ask me to fix that.

Water shifted against the canal wall. Far behind us, the depot alarm was still sounding. For the first time since she’d fallen, I wasn’t the only person listening.

Voeren pushed herself onto one elbow. “All right, Sergeant.” She looked toward the bend. “Let’s get the hell out of here.”

“Wait. You shouldn’t—”

She stopped halfway upright, her jaw tight. I reached for her, and she caught my arm. “Not leaving you to do the rest.”

She drew one boot beneath her, stood, and swayed. I held on. Neither of us spoke until she steadied. Blood had darkened the dressing at her side, and she looked down at it, then back at me.

“Come on.”

Getting up hurt badly enough that I couldn’t answer. She waited. When I had my feet under me, she drew my arm across her shoulders and put her own around my waist.

We tried a step. Stopped. Adjusted. Another. Neither of us could carry the other now.

But together, we moved.

The blue light appeared beyond the bend, small and steady. I stopped looking at my feet.

“There,” Voeren breathed.

Pier Four stretched over the canal, its decking silver in the dawn. We kept moving. Her grip tightened when my knee faltered, and a few steps later I felt her weight settle harder against me. I held what I could. The light stayed ahead.

Then a low pulse passed through my chest. Sound followed, building above the roofs. Voeren lifted her head.

“There you are.”

The tilt-rotor came over the canal, dark against the pale sky. As it banked, a faded red cross showed against its storm-gray tail. Downwash flattened the water, and spray raced across the pier and struck my face. I ducked toward my shoulder, and Voeren tightened her arm around me.

“Tilt-rotor,” she shouted. “Fancy.”

I tried to laugh. My legs weren’t working properly. The light blurred.

_Almost._

The aircraft settled. A ramp lowered. People ran toward us.

“Echo Three-One? Identify!”

“Sergeant Spade.”

Too quiet. I tried again. “Signals. Anvil—”

“Staff Sergeant Voeren!” Her voice carried where mine wouldn’t. “Anvil Two. We’re the two who walked out.”

Hands reached for me, and I flinched. “Easy. We’ve got you.” A medic took my weight, and Voeren’s arm slipped from my waist. For a moment I tried to follow it.

“Stay with me,” the medic said. “We’re putting you down.”

A stretcher beneath my legs. Hands behind my shoulders. My back caught, and I tried to tell them. “Back,” I managed.

“Keep still. Don’t try to sit up.” Someone steadied my head. “Neck support.”

“Possible spinal.”

The sky rocked above me.

“Look at these hands.”

I tried to look down.

“Keep your head still.”

Something warm closed around my fingers. Voeren. I hadn’t seen her take my hand. A medic stood beside her, speaking sharply, trying to get her to sit. She nodded, and kept looking at me.

“Hey.”

My fingers moved against hers. “Hey.”

“You did good.”

I wanted to tell her she had too. That I was glad she was here. That she needed to sit down before she fell again.

“Staff Sergeant,” I said.

“Voeren,” she corrected, gently.

Rank was what my mouth reached for, the way it does when I’m too tired to be anything else. The name took longer. I made myself find it.

“Voeren.”

Her thumb moved across my knuckles. Keeping my eyes open had become difficult.

“I’m going to…” I stopped for a breath. “Pass out now.”

“Yeah.”

Her fingers tightened. The stretcher shifted, and I held on before I could help it. She held on too.

“I know,” she said. “I’ve got you.”

Sky slid above me. For a little longer, there was her hand.

Then nothing.
