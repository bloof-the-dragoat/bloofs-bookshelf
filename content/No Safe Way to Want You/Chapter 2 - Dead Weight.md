## Chapter 2 — Dead Weight

>_Content note: Graphic violence, strangulation aftermath, injury and blood._

The pressure vanished.

For a second, Spade couldn’t understand why. The hand was still around their throat. The man was still above them.

Then his fingers opened.

Air scraped into their lungs.

His face went slack. A wet, startled grunt escaped him, followed by a crack so sharp Spade felt it in their teeth. His head turned farther than it should have.

He fell sideways, striking the concrete beside them.

Blood spattered Spade’s cheek.

Voeren stood behind him.

Swaying.

Spade dragged in another breath and coughed until their ribs hurt. Their throat seemed determined to close around every scrap of air they managed to get through it.

A minute ago the dark had been simple. No future in it. No rank, no failed jobs, no ordinary life they were never going to reach. Just the end of the chase.

They had been wrong about that too.

They wanted the next breath. They wanted it badly enough to hate how much it hurt.

Voeren’s left arm hung stiff against her side. A muscle jumped in her jaw. Under the blood and grime, her face had gone a color Spade didn’t want to recognize. Lighter than theirs, even now. Golden where the strobe caught her, and wrong with it.

But she was upright.

She was looking at them.

Then, somehow, she smiled.

Not the grin from the pump house. This one was small and exhausted, and Spade could have stared at it for the rest of their life.

“Hey.” Her voice shook. “You okay?”

Spade tried to answer. A cough came first.

“I’m—” They swallowed, winced, and tried again. “I’m alive.”

“Good.”

Voeren lowered herself onto one knee with the care of someone negotiating a bad landing. Her hand went to her flank.

The bleeding had slowed, though her shirt was soaked. Along the burned edges of the wound, raw pink tissue had begun to gather. Spade watched her fingers tremble against it.

“You put my shooter down,” she murmured. “And dropped half the depot on his friend.”

A laugh caught in Spade’s throat and turned into something painful. “You saw that?”

“Heard enough.” Her mouth tipped at one corner. “Horn. Chain. A lot of screaming. Put it together.”

A minute ago, they had been certain she would never speak again.

Now she was kneeling beside them, making jokes.

Spade didn’t know whether to laugh or be sick.

“Most people can’t think with somebody trying to kill them,” Voeren continued. “You can.”

They opened their mouth, then closed it.

Praise sat wrong when it was about the part of them that worked after everything else had gone quiet. They almost said it wasn’t like that. They almost apologized for needing her to finish what they couldn’t.

Anything they might have said felt embarrassingly small beside the way she was looking at them.

Her gaze moved toward the tool crib.

“There was a hatch. I saw it before I went down.”

Spade looked at the floor. The alarm was still sounding. With every pulse of the strobes, the dead man’s boots appeared beside their leg.

“You could’ve left.”

They wiped a hand across their mouth. Someone else’s blood came away on their fingers.

“You stayed,” Voeren said, more quietly.

Something tightened beneath Spade’s ribs.

People said things like that and then moved on. _It’ll be fine. You worry too much._ This didn’t sound like that. She had heard the horn and the chain and still come back to the part where Spade had not gone through the hatch.

“I just—” Swallowing hurt. “Didn’t want you to die.”

Voeren watched them for a moment. Then her hand settled on their shoulder, careful and warm through the uniform.

“I know.” Her fingers pressed once. “Thank you.”

Spade nodded at the concrete.

Orders would have been easier. A question. Something broken that needed fixing.

Anything but this.

_Get it together. You’re still on the floor._

Their knee throbbed. Their back hurt. Neither of them knew who else might come through those doors.

_Think about that. Not her hand._

The hand stayed anyway. Warm. Specific. Not safety. They knew better than to call it that, and their body tried to anyway.

Spade pushed themself upright.

The room tilted.

Voeren’s grip tightened until the floor settled beneath them again. They drew their left foot closer and tested the knee, keeping most of their weight on the other leg.

It held.

That was all they knew. It held, and it hurt, and they couldn’t afford a more complicated answer yet.

“Knee’s twisted,” Spade reported. “Back’s bad.” They paused for a breath. “I can walk. Barely.”

Their eyes found the southern doorway.

“The canal. We might get a better signal there. If we cross the rail bridge by the old pump station—”

Voeren was watching their face instead of the route.

Spade lost the rest of the sentence.

“What?”

“You’re doing it again.”

“Doing what?”

“Planning.”

They glanced toward the doorway. “Someone has to.”

“Yeah.” The amusement softened around her eyes. “Someone does.”

Their face warmed.

“It’s just—”

“It’s not just anything.”

They had no answer that wouldn’t sound like they were asking her to keep looking at them.

Voeren pushed herself upright, swayed, and caught a pillar. She stayed there for a beat, fingers spread against the concrete, before letting go.

“Come on. We’re moving.”

Spade braced a hand against the shelving. “I can walk—”

“Sure you can.”

She bent before they could explain, slid one arm beneath their knees and the other behind their back, and lifted.

Spade grabbed her shoulder.

“What—Voeren!”

“You said barely.” She settled them against her chest. “Barely’s not fast enough.”

Every useful thought left their head.

The woman they had spent eight months trying not to stare at was holding them against her.

That was the situation.

Apparently, this was something Spade now had to survive as well.

She was warm through the damp uniform and the bulk of their vest. Her forearm shifted beneath their knees, scarred where their own arm was only ink under a sleeve. A torn edge of cloth brushed their wrist. Their cheek hovered close enough to her shoulder that they could smell gun oil, sweat, blood—and something clean beneath it all.

Spade’s hand tightened against the back of her shirt.

_Oh, no. Oh god._

Not hollow. Not logical. If the flat had been going to save them from this, it would have started already.

“Comfortable?” Voeren asked.

“Fine,” they croaked.

She glanced down, and the corner of her mouth moved.

“You’re blushing.”

“I’m not.”

“Really?”

Spade inspected a dead light fitting above the doorway with desperate concentration.

“You’re blushing pretty hard.”

“Can we please move?”

Her laugh was quieter this time. They felt it beneath their palm.

“It’s cute.”

Spade shut their eyes.

_Absolutely not. We are not doing this right now._

Then she started running.

Each footfall travelled through Voeren’s arms into Spade’s back. They clenched their teeth and tried to make themself easier to carry, though they had no idea how to do that without moving something that hurt.

She turned her shoulders to clear a narrow passage. Pipes slid past overhead. Water splashed beneath her boots.

Behind them, the loading-bay alarm grew faint.

The rail bridge rose out of the dark ahead, its old planks suspended over black water.

Spade looked back over her shoulder.

No movement.

No one following that they could see.

Their hand was resting against the back of her neck.

They noticed this with considerable alarm.

Considered moving it.

Couldn’t find anywhere less intimate to put it.

_She’s carrying me._

After the pump house and the rifle and the man kneeling on their chest, there was this: an arm beneath their legs, a secure hold behind their back, and the terrible ease with which their body wanted to mistake being held for being safe.

They had wanted, for months, for someone to actually see them. This was not that. This was weight and blood and a woman who might fall if they asked the wrong thing.

It still felt like a light had been set down in the middle of the yard.

Then Voeren’s next step landed late.

A small hitch. A sharp catch in her breathing.

Spade lifted their head.

“Voeren?”

“I’m fine.”

She reached the bridge. The planks drummed beneath her boots.

One arm trembled before she tightened it again.

“You’re hurt.”

“Noticed.”

It might have been funny if she’d had enough breath to spare.

“Put me down,” Spade urged.

“We’re almost across.”

Her breathing had shortened. Each inhale seemed deliberate now, something she had to remember to do. Spade looked at the blood along her side.

_She was hurt when she reached me._

Before the charged rifle.

Before she picked them up.

“Voeren. Put me down. I can—”

“No.”

The word came out ragged.

She carried them off the bridge into a narrow lane between the warehouse wall and the canal. Metal posts lined the water’s edge. Farther ahead, a railing caught the first gray light above the roofs.

Voeren took another step.

Her grip tightened.

Then her knee folded.

Spade felt her twist, arms drawing them closer as she tried to control the fall. For an instant their face pressed against her shoulder.

Then her hold failed.

The ground came up sideways.

Their back struck the bricks.

Pain emptied the world.

They rolled against the base of a metal post and lay there, unable to understand the ringing in their ears.

It thinned.

Their own breath came through, broken and much too loud.

Spade opened their eyes.

Voeren lay several feet away with one arm outstretched.

“Voeren?”

She didn’t move.

The wanting didn’t leave. It just had nowhere to go.

Spade tried to stand. Their left knee gave before they could straighten it, and they caught themself hard on both hands.

“Voeren!”

They crawled.

Brick scraped their palms. Each movement pulled at their back, but stopping felt worse. They reached her, pressed two fingers beneath her jaw, and waited.

_Come on._

There.

Fast. Faint beneath their shaking hand.

“Oh, thank God.” Their breath fell apart around the words. “Okay. Okay.”

Her chest lifted.

Spade watched it happen again.

“You’re alive.”

Blood was seeping from her flank.

They pulled the medical pouch from her belt and struggled with the fastening until it came free. Tore open the dressing packet. Pressed gauze against the wound.

Voeren didn’t flinch.

Spade looked at her face.

“Come on,” they whispered.

Nothing.

They secured the dressing as well as they could, checked her breathing again, and sat back on their heels.

Their hands were red.

_She should’ve stopped._

The thought went around and around, useless and insistent.

_She should’ve put me down._

Spade looked toward the bridge.

No one.

Ahead, the lane disappeared beyond the buildings.

The panic was still there. Under it, something else arrived, and they hated the shape of it.

Quiet. Not peace. The part of them that showed up when there was nothing left to feel with. Work, stripped to the next true thing.

_Shit. Focus._

There had to be something.

The handset pressed against their thigh.

_The radio._

They pulled it from their pocket and set it on the bricks.

Something they understood. Not easy. Not certain.

But something.

The screen was dead.

Spade turned the handset in their hands, examining the cracked casing, the battery mount, the antenna connection. Damage to the outside didn’t mean everything inside was gone.

Their hands had stopped shaking as much as they should have.

They noticed. Filed it. Did not thank it.

They released the battery.

A broken corner of the housing had caught against a contact. Spade worked it free, checked the terminals, and reached into their own pouch for the spare pack.

They fitted it.

A light flickered.

Went out.

“Come on.”

Spade eased the split casing apart. The movement pulled at their back, and they had to stop with the radio balanced against their knee until the worst of it passed.

Inside, a connector had worked loose.

They pressed it home.

The light returned.

Stayed.

Spade stared at it, afraid to trust something so small.

Then they keyed the handset. Their voice came out flat, exact, the cordial lift gone. Better for a net. Worse as a person.

“Kestrel Control, Kestrel Control, this is Echo Three-One. Do you copy, over?”

Static.

They shifted closer to the wall and tried again.

“Kestrel Control, Echo Three-One.”

Nothing.

Behind them, Voeren drew a shallow breath.

The flat cracked.

Spade looked back.

Still breathing.

They turned toward the canal before the crack could widen. The railing ran along the lane ahead: weathered steel bolted into the masonry. Their gaze moved between it and the damaged antenna connection.

_Maybe._

They drew their knife and worked at the connection.

Their fingers wouldn’t stay steady. Twice they missed what they were trying to catch. The third time, they braced their wrist against a knee and made themself slow down.

It was ugly.

They knew it was ugly.

But between the handset, the knife, and the railing, they might get enough improvement to make themselves heard.

Sequence. Angle. Key. Listen.

This was the version of them the army kept promoting. Empty enough to be useful.

Spade shifted between two metal posts and raised the radio.

“Kestrel Control. Echo Three-One. How copy?”

The hiss thickened.

They changed the angle.

“Any station on this net, this is Echo Three-One.”

Water lapped against the canal wall. Somewhere behind them, loose fabric stirred in the wind.

“I have a casualty. Staff Sergeant down. Request—”

Their voice cracked.

There she was again, even unconscious. A fact that would not stay a fact.

Spade stopped. Swallowed against the pain.

“Any station. Echo Three-One.”

The static broke.

“—Three-One—Kestrel—say again—”

They froze.

For a second, they were afraid to move anything, even their mouth.

Then they pressed the key.

“Kestrel, Echo Three-One. I read you weak. How do you read me?”

“—trying to raise you—sitrep—”

“Two survivors. Staff Sergeant Voeren and myself. She’s wounded, unconscious, breathing. Flank wound.”

The words were crowding together. Logic tried to space them. Her name would not stay in its slot.

Spade forced a breath between them.

“I’m injured but mobile. Request immediate MEDEVAC.”

“—Copy. Stand by.”

They lowered their forehead toward the cold railing.

Someone had heard.

Beyond the warehouses, beyond the interference and the blood and this endless fucking night, someone knew they were alive.

Their eyes burned.

Not the hollow. The hollow didn’t do this.

Spade wiped at them with a sleeve and left a smear across their face.

“Kestrel?”

No answer.

They looked back at Voeren. At the hand lying open beside her.

_Please._

“Kestrel, she’s—”

The handset crackled.

“Echo Three-One. Kestrel. Interference still heavy at your position. We can’t bring the aircraft in there. Can you move?”

Spade stared down the lane.

“How far?”

“Pier Four. Old ferry slip, southwest along the canal. Approximately five hundred meters.”

_No._

“Dustoff is airborne,” the operator continued. “Twenty-five minutes out. Ten-minute holding window after that.”

Spade looked at their knee.

At the blood spreading through Voeren’s dressing.

“Say again the distance.”

“Five hundred meters. Follow the canal wall. Blue light on the pier.”

Of course.

Of course it couldn’t be here.

“Echo, can you move?”

_No._

They could barely stand. Moving Voeren across the loading bay had taken everything they had, and now their knee was worse and their back—

_How the fuck am I supposed to—_

The clean answer was no. A logical sergeant would have said the casualty could not be moved that far by one injured soldier and asked for another window.

Spade looked at her.

A strand of hair had caught against her mouth.

They reached back and brushed it away.

The flat did not survive the touch.

“Yeah.”

Their own voice startled them.

Spade pressed the key more firmly.

“I’ll move her. Tell them to hold.”

The drag sheet was strapped to Voeren’s pack.

Spade had passed casualty drills with one. Clean floor. Cooperative partner. Someone nearby to explain what they had done wrong.

They had never hated a memory so much.

First, the knife.

They worked it free of the railing, wiped the rust and grit against their trousers, and returned it to the sheath behind their right hip.

Then the sheet.

Gray polymer. Webbing loops.

Familiar things.

Spade spread it beside her and started moving.

It took nearly six minutes to get Voeren onto it.

They worked in small increments, stopping when their back locked or their knee threatened to fold. Checked the dressing. Checked her face. Apologized when her shoulder struck the bricks, though she gave no sign of hearing.

At last she lay on the sheet.

Spade crouched beside her with their head hanging.

Sweat dripped from their nose onto the polymer.

_Get up._

They gathered the webbing.

Their body offered no cooperation.

_Get. Up._

Their good leg straightened. The other followed badly.

Spade leaned into the straps.

The sheet scraped forward.

A foot.

Another.

They reached the next post and collapsed against it, breathing through their mouth.

Behind them, a pale scrape marked the wet bricks.

That was all they had managed.

_Four hundred and ninety-eight._

Spade shut their eyes.

For a moment, the number was too large to think about.

So they stopped thinking about it.

_Next post._

They pulled.

Pull. Step. Set the foot.

Pull again.

It became a sequence small enough to obey.

When their knee flared, Spade shifted weight off it. When their back seized, they leaned against the railing and waited until they could straighten.

Then they moved.

The webbing rubbed their hands raw. They changed their grip, then changed it back when the new position hurt worse.

This was the closest the hollow could get while she was still the reason for the work. Not gone. Narrowed. Next post, next breath, next check that the dressing had not slipped.

Behind them, Voeren shifted.

Spade turned immediately.

Her head had rolled to one side.

Still breathing.

They watched the movement beneath her shirt until the next breath came.

Their gaze lingered on the line of her shoulder. Her exposed forearm, scarred and heavy with muscle, lighter than the hands pulling her. The fingers resting loose against the polymer.

It was strange seeing her hand open.

They had watched it tear a door aside.

Then it had settled on their shoulder, gentle enough to make them forget where they were.

_Thank you._

Spade’s face warmed.

Here. With blood drying between their fingers and their back hurting so badly they wanted to fold around it.

_Seriously?_

They turned away and pulled.

For months, Voeren had existed in fragments: a laugh across a room, a broad back disappearing through a doorway, a glance Spade had looked away from too quickly.

Now they knew the weight of her arm beneath their knees. The roughness in her voice when she was hurt. That she would make a joke when she could barely stand.

That she knew their name.

_God, she’s—_

The sheet caught against a seam.

Spade stumbled and gripped the railing.

_Alive. She’s alive. Keep it that way._

They reset their feet and pulled again.

By the time the pain forced its way back to the center of their attention, they had covered more ground than they expected.

Not enough.

But more.

Across the canal, headlights swept the far bank.

Spade stopped.

Their hands tightened on the straps. The beams crossed a warehouse wall, swung away, and vanished. An engine faded into the distance.

They waited another breath.

Then hauled.

The lane opened ahead. Buildings fell back from the water, leaving a stretch of quay exposed to the wind.

No shelter.

No easy place to stop.

The sky above the roofs had turned pale.

Spade looked back, trying to estimate the distance from the buildings and the gaps between access lanes.

Two hundred meters, perhaps.

Their throat hurt too much to swallow.

They kept moving.

At the next rough patch, the sheet caught again.

Spade pulled harder.

Nothing.

A broken edge of paving had folded beneath the front of the polymer.

They stared at it.

Such a small thing.

A stupid piece of brick.

For a moment, they wanted to sit down beside it and stay there. Not die. Just stop. The old math: not enough left to want the next thing, and not enough force to quit.

Voeren’s chest lifted.

Spade went back, freed the corner, and returned to the straps.

Another task.

Another thing done.

Three hundred meters.

Maybe.

Their left leg shook whenever they asked it to bear weight. Wetness gathered inside their grip, and they refused to look at their palms.

Ahead, the canal wall bent.

Pier Four should be beyond it.

_To the bend._

Spade leaned forward.

_Just to the bend._

When they stopped again, their breathing had become a harsh, scraping sound they barely recognized as their own.

They sank onto their good knee, straps clenched in both hands.

A minute.

They needed a minute.

“Hey.”

Voeren’s voice was barely more than breath.

The sequence broke.

Spade lifted their head.

Her eyes were open.

Spade stared.

They had imagined this often enough along the lane that, for a second, they didn’t trust it.

Then Voeren blinked.

Her gaze moved from their face to the webbing in their fists, then past them, following the scraped track along the bricks.

Back to their face.

“You—” Her voice failed. She swallowed. “You dragged me?”

“Yeah.”

Spade’s own voice barely worked. They glanced toward the bend, because looking at her was already too much feeling for a body that had just been counting posts.

“Pier Four’s ahead. Hundred and twenty meters. Maybe a bit more.”

Voeren didn’t look.

“How far?”

“From where you—”

“How far did you come?”

Spade looked back.

“Three-seventy-ish.”

She went quiet.

The wind lifted a strand of hair from her cheek.

Under her attention, Spade became painfully aware of their own body: kneeling badly, arms shaking, mouth hanging open because they couldn’t get enough air through their nose.

They must have looked terrible.

The flat was gone. They missed its usefulness and not the rest of it.

“I wasn’t going to leave you.”

The words sounded obvious once spoken. They tried to make them less so.

“I just—it took longer than I thought. You’re really heavy. Not—”

Voeren’s eyebrows shifted.

“Not like that. I mean, you’ve got a lot of—”

Spade closed their eyes.

“I’m going to stop talking.”

A small sound escaped her.

They looked again.

Voeren was laughing.

Quietly, until it caught at her side and made her wince. She pressed a hand against the dressing, shook her head, and laughed anyway.

Spade tried not to join her.

Failed immediately.

Their laugh came out broken and breathless. That made hers worse. For a few seconds both of them were on the cold quay, filthy and hurt and laughing at something that wasn’t even funny.

Spade’s eyes watered.

This did not fix the night. It did not fix the years under it. It was only her, awake, trying.

That was already more than they had thought they would get.

Voeren took a careful breath.

“Almost four hundred meters.”

They looked down at their hands.

“On that knee.”

Spade shifted.

“It’s what anyone would—”

“No.”

Her hand rose slowly and settled over theirs, where they still gripped the strap.

“Not anyone.”

Spade looked up.

Voeren held their gaze without a trace of teasing.

They could have managed a joke. Something easy to dismiss once the heat left their face.

There was nowhere to put this.

Most people did not try. The ones who tried a little still missed. She was looking at the drag, the knee, the stupid apology, and refusing the smaller version.

Spade did not know what to do with being known for three minutes on a quay. They wanted it anyway.

Her fingers closed around their hand.

“I’m not going to forget this.” Her voice had dropped. “Okay?”

Spade nodded.

“Not a damn minute of it.”

Water moved against the canal wall. The depot alarm still sounded somewhere behind them, thin with distance.

For the first time since she had fallen, Spade wasn’t the only person listening.

Voeren drew a breath and pushed herself onto one elbow.

“All right, Sergeant.” She glanced toward the bend. “Let’s get the hell out of here.”

“Wait. You shouldn’t—”

She stopped halfway upright, jaw tight.

Spade reached for her.

Voeren caught their arm, steadied herself, and drew one boot beneath her.

“Not leaving you to do the rest.”

She stood.

Swayed.

Both waited.

Blood had darkened the dressing at her flank. Voeren looked down at it with an expression Spade couldn’t read, then offered her hand.

“Come on.”

Getting upright hurt badly enough that Spade couldn’t speak.

She waited until they had set their feet, then drew their arm across her shoulders and settled her own around their waist.

They tried a step.

Stopped.

Adjusted.

Another.

Neither could carry the other now.

But together, they moved.

The blue light appeared beyond the bend.

Small. Steady.

Spade had never seen anything so beautiful.

“There,” Voeren breathed.

They nodded, though she wasn’t looking at them.

Pier Four extended over the canal, its weathered decking silver in the dawn. The last stretch seemed to lengthen as they approached.

Spade kept their eyes on the light.

Not the distance.

The light.

Then a low pulse passed through their chest.

Sound followed, building above the roofs.

Voeren lifted her head.

“There you are.”

The tilt-rotor came over the canal, dark against the pale sky. Its wingtip nacelles had turned upward. As it banked, a faded red cross showed against the storm-gray tail.

Downwash flattened the water.

Spray raced across the pier and struck Spade’s face. They ducked toward their shoulder.

Voeren tightened her arm around them.

“Tilt-rotor,” she shouted. “Fancy.”

Spade tried to laugh.

Their legs weren’t working properly.

The light blurred.

_Almost._

The aircraft settled. A ramp lowered.

People ran toward them.

_Just—_

“Echo Three-One? Identify!”

The medic’s voice cut through the roar.

“Sergeant Spade.”

Too quiet.

They tried again.

“Signals. Anvil—”

“Staff Sergeant Voeren!” Her voice carried where theirs wouldn’t. “Anvil Two. We’re the two who walked out.”

Hands reached for them.

Spade flinched.

“Easy. We’ve got you.”

A medic took their weight. Voeren’s arm loosened, and for a second, losing it frightened Spade more than falling had.

Not because she had made them well.

Because the dark was still there, and she had been the only warm thing in it.

Then there was a stretcher. Hands guiding them down.

Their back hurt. They tried to explain, but their throat failed them.

“Keep still.” The medic beside their shoulder leaned into view. “We’ve got you. Don’t try to sit up.”

Another pair of hands steadied their head.

“Neck support.”

“Possible spinal.”

Someone near their feet drew a sharp breath.

“Look at their hands.”

Spade lowered their gaze without moving their head.

Voeren held one of their hands.

They hadn’t noticed her take it.

A medic stood at her other side, speaking sharply, trying to get her to sit. She listened. Nodded once.

Kept looking at Spade.

“Hey.”

Their fingers moved against hers.

“Hey.”

“You did good.”

The words reached them through the rotors, through the pain, through the strange distance opening between their body and everything around it.

They wanted to answer properly.

Tell her she had too. Tell her they were glad. Tell her not to make a life out of one clean report and a drag sheet.

Their mouth wouldn’t hold that many words.

“Staff Sergeant.”

“Voeren,” she corrected gently.

They found the name.

“Voeren.”

Her thumb moved across their knuckles.

Spade tried to keep their eyes open. It had become difficult in a way that no longer seemed negotiable.

“I’m going to…”

They took a breath.

“Pass out now.”

Her fingers tightened.

“Yeah.”

They could barely hear her, but her mouth was close enough to read. The tired curve of it. Blood drying along her jaw.

“I know. I’ve got you.”

The stretcher moved.

Sky slid above them.

Spade held on as long as they could.

Then there was only the warmth of her hand.

Then nothing.
