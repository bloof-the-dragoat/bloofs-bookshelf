
# Chapter 10: Fast Enough

> [!warning] Content Note  
> Graphic violence, injury, and killing.

---

The rifle came up the way a decision does, all at once and without any flourish.

I was on my feet in the salt grass with a tag receiver in my vest and a burning truck behind me. Between me and the pump station lay the ditch, the road, and eighty meters of open gravel. The man on the roof had dropped the empty launcher and shouldered a rifle in the time it took me to stand up. I watched his cheek settle against the stock and felt the world go narrow.

_Single shooter. Readable._

It wasn't a thought so much as a list, and I'd been building it all morning without knowing. A muzzle swings, then stops. A body that has decided stops breathing for half a second. I couldn't see his finger, but I could see the line of his shoulder, and I saw it set.

I moved before the rifle did.

The shot cracked out across the road, and a fist of gravel kicked up exactly where my chest had been. I'd gone left, a long, ugly step that I felt in my hips. He adjusted. I watched the muzzle swing, and I watched it stop again, and I went right. The second round went by with a sound like tearing cloth.

_Don't stay in one place. Don't be where the stop is._

I ran.

The first stride was ordinary. The second wasn't. Something in my legs let go, and the ground began to flow under me like a belt, and the pump station, which had been a long way off, came at me with a speed that I'd never felt in my life. The wind hit my face. The road blurred. I saw a third muzzle flash and didn't hear it, because I was already past the place where it had been.

_Stop._

I tried to stop. I leaned back, planted my heels, and nothing happened. I covered the last fifteen meters in the time it took to think the word, and I hit the wall of the pump station with my left shoulder at a speed no wall should have to accept.

The impact lit up my whole arm. Something in my collarbone made a wet crack. I slid down the concrete and sat in the dust with my teeth clenched, and for a second I couldn't tell which of my arms was still attached.

Then the heat began, the crawling, burning pull, and under it the bone slid back together with a sound I would remember for the rest of my life.

Above me, a boot scraped.

He couldn't shoot straight down. He'd have to lean out over the parapet to find me, and I'd be gone before he did. I made myself breathe. I looked at the pipe rack running up the side of the building, a rusted lattice of conduit bolted to the wall, and at my left hand, which had already stopped hurting.

I climbed.

It wasn't graceful. My fingers found the pipe and my feet found the bracket, and for three or four meters, everything worked. Then a surge went through my legs like a spring letting go, and I overshot a handhold by an arm's length, slammed into the wall, and caught the next pipe with one hand. My boots scraped the concrete. I hung there for a second with my heart in my throat.

_Smaller. Do it smaller._

I did it smaller. The last two meters were slow, and quiet, and ugly. Then I put my hand over the edge of the parapet and went over it.

He was waiting.

I'd hoped for a back. I got a gaiter and a black visor and a rifle already swinging around, and I had no time to think. I went in under it. The muzzle went past my ear, and I drove the knife at his throat.

It hit his collarbone.

The blade skidded off bone and plate and bit into nothing. His forearm came across like a bat and caught my ribs, and the air left me in a rush. I felt something crack, and I felt the roof tilt. A hand closed on my wrist, a hand like a vise, and I felt the bones in it begin to grind.

_Not the strike. The gap._

I twisted my wrist the way I'd been taught, against the thumb, and the grip broke. I brought the knife up again, and this time I didn't aim at the man. I aimed at the narrow wedge of throat between the bottom of his visor and the top of his collar, a hand's width of skin.

It went in.

I felt it go, and I felt his whole body convulse. Blood poured over my knuckles, hot and slick and endless, and he made a sound I hadn't known a person could make. His hand came up and found my face and pushed, and I pushed back with everything I had and twisted the blade.

I counted. I always count.

_One. Two. Three._

His strength went on for a long time. His boots scraped the roof. He tried to scream, and the sound came out as a wet whistle.

_Eight. Nine._

He stopped at eleven.

I let go. He slid down the parapet and lay on his side with his head turned and his visor pointing at the sky. Blood spread out under him in a dark sheet. I stood over him with my hands open at my sides and my pulse in my ears, and the roof tilted again.

_Don't._

I bent over and put my hands on my knees. I breathed in and out until it passed. It wasn't triumph and it wasn't horror. It was something flat and heavy that I had no name for, like a coin that had landed on its edge.

A radio crackled at his belt.

"_Roof, status._"

A flat, bored voice. I looked down at the handset. A small green light blinked on its side.

I'd listened to a thousand check-ins. I knew what a person does when he's too busy to talk, and I knew what the voice wanted. I knelt, unclipped the radio, and keyed the mic twice, a dry, quick, clipped _click-click._

A pause.

"_Copy. Eyes on the road._"

The light went out. I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding.

_That's two minutes. Maybe three._

His vest had a flat pocket on the chest. I opened it with a hand that wasn't quite steady. Inside was a foil packet, the kind they issued in the field, and when I tore the corner open, a dense, dark paste the color of brick lay inside.

I ate it standing over his body.

I ate every bit of it, and I didn't taste it, and the worst part was that I'd have eaten it either way. The hunger didn't care. It had waited, patient and merciless, through a rocket and a fall and a kill, and now it wanted more. I folded the foil in half and put it in my pocket without knowing why.

I looked at my knife. It was clean to the guard, and red from there to the tip.

_The speed got me up the wall._ I wiped it on his sleeve. _The blade did the killing. And the blade only worked when I put it in the gap, not at the man._

It was the only thing I'd learned all morning that I was sure was true.

---

I went north.

The map in my head was the one I'd made in the briefing room at 0358, and I'd stared at it until the shapes had burned into my eyes. The canal ran along my right. The old rail spur crossed it at the bridge, and past the bridge, the road split. The annex stood at the north end of the complex, five or six hundred meters ahead, a low gray block against a gray sky.

I couldn't see it. I could smell it.

It wasn't a smell I could have described. It was somewhere between cold concrete and old blood, with a thread of ozone running through it. It came and went on the wind, and every time it came, the back of my neck went tight.

I ran for forty meters and stopped.

I wasn't tired. That was what was strange. I was hungry and hot and raw, and I felt as though I'd run ten kilometers, but my legs weren't tired. Something in me was _spending_, and I could feel it going out like a tide. Every burst of speed was a coin. I had a limited number of coins.

_Use them where they count._

I took a breath. I looked at the open ground ahead, a flat strip of gravel between two rusted sheds, and I thought: _the first step._

On the roof, I'd gone wrong in the second stride. The first one had been ordinary. The second had carried me past the thing I wanted. If I could hold myself to that first step, if I could let the speed come in a half-stride and then catch it, drop my weight, and brake the way I'd braked on a loose road a hundred times—

I tried it.

I went from a standstill to a half-stride and stopped, and it worked. My boot skidded in the gravel. I'd covered six meters in the time it should have taken to cover two, and I'd stopped exactly where I meant to. I did it again. Again. By the fifth time, I wasn't thinking about it.

_Fast enough to get there. Slow enough to stop._

That was the whole lesson, and I couldn't have put it into words.

The first picket stood by the old gantry crane.

There were two of them, a few meters apart, in the same black gear as the man on the roof. They stood with their backs to a rusted sheet of siding and their rifles slung, talking in low voices and looking at the road. One of them was eating something out of a foil packet. I watched him lift it to his mouth, and my stomach turned over so hard it hurt.

_Not yet._

I looked for the wire.

I found it at once: a thin copper loop strung along the fence posts at ankle height, running from post to post and into the shed. It wasn't radio. It was an old wired tripwire, the kind they put on the doors of warehouses, with no signal to jam and no battery to die. I'd have missed it a day ago. Now, with the world sharp as glass, it stood out against the rust like a scratch on a lens.

_They know about the jammer. They're not using the air._

I stepped over it.

I took the long way around the shed, keeping low, keeping the metal between me and their line of sight. I waited for the wind to carry their voices away. When the one with the packet turned his head to spit, I moved.

The first step. A half-stride, a blur of gravel, and I was at his back.

I didn't think. I put the knife into the gap under his jaw, behind the hinge of the bone, where the blade goes up and the artery is. It went in clean. He made a small, surprised sound, and his hand came up, and I turned the blade and pulled it out.

The other man had started to turn.

_Second step._

I let the burst come and stopped it exactly where I meant to. I wasn't behind him. I was at his side, my left hand across his mouth, and the knife went into the soft hollow at the base of his skull. His body went rigid. His hand scrabbled at my wrist. I held on and felt his heart stutter against my chest.

I counted. _Six. Seven._

He went down.

Behind me, the first man lay folded against the siding, still dying. I looked at him and made myself keep looking. It took eight seconds. He didn't make a sound.

I stood there with my pulse in my ears and thought: _That was clean._

It was the wrong word. It was the only one I had.

Both of them had packets. I knelt and took them. One was nearly full, the other half-eaten with a thumbprint still on the foil, and I ate both without tasting a mouthful. There was a canteen on the first man's belt. I drank it dry. The cold water went into my stomach like iron, and for a few seconds, the hunger eased enough that I could think.

I folded the foil and put it away with the other.

I checked the tag receiver.

The little screen blinked once and showed a number. _51._ It was higher than before. It stayed for a full two seconds before the display went dark.

_She's awake._

I put the receiver away and kept moving.

---

The annex rose out of the flat ground like a tooth.

It was a long, low rectangle of poured concrete with no windows on this side, no doors, and a roofline broken by a single squat exhaust stack. I'd seen it in a drawing and on a screen. I'd never seen it with my own eyes, and now that I did, I understood why a person could hate a building.

I lay in a drainage cut two hundred meters out and watched it for ninety seconds.

Two sentries walked the roofline, bored and thorough. A vehicle stood at the foot of the south wall, a squat gray truck with its rear ramp lowered, and men were loading crates onto it. They were in no hurry. They'd left the south door sealed, and I knew the reason.

_They're not leaving. They're moving the crates. They're waiting for something._

The strike. They knew about the strike, or they'd guessed it, and they were taking what they could carry.

I looked at my watch. 0907.

_Twenty-three minutes._

I couldn't go in through the front. I'd known that since the briefing, and I'd known it every minute since. But there was something else I'd noticed, something I'd seen on a map and filed away without understanding why.

The north wall had a junction box.

It sat low against the concrete, a gray steel cabinet with a conduit running out of the top, and from where I lay, I could see a rectangle of darker shadow behind it. A cable duct. It was too small for a person, too small for any enhanced soldier I'd ever known.

It might have been just big enough for me.

I took a long loop around the east side, using the cut and a row of dead shipping containers. I crossed the last stretch of open ground in two controlled half-strides, with one stop to count the sentry's turn. I reached the wall, put my back against the concrete, and let out a breath.

The duct was exactly what I'd hoped for. It was a rectangular hole, forty centimeters by fifty, set at knee height and screened by a rusted grille held on with four bolts. I could have unscrewed them. I didn't have time. I took the knife and slid the blade behind the grille and levered, and the old metal popped free and I caught it before it fell.

I stood there with the grille in my hands and looked into the dark.

_Voeren would never fit._

The thought came on its own, and I stopped on it. It wasn't pride. I didn't feel proud. It was something quieter. She could throw a man through a wall, and she couldn't have followed me into this hole. I'd built my whole life to be small and quick and easy to miss, and for the first time it was the only thing that mattered.

I got down on my stomach and went in.

---

The duct smelled of dust and warm metal and old wire.

It ran straight for fifteen meters, then bent left, then narrowed. I crawled on my elbows with the carbine dragging behind me and my hips scraping both walls. The air was close and dry and tasted of copper. My heartbeat was a drum in the dark. Every sound I made was enormous. My own breathing filled the tunnel, and I held my breath in short bursts and listened.

At the second bend, a grate opened in the floor.

It was a square of slotted steel set into the duct, and light came through it in thin, pale stripes. Below it, a corridor. I could hear boots. They were slow and regular, two sets, one a half-step behind the other. A pair of guards on a patrol loop, walking the way tired men walk.

I lay on my stomach and looked down through the slots.

Concrete floor. A strip of emergency light. Two men in black gear, rifles slung, passing under the grate and moving on. One carried a canteen on his belt and a foil packet in a chest pocket. The other had a knife on his hip, a long matte-black combat blade, and I looked at it for a moment longer than I should have.

_I could use that._

I waited for them to reach the far end of the corridor, turn, and start back. I counted their steps. Forty-one down, forty-one back, and a pause at each end. The pause was about four seconds.

I put my fingers through the slots of the grate and tested it.

It lifted.

I let the next pass go by. When they turned at the far end, I eased the grate up and out, set it on the floor of the duct without a sound, and lowered myself through the opening feet first.

The drop was two and a half meters. I let go.

I landed in a crouch, and I'd meant it to be silent, and it was almost silent. A boot scuffed. I froze.

The guards stopped at the far end of the corridor, thirty meters away. One of them said something low. The other laughed. I held my breath, and the laugh died, and the boots started back toward me.

_Forty-one steps._

I pressed myself against the wall in the shadow of a conduit, and I let them come.

They were three meters away when the first one stopped. His head came up. He'd smelled something, or seen something, or his skin had registered the wrongness of a space that was supposed to be empty.

I moved.

The first step took me across the gap. I came up under his rifle, and the knife went into the side of his neck, behind the jaw, and I felt it cross the artery and I pulled. He folded without a sound. The second man had his rifle half up and his mouth half open.

_The throat._

I hit the larynx with the heel of my left hand, hard, and I felt the cartilage give, and his shout died unspoken in his chest. He staggered. I took the combat knife from his belt with my left hand, and I put it into the soft place under his arm, where the plates don't meet, and drove it up.

He went down on top of his friend.

I stood over them with a knife in each hand, my breath loud in the empty corridor. Two. Four, counting the roof and the pickets. My hands didn't shake. It surprised me, and then it frightened me, and then I put it away.

I checked their pockets.

Both had packets. I ate one standing up. The other I tore open and spread flat, and I took the foil and held it up against the light. A thin, clean, bright strip, three centimeters wide.

_Good._

I pressed it flat and slid it into my vest.

I took the combat knife, a heavier, better blade than my own, and slid it into my belt. I took a canteen. I looked at the man whose throat I'd crushed and waited for something to rise in me, and nothing did. I moved on.

---

The corridor ran north for forty meters, and then it ended in a door.

I passed the cold room first. It stood on the left, a heavy steel slab with a handle on the outside and a rubber seal around the edge, and a bead of white frost along the bottom. A compressor hummed somewhere beyond it, low and steady. The seal wasn't tight. A thread of air came out along the floor, thin and cold, and it carried a smell.

It was sweet and wrong.

I stopped.

I'd been smelling the world all morning, brine and diesel and the sharp, clean smell of my own sweat. This was something else. It was a smell I'd never had a name for, but my body knew it the way a rabbit knows a hawk. It wanted me to turn around.

_Don't._

I looked at the handle. It would have been easy. A hand on the steel, a pull, a look. I'd have learned something I would never be able to unlearn.

I thought of Haskins.

I took my hand off the door.

_I know better than to open anything I can't afford to close._

I walked past it. I didn't let myself have an opinion, and I didn't look back, and the smell followed me for a few steps and then it was gone.

The pressure door was at the end.

It was a gray steel disc set into the concrete, with a wheel in the center and a rubber gasket around its rim. To one side, bolted to the wall at shoulder height, hung a small gray box with a keypad and a status light. The light was red.

_Wired._

I knew it before I touched it. There was a flat steel conduit running from the top of the box down to the floor and along the wall, and it didn't carry a signal. It carried a current. The Combine had sealed the south door through this system and no radio at all.

I looked at the box for five seconds. Then I took the combat knife and slid the tip behind the faceplate and levered.

The plate came away.

Behind it, a tangle of wire and a small, dull relay block, the kind that had been old when I was a child. Six terminals. A coil. A spring-loaded contact that held a solenoid shut as long as the coil stayed dark.

_It doesn't want a code. It wants to be told it has one._

I'd spent four years learning how to lie to machines. I'd never done it with a knife in one hand and a dead man's foil in the other.

I took the strip of foil from my vest. I folded it in half, then in half again, until it was a stiff, narrow jumper no longer than my finger. I found the two terminals I wanted and I held my breath. I touched the foil across them.

The relay clicked.

Somewhere inside the wall, a bolt slid back. The red light on the box flickered, went amber, and then went green. The gasket sighed, and a thin line of gray light opened along the edge of the door, and cold air flowed out into the corridor with a sound like a long breath.

I stood still.

I held the door with two fingers, a hair's breadth open, and listened.

Beyond it, somebody was breathing.

---
The hall was bigger than it had any right to be.

I took it in through a gap no wider than my hand, and my first thought was _brine._ It had been a settling basin once, long ago, a cavernous concrete room built to hold the dirty water of the old plant. The roof was twelve meters up. A steel catwalk ran around the walls at half height, cut by dark stairwells. The floor sloped to a drain at the center, and the drain had been used.

Work lights hung from chains over the middle of the room. They threw hard white circles on the concrete and left the corners black. Nothing moved. I counted the shadows twice and found no one in them.

Then I looked at what was under the lights.

Two frames stood in the center of the floor, ten meters apart, bolted down through the concrete. They were steel, heavy, and built with an ugly, patient intelligence. Each one held a person upright, and each had cuffs at the wrists, at the ankles, and above the elbows and knees. A flat bar ran across the chest at the sternum, locking the shoulders back, and another curved behind the neck. I understood it at once. They'd been designed by someone who knew what to take from a strong person. A strong person needs leverage, a line of force to push or pull along. These frames cut every line.

The nearer one held Doyle.

I almost didn't know him. His helmet was gone and his armor had been stripped to a torn, dark undershirt, and his head hung forward with his chin on his chest. One side of his ribs had a long, black, wet stain. His breath came in short, wet pulls, with a rattle at the end of each one, and there was blood in the corner of his mouth.

The second frame held *her*.

I'd spent two hours imagining it. It was smaller than I'd imagined, and worse.

Her armor was gone. She was in an undershirt, dark with sweat and blood, and the left sleeve had been cut away from a burned and swollen shoulder. A second burn ran down her thigh where the charged rifle had hit. Her face was a ruin of bruises. A split ran through her bottom lip. The scar over her right eye had disappeared under a purple swelling, and one eye was almost shut. Dried blood ran from her nose down her chin.

Her head was up.

That was what I couldn't get past. Whatever they'd done, whatever dose they'd put in her, she was holding her head up, and I could see the effort of it in the cords of her neck.

_Not dead._

The relief hit me so hard that my knees went. I caught myself on the door.

_Don't. Not now._

I slid through the gap.

The cold hit me first, then the smell, iron and sweat and something sweeter beneath it. My boots made no sound on the concrete. I crossed the first five meters in a half-stride, stopped in a patch of shadow, and listened to the hall breathe. Doyle's rattle. The creak of the frames. A drip somewhere far off.

I took another step.

Her head turned.

Her good eye found me in the dark. It was pale gray-green, rimmed in red, and it didn't understand what it saw. I watched it travel from my boots to my face, and I watched it stop.

"Spade?"

It was barely a voice. It cracked on the second syllable and broke.

I was across the floor before I decided to be.

"Hey," I said. It came out ridiculous. My voice shook. "Hey. Hey, I'm here."

"You're—" She stared at me. She was breathing fast, shallow and careful, the way you breathe around a broken rib. "You're _here._"

"Yeah."

"How—" Her eye dropped to my hands, to the knives, to the blood on my sleeves, and came up again slowly. "What did you—"

"Later."

"_Spade._"

"*Later*." I put my hand against her cheek before I could think about it, and she turned into it. For one second, one single second in the middle of that cold, white room, her whole face went still and her eyes closed. I felt her breath on my wrist. "I've got to get you out of this."

"You idiot." It was a whisper. Her mouth moved around it. It was the warmest thing anyone had ever said to me.

"I know."

"You can't be here."

"I know. I'm here."

She opened her eyes. They were wet, and furious, and glad, all at once.

"Doyle," she said.

I turned.

I crossed to him in four steps and put two fingers under his jaw. His skin was cold and clammy. The pulse under my fingers was thin and quick and uneven, like a bird's. His eyes rolled under their lids.

"He's alive," I said. "Barely."

"They—" Her voice caught. "They worked on him a long time. He wouldn't—"

"Don't."

"He wouldn't say anything."

"I know."

I didn't know. I took it on faith, the way you take a piece of someone else's grief, and I held it. Then I looked at the wall.

The release rack stood on the far side of the hall, a bank of long steel levers under a hooded lamp. I could see them from where I stood, six handles in a row, each tagged with a stained strip of tape. A wired panel, a hard line, no radio at all. I looked at the distance, thirty meters of open floor under the work lights, and I looked at Voeren.

"Pull them in order," she said.

"What?"

"The levers. Don't pull them all at once, they'll seize." Her voice was steadier now. It was the voice she used in the field, tight and clear, like a rope. "Top left is Doyle. It's hydraulic. You've got to hold it down."

"How do you know?"

"I watched them do it." Her jaw tightened. "Twice."

I looked at her for a moment longer. I thought of everything she must have watched, and I put it down very carefully.

"Okay."

"Spade." She caught my eye. "It's too quiet."

I'd been thinking it for a full minute. The hall had no guards. There were no sentries on the catwalk, and no one in the stairwells. A room that held a prisoner like her, a prisoner they'd gone to this much trouble to build a cage for, and nobody watching it.

"I know," I said.

"It's a—"

"I _know._"

I turned and started across the floor.

I took it in the first step, the controlled half-stride, and then another. The work lights threw my shadow in front of me, long and thin. Thirty meters. Twenty. Behind me, I heard Voeren's breath catch, and I didn't turn. Fifteen. I could see the tape on the levers now, the handwritten numbers, a stain on the first handle that was exactly the size of a palm.

Ten.

I reached the rack, put my hand on the first lever, and the lights went out.

---

The dark was total.

It came down all at once, with a hollow _thunk_ from somewhere overhead, and the whole room dropped into black. I felt it in my skin, the way you feel a door close in a room you can't see. My hand was still on the lever. I didn't pull it.

_Wait._

The sound of boots was soft and unhurried.

I heard them everywhere. On the catwalk above me, a slow tread on steel grating. In the stairwells, two sets. Behind me, at the door I'd come through, a heavy, flat _clang_ as something slid across it and locked.

_They closed the door._

A single work light came back on, one bulb in the center of the room, flooding the frames in white. It made the rest of the hall blacker. I stood at the edge of its glow with my back to the rack and my hand still on the handle, and men came out of the dark.

They didn't hurry. They came the way people come to a table. There were six of them.

I counted them, because I count. Two dropped from the catwalk and landed without a sound, five meters to my right. Two stepped out of the stairwell on my left. Two came around the frames from behind Voeren's, quiet and easy, with their hands loose at their sides. They were in the black gear I'd seen all morning, with gaiters and visors, and not one of them carried a rifle.

_No rifles._

That was the thing I understood last and felt first. They'd put them down. They'd left them on the catwalk, or in the corridor, or wherever men put rifles when they've decided not to need them. They had come to do this with their hands.

The one in front took off his gaiter.

He was a big man, bigger than any of the others, with a close-cropped skull and the pleasant, tired face of a man at the end of a long shift. A gray ring cut by a black bar was stitched on his shoulder. He folded the cloth and tucked it into his belt, and he smiled.

"Well," he said. "Look at that."

His voice was light, and dry, and amused. It was the voice of someone watching a dog do a trick.

"Sergeant Spade, I presume."

I didn't answer.

"We've been listening to you all morning." He tilted his head. "You've got a lovely voice when you're begging. _I'm right here, Voeren. I'm right here._" He mimicked it, high and cracked. A couple of the others laughed. "Quite touching. We thought you might actually come."

_Don't talk to him. Don't talk._

"Spade," said Voeren, behind me. Her voice was low and flat and terrified, and I hated it. "Spade, _go._ Go now. Don't—"

"Quiet," the big man said, almost kindly. "You'll get your turn."

I let go of the lever.

My mind was doing something very cold. It had gone clear and slow, like the surface of a lake before a storm. It listed things, one at a time.

_Six. All enhanced. One punch from any of them breaks my ribs. Two punches kills me._

_The door's locked behind me. The stairs are covered. The carbine is a noise machine, and it's loud, and they're close._

_The frames. The chains. The lights._

_The first step._

_I can't beat six. I can't beat two. But the first two don't know what I am yet._

I looked at them. They weren't looking at my hands. They weren't looking at my knives. They were looking at a sergeant in a comms uniform with a plain black rifle on a sling, and they'd already decided how it would end.

The nearest one, a lean man on my right with a long scar through his visor, cracked his knuckles.

I moved.

The first step took me five meters. The second took me two more. The scarred man's weight was still settling onto his lead foot when I came in under his guard, low, with the combat knife in my left hand and my own in my right. His arm started to swing. I didn't wait for it. I put the heavier blade into the soft hollow under his raised armpit, the place where the plates don't meet, and I drove it up with everything I had.

It went in to the hilt.

I felt the blade hit something that wasn't bone. I felt him stiffen. I felt, very distinctly, the artery under it give. He made a small, startled sound, and the swing went wide and caught nothing, and I let go of the knife and spun away.

_One._

He didn't fall at once. He stood there for a second with his arm still raised and his mouth open, the way a man stands when he's forgotten what to say. Then his knees went.

The second one was already coming.

He was shorter and broader, and he came in low with his shoulder down, the way you'd go through a door. I read the line of it in the same instant, the tilt of his head, the set of his weight, and I stepped to the side and let him go past. His shoulder missed me by a hand. I came around him, caught the edge of the nearest frame, and used it to spin. I slashed the back of his knee with my own knife, the thin one, and I felt the tendon part.

He screamed and went down on one leg.

I didn't give him time. I took his visor in my left hand, tilted his head back, and slid the point of the blade up under the rim and into his eye.

It was a sound I would remember.

_Two._

I stepped back. My heart was pounding. The scarred man had folded onto his side on the concrete, and the short man lay across him, twitching. I stood in the white circle with my own knife dripping and a rifle on my back that I hadn't touched, and for the length of one breath the hall was utterly silent.

"Spade!" Voeren's voice cracked across the room. "_Left!_"

I turned.

A fist went by my head so close I felt the wind of it.

I'd seen the third man move. I'd seen him from the corner of my eye, crossing the floor in three long, quiet strides. I'd thought he was ten meters off. He wasn't. He was _there_, and his arm had already come around, and only the half-second of her shout had saved my skull.

I went under it and rolled.

A boot came down where my head had been. A hand closed on the edge of my vest and ripped it, and the webbing parted with a snap. I threw myself away from the grip and hit the floor on my shoulder and came up with my own knife in my hand and nothing in the other.

_I'm out of one blade. I've got one._

The big man was laughing.

"Oh, that's _good,_" he said. He hadn't moved from where he stood. He was watching me the way you'd watch a bird in a box. "Oh, that's very good. Two down in ten seconds. Do you hear that, Staff Sergeant? That's your little comms sergeant."

"Don't you touch them," Voeren said. Her voice was shaking with rage. "Don't you lay one finger—"

"I wouldn't dream of it. I'll let the boys do that."

The four who were left began to spread out.

They were smart. They didn't rush. They moved to the edges of the circle, one to my left, one to my right, one behind, one in front, so that whichever way I turned, there was a man at my back. A box. The oldest trick in the world.

I breathed in.

My breath was ragged and hot. The hunger had come back, a hollow, burning ache under my ribs, and my hands were slick with blood that wasn't mine. I could feel the reserve running down like water out of a cracked tank. Every burst was a coin. I'd spent four.

_Think._

I looked at the room. The hanging light. The frames. A chain that dangled from the nearest crossbar, glinting. A man in front of me, a man behind, one on each side. And the drain, at the center of the floor, a dark round mouth in the concrete.

_You can't beat four. You can't get out. You can only make them pay for every step._

I shifted my weight to my left foot, and I felt the floor.

The knife-man was the one on my right.

I hadn't seen him until that moment. He'd come out of the stairwell last, a thin man in a black coat, and he'd drifted toward the edge of the circle without looking at me. He held a long, narrow blade low against his thigh, the kind used for opening things. He was smiling, a small, private smile.

_That's the one._

I didn't know why I knew it. I knew it the way I'd known the launcher was a launcher. I turned my head, very slightly, toward him, and I saw him see it.

His smile widened.

I took a step, and the world went to pieces.
