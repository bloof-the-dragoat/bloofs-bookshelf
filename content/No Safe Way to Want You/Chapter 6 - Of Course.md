
# Chapter 6: Of Course

I woke with my cheek against Voeren's shoulder and the immediate conviction that I had done something I would need to explain.

Then I remembered what it was.

The kisses. Her hand in mine. Me asking her to stay close, repeatedly, with increasingly little concern for subtlety.

I kept my eyes shut.

Under my cheek, she breathed in. Her arm rested along my back, warm through the borrowed shirt, and one of my hands had settled against her stomach sometime in the night. I could move it. I probably should move it. I didn't.

My mouth tasted awful. There was a dull pressure behind my eyes, and the light reaching through the curtains seemed unnecessarily bright even through my eyelids. When I shifted my leg, my knee ached. My ribs hurt less than I expected, which was welcome until I tried taking a deeper breath and found the limit.

"Morning," Voeren said.

I opened my eyes. She was looking down at me, her hair flattened on one side and standing up on the other.

"How long have you been awake?"

"Long enough to need that arm back."

I lifted my head immediately. "Shit. Sorry."

She laughed and flexed her fingers as I eased away. "Wasn't a complaint."

"You've lost circulation."

"Temporary setback."

I sat up slowly, using my forearm instead of my palm. The room stayed where it belonged, which was an improvement. So was the fact that my lower back let me straighten without the sharp catch from last night. Voeren rolled her shoulder and watched me settle against the pillows.

"How bad?"

"Which part?"

"Start at the top."

"Thirsty. Headache. Several regrettable decisions."

Her smile thinned a little.

I noticed too late. _Idiot._

"The tequila," I said. "I meant the tequila."

"Right."

For a moment neither of us moved. In daylight I could see the crease the pillow had left along her cheek. She was still wearing the faded shirt from last night. There was nothing strange about any of it except that I was in her bed and didn't know what happened after this.

_Maybe I should have gone home._

The thought made me look toward the doorway. My clothes were still in the living room. I could get them, apologize for taking up her morning, and leave before I managed to misunderstand something important.

Voeren sat up beside me. "Water's there," she said, nodding toward the bedside table.

I reached for the glass. There was a little left, warm now. I drank it anyway.

"You said tomorrow," she added.

I lowered the glass. "I did."

"Here it is."

"Feels poorly organized."

She gave a small laugh, but she waited.

I turned the empty glass between my hands. Last night I had imagined that waking up sober might supply whatever understanding I'd lacked. Instead, everything felt more exposed. I remembered wanting her. I remembered saying it. Now I had to sit beside her without the film or the tequila or the excuse of being half asleep.

"I don't have a better answer yet," I admitted.

Voeren looked down at the blanket. "Okay."

"But I'd like to see you again."

Her eyes came back to mine.

"Preferably while awake," I added. "For more of it."

"That can be arranged."

"Good."

She smiled then, properly, and I felt myself smile back. I still didn't know what I was offering beyond another day. I hoped she could hear that without hearing less than I meant.

My stomach made a noise.

Voeren glanced down. "Strong contribution."

"It's been waiting for an opening."

"Breakfast?"

"Yes. Please."

She moved the blanket aside and stood, stretching her back before she picked up both glasses. I watched her reach the doorway, then caught myself still watching when she turned.

"Toast all right?"

"Toast is an excellent idea."

"Finally. Someone who appreciates my cooking."

---

By the time I came out of the bathroom, Voeren had put bread on two plates and was searching a cupboard for something.

I was still in her shirt and sweats. I'd splashed water on my face and looked at my neck in the mirror for longer than I wanted to admit. The bruises were there in full daylight, above the loose collar. I'd tried turning my head both ways. It was easier than last night, but not comfortable. My hands looked better, too, though they still stung when I washed them.

Voeren set a jar on the counter and looked up. "All right?"

"I should get checked."

Her expression changed. "Something worse?"

"No. I don't think so." I pulled out a chair and sat. "I'm moving better, actually."

"But?"

I reached for the toast, then left it on the plate. "Marlowe told me not to take any more Recovery Aid without someone reviewing it."

Voeren rested her hands against the counter.

"And not to drink," I added.

"Yeah."

"For seventy-two hours."

She looked toward the living room. I followed her gaze to the bottle beside the couch.

"I knew about the drinking," she said. "The injector—"

"I should've said something before we used it."

Voeren looked back at me. Her mouth tightened, and for a moment I thought she was going to argue about whose fault it was. Instead she asked, "Military hospital?"

I shook my head. "Somewhere outside. I'd rather deal with the doctor before I deal with anyone else."

"All right. I'll drive."

"You don't have to spend your Saturday—"

"I can drive you, Spade."

She said it plainly, without a grin. I looked down at my plate.

"Okay. Thank you."

Voeren pushed the jar toward me. "Eat something while I get dressed."

I picked up the toast. She waited until I took a bite before leaving the kitchen.

---

My clothes were where I'd left them, folded on the chair beside the couch. In morning light, the cream shirt looked worse. I lifted one corner, saw the stiff brown patches, and put it back down.

Voeren came down the passage fastening her jeans. Her gaze dropped to the bundle.

"You can keep those on."

"I'll bring them back."

"Wasn't worried." She stopped working the fastening and smiled. "You know where I live."

I wanted to say something easy, something that didn't reveal how pleased I was by the suggestion that coming back could be ordinary.

"Your security system knows I'm a threat now."

"_Terrifying._ Needed help with a shirt."

"An isolated incident."

"Documented, though."

I laughed, carefully this time, and unfolded my trousers far enough to retrieve my phone and the things from my pockets. The discharge instructions had acquired another crease. I smoothed them against my leg, then gave up and kept them with the phone.

Voeren brought a bag for the clothes. I put the knife and its sheath inside, the stiff shirt folded over both. My belt went in last. I didn't want to wear any of it.

At the entryway I sat on the bench to put my boots on. Bending still pulled at my side, but I managed the laces myself. Voeren waited beside the door with her jacket over one arm and her keys in her hand. When I stood, she glanced at the clothes I was wearing.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I looked down. One trouser leg had fallen over the back of my boot.

"Don't."

Her grin widened. "Haven't said a word."

I bent just far enough to roll the cuff again, then took the bag before she could offer to carry it.

Outside, the morning air was cool against my face. Voeren locked the door and walked beside me to the car. I put the bag by my feet, eased into the seat, and drew the belt across my chest without catching the worst of the bruising. She got in and looked over before starting the car.

"Ready?"

"Not especially."

"For the drive."

"Oh. Yes."

Voeren laughed, and I let my head rest against the seat. For the moment, that was the only answer I needed.

---

The hospital had glass doors that opened before I reached them and a reception desk positioned far enough inside to make me reconsider the walk. Voeren slowed beside me. I kept going.

A woman behind the desk looked up from her display. "What brings you in?"

I had rehearsed an answer in the car. It had been concise, medically useful, and entirely free of anything that might make her ask how I'd managed to do all of this in one week.

"I was discharged yesterday," I said. "From the military hospital. Then I got hurt again."

The woman's gaze moved to my neck.

"And took something I probably shouldn't have."

Voeren stood quietly beside me. The woman set aside what she was doing. "All right. Let's get you assessed."

---

The nurse who brought us through asked questions while fitting a cuff around my arm. She waited for the reading before asking me to describe the injuries, and I discovered that starting with Monday made the explanation worse.

"Yesterday's injuries first," she said. "We'll go back."

So I told her about the bar. Not the entire evening. Not the comments or the argument or the exact moment I'd decided that leaving would feel worse than staying. I told her where I'd been hit, where I'd landed, and that someone had held me down by the neck.

"Did you lose consciousness?"

"I don't think so."

"Anything you can't remember?"

"Not from that part."

She looked up.

"I drank afterward," I said.

"How much?"

I glanced at Voeren, who was sitting in the chair beside mine with my clothes bag between her boots. "We didn't measure it," I admitted.

"Best estimate."

Between us, we gave her one. Hearing it described without the evening attached made it sound like something I would have advised someone else not to do. The nurse recorded it without changing expression.

"And what did you take?"

"A field Recovery Aid dose."

"When?"

"After the fight. Before we went to her house."

She asked about the earlier treatment. I handed over Marlowe's instructions, creased enough now that she had to flatten them against her desk to read them. Voeren shifted in her chair, and I heard the leather of her jacket move.

The nurse finished the page. "We'll have the doctor review this with you. For now, I need you to tell me if anything changes while you're waiting. Breathing, swallowing, dizziness, anything new."

"All right."

She handed the instructions back. I folded them along the same worn lines.

---

We waited in a row of chairs outside the assessment rooms. A child farther down the corridor was trying to persuade someone that a bandage would work better if it had a different animal on it. The adult beside him appeared willing to consider this.

Voeren leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "I can tell them about the injector," she said quietly.

"You didn't leave anything out."

"I mean when the doctor—"

"I'll tell them."

She looked at me. I let the paper rest on my thigh instead of folding it again.

"I'm not going to say I don't know how it happened."

"That wasn't what I meant."

"I know."

Her jaw moved once. Then she sat back. I looked at her hand on the armrest between us. There was a pale mark across one knuckle I hadn't noticed before. I put two fingers against the side of her hand, and Voeren turned it over. We didn't say anything else about the dose.

When my name was called, she stood with me. The staff member waiting by the doors glanced at her. "Are you accompanying them?"

"Yeah," Voeren said.

"I'm sorry. No visitors through the imaging wing. You can wait out here."

Voeren looked past her, then at me. "Just for the scan?"

"We'll let them know what happens next once they've been assessed."

It wasn't an answer to how long. I could see Voeren noticing that too.

I took my hand away from hers and reached for the clothes bag. "Leave that with me," she said. "You don't need to carry it around."

I hesitated, then let go of the handles. "My phone's with me."

"Good." Her thumb brushed my wrist before she lowered her hand. "Let me know."

"I will."

She stepped back to give me room. I followed the staff member through the doors, and at the turn in the corridor I looked behind me. Voeren was still standing by the chairs, holding the bag. I lifted my hand. She lifted hers.

Then the door swung shut.

---

The doctor waited until I'd finished explaining before looking at Marlowe's instructions. She read them, asked about the field dose again, and set the paper down.

"Did you understand these when they were given to you?"

"Yes."

"Was there a reason you couldn't seek care after the new injuries?"

I looked at the edge of the examination bed. The covering had a small crease under my leg, and I pressed it flat with one finger. "I didn't want to go back."

"To the military hospital?"

"Yes."

She gave me a moment.

"I thought they'd ask questions."

"I'm going to ask questions too."

"I know."

"Then let's make sure I have accurate answers."

There was no edge to it. Somehow that made me sit straighter.

She asked me to describe the pressure on my neck, the pain in my ribs, and what had changed since last night. I answered as clearly as I could. When I couldn't remember something exactly, she asked what I did remember instead. Then she examined me. I lifted the borrowed shirt when she asked and watched her expression rather than looking down. Her fingers paused near the bruising at my side.

"Here?"

"Yes."

"More than yesterday?"

"Less."

"And when you take a full breath?"

I demonstrated. Halfway through, the ache sharpened. "About there."

She nodded and let me lower the shirt.

By the time she'd checked my hands, back, and knee, the headache had settled into a persistent throb. I wanted water. I wanted to stop describing things that had happened to my body as if they belonged on a list.

The doctor pulled the stool closer. "We're going to do imaging and bloodwork. I also want to keep you here while we work through the results."

"How long?"

"I can't give you a reliable time yet."

I looked toward my phone on the chair. "Someone's waiting for me."

"You can let them know."

I picked it up, then paused. "I'm moving better than I was last night."

"I heard you."

"So that's good?"

"It's useful information," she said. "It isn't the only information I need."

I looked down at the screen without unlocking it.

She rested one hand on the edge of the desk. "The field treatment doesn't tell us what injuries you have. And because you've recently had a supervised course, I need to review what you've taken before anyone gives you more."

"I wasn't going to take another."

"Good. Keep it that way unless the clinician treating you tells you otherwise. And tell us if you feel worse while you're here. Don't wait because you think it'll make the visit longer."

That one was accurate enough to be irritating.

"Okay."

She stood. "Someone will come for you shortly."

After she left, I unlocked my phone.

> _They're doing scans and bloodwork. Could take a while. You don't have to wait._

I read it twice before sending it. Voeren replied while I was still holding the phone.

> _Need anything before I go?_

I looked at the empty chair.

> _No. Thank you._

A second message arrived.

> _I've got your stuff. Message when you know._

I typed _Okay_, then added _Thanks for bringing me._ Her reply was immediate.

> _Of course._

I put the phone facedown before I could find another reason to keep the conversation going.

---

The scan required me to hold still in a position my back disliked. The person operating it talked me through each adjustment, and I concentrated on following the instructions instead of counting how long it had been since I'd last moved. Bloodwork came afterward. Then another room, another explanation of which results we were waiting for, and a promise that someone would update me when there was something to say.

For a while, there wasn't.

I could hear a trolley in the corridor with one wheel that clicked on every turn. Someone outside was explaining a room number over the phone. The person in the next bay asked for another blanket. I shifted against my pillow and checked the time.

Voeren had sent nothing else. There was no reason she should have. I opened the conversation anyway.

> _Of course._

Two words. She probably hadn't thought about them after sending them. I'd thanked her for a lift, and she'd answered. That was all.

I turned the screen off.

A little later, someone brought water and told me which tests were still pending. I thanked them, drank, and tried to find something to watch on my phone that wouldn't require me to care about it. Nothing held my attention. I wasn't bored enough to want work or worried enough to call anyone. I was tired, sore, and sitting in a hospital again less than a day after being told I could leave the last one.

And I missed her.

I set the phone down beside my leg.

_You saw her this morning._

The thought didn't help. I could still picture her in the kitchen, searching for the jar while the toast cooled. I wanted to know what she'd gone home to do. Whether she'd eaten anything else. Whether she was pleased to have the house to herself again.

I nearly picked up the phone to ask. Instead I settled farther back against the pillow and closed my eyes. When footsteps stopped outside the curtain, I opened them too quickly.

It was someone coming to check my observations.

---

By the time the doctor returned, the light at the edge of the curtain had changed. I'd stopped checking the time.

She pulled the stool toward the bed and sat with her display resting against one knee. "We can talk about getting you home."

I straightened. "Good."

"After we go through a few things."

"Less good."

Her mouth moved a little. "Still necessary."

She explained what they'd checked and which symptoms I needed to watch for. I made myself listen instead of hearing _home_ and deciding the rest was administrative. When she asked me to turn my head again, I did. It still pulled along the bruising, but I could look over my shoulder without moving my whole body.

"Better than this morning?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And compared with last night?"

"Much better."

She glanced at the display, then at my hands. "Your response to treatment is quite strong."

"Marlowe said that."

"The doctor who discharged you?"

I nodded.

"I'd like their team to review today's results alongside the earlier ones."

My fingers stopped moving against the blanket. "Is something wrong?"

"There's a finding in your bloodwork I'd like followed up. It isn't something I can explain from one set of tests, particularly after the treatment you've had."

"What kind of finding?"

"A result outside the range I'd expect. By itself, it doesn't tell me why." She set the display down. "It may be related to the recent Recovery Aid. I don't want to give you an explanation I can't support."

I looked at the small mark where they'd taken blood.

"So you're not keeping me?"

"Not for that finding. But I am putting it in the follow-up instructions."

"All right."

She waited until I looked up again. "Your bruising improving quickly doesn't mean you can ignore how you feel. You're still tired. You're still in pain. You've had new injuries on top of the ones you were recovering from."

I nodded, more slowly.

"Rest," she said. "No alcohol, no more Recovery Aid unless the clinician treating you directs it, and arrange the review with your usual team."

"I have work Monday."

"What sort of work?"

"Communications support."

"Then make sure whoever is responsible for your medical restrictions has the updated information. Being discharged from here isn't clearance for unrestricted duty."

I thought about Stroud standing at the foot of my bed. "I understand."

She asked me to repeat the main instructions back. I did, including the part about returning if anything changed. This time I didn't think about how quickly I could put the paper away.

---

The nurse brought my discharge packet and waited while I checked that I had everything. Phone. Folded instructions from Marlowe. New instructions. No clothes bag. Voeren still had that.

I turned a page while the nurse explained where the follow-up information would appear. Near the bottom, beneath a block of smaller print, was a line about third-party diagnostic processing. I checked the patient name at the top and moved on to the number I was supposed to call.

"Any questions?"

I had several, but none she could answer. "No. Thank you."

She showed me the way back to the public corridor. I recognized the chairs before I reached them. Someone else occupied the one Voeren had used that morning, a man bent over a paper cup with both hands around it. I stepped out of the foot traffic and unlocked my phone.

---

There were more messages than I expected. The first was practical.

> _Any news?_

Then, later:

> _They still keeping you?_

I checked the time beside it. I'd been in the scan. The next message had arrived while I was asleep.

> _Been thinking about this morning. And last night._

I stopped scrolling.

> _I said a lot. Didn't mean to put you on the spot._

A few minutes after that:

> _Hope I didn't scare you off_

I stood beside the wall with the discharge packet tucked under one arm and read those words twice. The last message was almost an hour old.

> _Would you want to do something tomorrow?_

People moved past me toward the doors. I shifted farther out of the way. I'd spent the afternoon wondering whether she was relieved to have the house to herself. Meanwhile, she had been on the other side of the conversation, apparently wondering what I'd meant by leaving it quiet.

I typed too quickly, deleted most of it, and started again.

> _They're letting me go, mb, I fell asleep between tests_

Then:

> _Tomorrow would be nice_

I sent it before I could add anything that made it sound less certain. Her reply came while I was finding somewhere to sit.

> _Good_

Another message followed.

> _About tomorrow, yk, and you leaving the hospital_

I smiled down at the screen.

> _Strong order of priorities huh?_

> _Want a lift? I've still got your clothes_

I looked at the doors, then at the packet balanced on my knee. I was tired enough that arranging another way home felt like a separate appointment.

> _Yes, please. Home this time._

> _Send me the address_

---

Voeren pulled up outside while I was standing beneath the entrance canopy. The passenger window lowered.

"Escaping?"

"With paperwork."

"Very official."

I opened the door and found my clothes bag tucked into the footwell where I'd put it that morning. For a moment, seeing it there made the whole day seem longer. Voeren waited while I sat and fastened the belt.

"What did they say?"

"Rest. No drinking. No more injectors. Follow-up."

"All right."

"They were very clear."

"I believe you."

I settled the new packet on top of the bag. "There's something in the bloodwork they want Marlowe to look at. They didn't say it was urgent."

Voeren's hand paused near the controls. "What something?"

"They don't know yet."

She looked at me.

"That's what they said," I added. "They don't want to guess."

"Okay."

I appreciated that she didn't immediately supply a guess of her own.

She pulled away from the entrance. For a while, I watched the hospital recede in the side mirror.

"You didn't have to come back," I said.

"Had your trousers."

"A compelling obligation."

"Couldn't abandon them."

I laughed, then glanced over. She was watching the road, but the corner of her mouth had lifted.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Any objections to music?"

"As a concept?"

"As something we could go look at."

"No objections."

"Good. There's a shop I think you'd like."

"I'd like that."

She glanced at me briefly, then back at the road. We left it there, and I was glad we did. The plan was small enough that I could stop trying to work out what it meant and simply look forward to it.

---

At my building, Voeren parked and turned toward me.

"Need a hand getting up?"

"I can manage."

She nodded, then looked down as I reached for the bag. "That's yours."

"What?"

"The bag. You can keep it."

"Oh. I was about to begin an elaborate return process."

"Already got one going with the clothes."

I looked down at the shirt I was still wearing. "Could just take it from me now." The words escaped before I'd decided to say them.

"Definitely considering it."

Her gaze stayed on me, and for a second neither of us moved. I wanted to lean over and kiss her. I also wanted to do it without spending five minutes considering the angle, the seat belt, and whether she was about to say something.

I unfastened the belt first. Voeren noticed, and her eyes came back to my face.

"Can I?"

"Yeah," she said, softly.

I leaned across the space between us, one hand braced against the seat instead of reaching too far. The kiss was brief. When I sat back, her smile had changed.

"Tomorrow," I said.

"Yeah."

I got out before I could make saying goodbye into another reason to stay. At the entrance, I turned and lifted my hand, and she returned the gesture from behind the wheel.

Then I went inside.

---

My apartment smelled faintly of paper and the room I hadn't opened for several days.

I set the clothes bag just inside the door and switched on the lamp beside my desk. Warm light reached across the PC, the art supplies crowded along one edge, and a sketch I'd left propped against the wall because I hadn't decided whether it was finished. The guitar stood where I'd left it. A black frame leaned crookedly above the shelf. My notebook sat beside the keyboard with a pen caught between the pages.

Nothing had changed.

I'd thought about this room while I was in the military hospital. Not in any grand, homesick way. I'd wanted my own chair. My own light. The ability to put something down and know where it would be when I reached for it again. Now I was here, and for the first few minutes I didn't know what to do.

I took my boots off. That helped.

I put the knife, still sheathed, away with my other belongings and left the dirty clothes in the bag until I had the energy to deal with them. The two sets of discharge instructions went on the desk where I couldn't pretend I hadn't seen them. Then I found something simple to eat.

By the time I'd finished, the room felt more like mine again. I went to change, opened the drawer, and stood there with a clean shirt in my hand.

The collar of Voeren's shirt had slipped toward one shoulder. I tugged it back into place, then smoothed the hem. The clothes were comfortable. Changing could wait.

I put the clean shirt back before I could think about it any further, closed the drawer, and went to get my notebook.

---

The pen was marking the end of a conversation I'd abandoned days ago.

My two characters had reached an inn. There was a damaged bridge behind them, rain against the windows, and a woman who had spent three pages finding reasons not to let go of someone's hand. I'd planned the kiss days ago, before the hospital, the bar, or any reasonable possibility of kissing Voeren myself.

I read the last page, found my place, and shifted the notebook toward the edge of the desk so my palm wouldn't drag across the paper.

_He lifted his free hand to her cheek. She turned into it before he could lose his nerve._

I stopped.

Voeren had done that. Last night, when I'd touched her face, she'd leaned into my hand as if she had been waiting for it. I could remember the warmth of her cheek, and the small change in her expression when she realized I meant to kiss her.

I looked down at the notebook. "Different people," I muttered.

I loosened my grip on the pen and kept going.

_"You're making it difficult to say goodnight," she said._

_"I haven't asked you to."_

That was good. I liked that. I read it again, then immediately imagined saying it to Voeren, and my face warmed.

_You would not get through that sentence._

Probably not. She'd look at me, her mouth would start to curve, and I'd either explain the joke until it died or forget what language I'd begun in. I put the pen down and pressed the backs of my fingers against my cheeks.

The room was not hot.

After a moment, I picked the pen up again.

_He leaned closer, slowly enough that she could have drawn away. Instead, her fingers tightened around his._

I knew what should happen next. I'd known for days. Her hand would move to his collar, he would pause just short of her mouth, and she would be the one to close the distance.

Except now I was thinking about the car. I'd asked, and Voeren had said yes so softly. I remembered the way she'd watched me unfasten the seat belt, the brief warmth of her mouth, and how reluctantly I'd sat back afterward.

I realized I was staring at the page with the pen resting against the same word. A small blot of ink had formed beneath the tip.

"Shit."

I lifted it and reached for a scrap of paper to blot the excess. Then I sat back, careful of my ribs, and looked at the ceiling.

I wanted to kiss her again.

That wasn't a useful discovery. I'd been aware of it when I got out of the car. Apparently I needed to keep discovering it.

My phone lit beside the notebook.

> _Home all right?_

I picked it up too quickly.

> _Yup. Eaten and everything._

> _Showing off._

> _Following medical advice. Trying something new._

Her reply took a moment. I watched the screen instead of writing.

> _Good. Get some sleep._

I glanced at the unfinished kiss.

> _You too._

Then, before I could decide against it:

> _Looking forward to tomorrow._

The answer arrived while I was still holding the phone.

> _Me too, Spade._

I smiled. There was nobody there to see it, which should have made it less embarrassing. It didn't.

I put the phone facedown and pulled the notebook closer. My characters were still waiting a fraction of an inch apart. I'd left them there while I thought about someone else's mouth.

"All right. Sorry."

I read the last few lines to find the rhythm again. The woman had been the bolder one all the way through the story. She could cross a room to argue with him, take his hand in public, and tell him when he was being an idiot. Letting him see how much she wanted this was what frightened her.

I crossed out the sentence where she waited for him.

_She caught his collar between her fingers._

_"Are you going to make me ask?"_

_His smile faltered. "No."_

_"Good."_

_She kissed him before he found anything else to say._

I managed the next paragraph without thinking about Voeren. Most of it, anyway. Then I wrote the way the woman smiled against his mouth, remembered something very specific, and had to stop again. I rested my forehead against my knuckles, careful of my palm.

"This used to be easier."

But when I looked back at the page, I liked what I'd written. I kept going until my hand began to cramp.

There was still more I wanted to write, which was unusual enough that I considered ignoring it. Then I saw the discharge packet beneath my elbow.

"All right," I said.

I left the pen between the pages and closed the notebook. Before bed, I put water within reach, checked the time, and set my phone to charge. I rolled the cuffs of the borrowed sweats back above my heels, then settled beneath the covers. Voeren's shirt bunched under my side. I tugged it smooth and left it on.

The apartment was quiet in the way I normally liked.

I switched off the lamp.

For once, when I thought about the next day, I had somewhere I wanted to be.
