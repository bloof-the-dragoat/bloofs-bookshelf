
## Chapter 1 — Contact

> [!warning] Content note
> Graphic violence, strangulation, and character death.

*Shit. I’m gonna die.*

That’s the clean version. The one I can stand to start with. No reel of my life, no sudden opinion about God, no speech about what any of it meant. Just me in the corner of a flooded pump house, empty carbine across my knees, and the small polite fact that I had run out of places to go.

Water had found the seams in my boots. My left foot was numb. I shifted it, got pins and needles up the ankle, and stopped. No point spending the last minute of my life on a foot.

On the other side of the room, someone hit the door.

The bulkhead shuddered. The tool locker I’d wedged under the handle scraped backward through the shallow water and caught on the valve wheel.

Not a kick. Not a shoulder.

A fist.

I knew the sound by then. Flat, ugly _whump_, then steel complaining. The third hit had left a dent shaped like knuckles. The fourth had opened the door a finger’s width. This one cracked something in the frame.

Dust came down through the red emergency lamp and settled on the water.

_They’re not even in a hurry._

Shit. Of course they weren’t. One door. They had all night, and I had a rifle that was a very expensive stick.

The bolt was locked back on an empty magazine. Fourth one since the east gate. I checked it anyway, thumb on the follower, as if the ammunition might have come back while I was being pathetic.

Nothing.

Holster empty too.

_Fuck._

I’d thrown the pistol at a man’s face an hour ago. Not a plan. A plan would have been to keep the only weapon that still had a round in it. My hand had decided the argument was over and my arm had agreed, and the pistol had bounced off his jaw like I’d hit him with a moth. Great throw. *Fuckin historic*. I’d think about it at my funeral, which was apparently going to be a pump house.

That was the first time the night split me in two. The part of me that counts magazines had said _don’t_. The other part had already let go.

Knife behind my hip. One cracked plate. Field relay on its side with a hole through the casing, water licking the wires. My earpiece still hissed. I’d stopped hearing it as static a while ago. It was just there, dry and steady, under everything else. A dead net, still polite enough to make noise.

Twenty-six hours ago, I was Sergeant Ranelle Spade, Columbian Army Signals, available because the enhanced team’s actual comms specialist had a fever. A damn *fever*.

“You’re available,” the lieutenant had said.

I nodded. That’s the job. You nod, and then the job is your life.

I was angry even then. Not the kind of angry that gets you written up. The kind you can keep behind your teeth while you say _yes, sir_ and mean _I hate that "good at this" counts as volunteering_. I’d called it nothing for years. Nothing was safer. Nothing didn’t pick fights with lieutenants.

“You’ll sit in the back. Keep the relay up. It’s a milk run.”

Eleven minutes. The relay got eleven minutes.

I closed my eyes.

East gate. Rail cars. Wet gravel. Reyes had yelled, “Contact, contact,” and then was gone out of the channel like someone had cut the wire in him. Chen laughed once. Startled. The laugh changed before it finished, and I still don’t know what I heard. I am not going to play it back if I can help it.

Every channel died at the same instant. Not faded. Cut. I knew that sound the way other people know a door slam in their own house. Somebody had put a jammer on us and done it clean.

Then running. Hiding. Trying the net again because trying the net was the one thing I was good for. Different roof. Different wall. Same silence. My hands wanted a panel so badly I caught myself tracing imaginary controls on a brick wall. Stupid. Useful, if I’d had anything left to talk to.

Every enhanced soldier on my team was dead, or somewhere I couldn’t see, which in that yard was the same courtesy. The people hunting me were **not** dead. They’d followed me through three buildings. The only reason I was still breathing was that a man who could punch through a wall still had to guess *which* wall.

The door buckled.

Blue-white light opened along the edge. A work lamp swung in the corridor and dragged itself across the floor.

I pressed farther into the corner.

_Shit. SHIT. I didn’t even want to be here._

Not the pump house. Not the raid. Not the army. Not the rank somebody waived into my hands because I was good at a job I hated, and good was apparently a kind of consent.

Most mornings there wasn’t anything ahead of me I particularly wanted. Next task. Next shift. I’d called that indifference. It had felt like nothing, and nothing had seemed close enough to fine.

I had thought dying might not bother me.

My hands were shaking so hard the carbine rattled against a buckle.

_Of course._

A laugh caught behind my teeth. Stupid. Late. Mine.

_Of course I find out now._

My body wanted to live. It had not gotten the memo about how tired I was of the whole arrangement. My chest was doing that bright, animal thing, and all the polished nothing I’d practiced for years had nowhere to sit. I was furious at it. I was grateful to it. Both at once, which is a rotten way to spend your last minute.

Something hit the door.

Not a fist.

Heavy. Soft. Wet. The locker jumped. Another impact. Then a crack that came through the steel like it had been aimed at my teeth.

A man screamed.

The sound stopped halfway through a word.

I lifted my head.

Boots. A grunt. A fist going into a person and the person not liking it. Somebody shouted in a language I didn’t have, and somebody else answered, short, pleased.

Then laughter.

Loud. Rough at the edges. Delighted, which was a deranged choice of emotion for a corridor full of murder.

I knew that laugh.

I couldn’t place it. Not from there, knees up, fingers locked on an empty rifle, brain kind enough to hand me a laugh and not a name. But I knew it. I’d spent eight months pretending I didn’t.

The fighting stopped.

Water ticked off a pipe. One drop. Another.

The valve wheel jerked under the handle.

I flinched. The locker screamed across the floor. The bulkhead tore off its upper hinge. A hand curled around the edge and pulled, and the steel folded out with a shriek that went straight into my fillings.

She was almost too tall for the doorway.

Shoulders ate the corridor light. Black hair stuck to her forehead and the side of her neck. Blood on her jaw, her throat, the front of her vest. Some of it drying brown. Some of it still bright, still wet, still deciding whether it was finished.

One sleeve torn open over a long cut in her forearm. A scorched strip of plate carrier hanging loose on the left. Behind her shoulder, a rifle barrel bent at an angle rifle barrels do not survive.

Knife loose at her side.

*Staff Sergeant Aeirith Voeren*. It was really her. Here. As I was about to die.

Then she grinned.

Wide. Crooked. Like finding me alive in a foot of filthy water had genuinely improved her evening, and she was not going to pretend otherwise.

“HAH! Need help, Spade?”

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out. Excellent. Twenty-four years of practice speaking like a person, and the first time she said my name I turned into a broken handset.

_She’s wounded. She’s covered in blood. Those are the facts. Start with the facts._

My eyes did not start with the facts. They caught on her chest moving, the torn sleeve tight around her arm, the hand still on the ruined door. Blood, lamp-light, that grin. She looked like the raid had tried to kill her and she had found it funny.

_She’s… the most beautiful thing I’ve ever seen._

Nope. Not now. Wrong time. Wrong room. Wrong everything. The thought didn’t care. It had been waiting in stairwells and chow lines for eight months, and a woman folding a *steel door* in half was apparently the permission slip.

I knew her the way you know someone you’ve tried not to stare at. Which is to say: too well, and not at all.

Chow line. Arms room. A stairwell where I’d put my back to the wall so she could pass, because taking up space near Aeirith Voeren felt like a tactical error. My ears went hot if she laughed anywhere in the same building. I had a whole stupid system for not being in her line of sight, and it had worked until she tore my hiding place in half.

“’Scuse me,” she’d said once, and grinned, and I had remembered it for an unreasonable amount of time. Not the words. The fact that she’d bothered.

Her laugh carried in the mess. She usually ate alone. I had noticed that too, and then I had told myself enhanced soldiers preferred their own company, or each other’s. Cleaner than the other thought, which was that I wanted to sit there and didn’t have a single excuse that wouldn’t sound like I’d lost my mind.

People like her moved through a different army. I saw it from the edge. I fixed what they broke. I did not get looked at like, well, *this*.

She had never said my name before.

I hadn’t known she knew it. That landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there, hot and useless.

“Staff Sergeant.” My voice scraped. I was looking at her collarbone, not her face, because her face was a problem I was not cleared to work. “How—”

“Later.”

She stepped over the broken door and took my forearm. Firm. Then my fingers locked around her wrist on their own, and she adjusted. Not a flinch. A correction, like she’d felt the panic and decided not to make it worse.

_She felt that. Great. Fuck. Add it to the list of things I will be thinking about if I live._

She pulled me up.

My numb foot hit wrong. I caught myself. Pride, or what was left of it, did most of the work. I wanted to say something that sounded like a person and not a shorted cable. What came out was nothing, again. My face was probably doing that blank thing it does when the inside of my head is on fire. People mistake it for calm. I have never corrected them.

“You hurt?”

“No.” I looked down, as if the uniform might have a note. “Not yet.”

“Good place to be.” Her eyes went to the relay. “Comms?”

There. A question with an answer. My mouth remembered how to be a sergeant, and worse, it remembered how to be me.

“Gone. Jammer. Not a fade, a hard cut, all channels at once, so it’s not the relay dying on its own. Probably centered on the main depot. If I’m right about the null, it’s wiping everything from the east gate to the canal spur, maybe seven, eight hundred meters if the—”

She was still smiling.

I stopped. The rest of the radius died in my throat. I’d been about to draw her a coverage map with my hands. In a foot of pump-house water. While she bled. My ears went hot so fast I felt it in my scalp.

_Shut up. She asked if it was gone, not for a sermon._

I stared at her instead, which was not better. Blood on her teeth, and she was smiling at me like I’d handed her good news. “Why are you smiling?”

“Because you’re alive.” No hesitation. Like it was obvious, like my being alive was a fact worth stating out loud. Then her eyes cut to the corridor. “And because there’s more of them.”

I followed her look.

Three soldiers in gray. No patches. No flags. One folded against the wall at an angle that didn’t belong to a person. Another’s head had gone too far around. The third—

I looked at the floor.

My stomach pulled in on itself. Anger came up under the nausea, mean and private. These were the men who had been punching through steel to get at me, and part of me was glad they were meat, and part of me wanted to be sick about being glad. I kept my face where it was. Blank. Useful. A lie.

_Don’t do that. Don’t be grateful like that. You don’t know why she did it._

I didn’t know. I wanted it to be for me anyway. That was worse.

“Don’t look at the mess,” Voeren said. “Look at the gear. Take what’s useful.”

Right.

A task. I could do a task. Tasks were the only room in my head where the noise sat down.

I left the carbine by the dead relay and crouched by the nearest body. Fingers slipped on the holster. I wiped them on my trousers and tried again, because shaking was allowed and failing a simple motion was not.

Compact sidearm. Controls I knew. Chamber. Magazine.

Four rounds.

I seated it, chambered one, and put the pistol where mine should have been. My hands liked the weight. My head was already spending the rounds in theories I did not have time for.

“Four rounds.” My tongue felt thick. “That’s it.”

“Then don’t waste them.” She checked the corner, rolled one shoulder, and stopped halfway. Hurt. She didn’t offer me the information, and I didn’t ask. Asking would have meant looking at the cut again, and looking at the cut meant looking at her. “East-gate rally point’s a wash. Everybody’s dead or running. We’re going south.”

“Toward the canal?”

“Past it. Bird can’t land under the jam.” She glanced back. “How far did you say it reaches?”

She had been listening. While smiling. While bleeding. I filed that somewhere dangerous and immediately wanted to explain the whole propagation model, which was not what she had asked, and also not something I could say without sounding like I was showing off for a woman who had just folded a door.

“Seven, eight hundred meters, if the signal’s behaving. I won’t know until I try.” I scrubbed water and grit off my face so I had somewhere to put my eyes. “If we can get out from under it, I can— Is your radio working?”

“Took a round on the way in.” She tapped her chest rig. The handset shifted, screen black. “Eaten.”

I caught it before she could drop it. “I’ll hold onto this.”

The logical move was to leave dead weight. I knew that. My hand had already closed.

Her eyebrow went up. “You think you can fix it?”

“Maybe.” Cracked housing. Dead display. Not automatically a dead radio. The want hit me so hard it was almost relief: a broken thing, a shape I understood, screws and a board and the chance that the round had only killed the face of it. I turned it once, because my hands needed the problem more than they needed sense. “It’s all I have.”

_And if I put it down, I am only a person you have to drag._

She watched me shove it into a cargo pocket. I couldn’t tell if she was deciding I was useful or deciding I was funny. Both made my ears hot. I did not look up far enough to find out which.

“Stay on my left,” she said. “Watch the high ground. Anything you see that I don’t, you say it. You hear me?”

“Yes, Staff Sergeant.”

“Voeren. Just Voeren.”

My mouth tried the name and refused to spend it out loud. I nodded instead, which was cowardice with good posture.

She turned into the corridor.

I followed before my legs could hold a vote.

---

The Salt Yards used to make a city drinkable.

Now the pipes were wider than trucks, joints crusted white, rail spurs vanishing between warehouses. Gantry cranes leaned over the bays. Cables moved when the wind bothered them.

A corporate sign hung crooked over the service road.

COVERAGE IS A PRIVILEGE.

Somebody had painted out the second word. Funny. Maybe I’d think it was funny tomorrow, if tomorrow was a thing I got. The anger under the joke was older than the raid. Coverage, rank, a seat in the back of somebody else’s mission. Privilege, if you were the kind of person the yard had been built to serve. I was the kind of person who kept their signal up until it didn’t.

I took the yard in pieces. Yellow handrail. Oil on a puddle. A bootprint in wet salt. Looking at the whole place was how you missed the doorway with the rifle in it.

Brine. Diesel. Hot metal.

Something sweeter under it.

I didn’t think about that. I knew what sweet meant out here. My brain could shut up and keep the roofs.

Voeren moved in short bursts, nothing wasted. I stayed on her left and watched over her shoulder, which was a professional arrangement and also an excuse. She was huge even from behind. Blood had darkened the vest. Where it hadn’t, the work lamps caught sweat on her neck and the torn edge of the sleeve, and I had to make myself count catwalks instead of the line of her shoulder. I hated that the thought showed up while I was working. I hated that it was accurate.

A glint, high.

“High, eleven—”

Her knife left her hand.

The man jerked back before his weapon finished the trip to his shoulder.

She was already moving.

I went with her. Not because I was brave. Because she had told me left, and left was a direction, and directions were easier than the rest of it. The counting part of me wanted a sector sketch. The other part just wanted to stay where she had put me.

A shape between containers. I called it. She turned before I finished the sentence and took us through a gap, wall on one side, broken pumps on the other. She used my voice like it was part of her kit. I felt that in my chest and told it to wait its turn. It did not. It sat there, warm and humiliating, while I checked the next corner.

Loading bay.

Three men.

She met the first before he cleared the doorway.

His punch cracked into her ribs.

She laughed.

I felt it in my own stomach. Not fear. Not only fear. The sound was happy. Actually happy. Reyes was dead, Chen was dead, I had four rounds and a borrowed radio, and Aeirith Voeren sounded like she was having a better time than she’d had at breakfast. I didn’t know that. I only knew the laugh, and that it made me angry and dizzy in the same breath. Angry that anyone could like this. Dizzy that I couldn’t stop listening to it.

She caught the second man’s arm, turned under it, forced it back. His elbow went. She didn’t wait for his knees. She put him into the third and followed them in.

Nothing wild about it.

That was the problem. That was the thing I couldn’t stop watching.

Feet. Hips. How little she wasted, even when she hit someone hard enough to take him off the floor. I knew competence. I was competence, on a good day, with a panel in front of me. This was competence that looked like it liked itself. 

The first man came back.

She put him into the wall.

The other two folded near the doors. One tried to get an arm under himself. The other dragged him down. Neither stood.

Voeren turned. Blood on her teeth.

_She likes this._

I don’t know if it was fair. I know I believed it, standing there with a pistol I was afraid to waste, watching her breathe like the fight had fed her.

I hated violence. The army had not fixed that. It had only taught me to keep moving while I hated it. My face stayed quiet. Inside, I wanted the bay to stop being a bay. I also wanted her to turn around again. Those two wants did not speak to each other. They just occupied me.

Torn fabric shifted across her back. She looked so certain of her own body that something in my chest pulled tight enough to hurt. I was a person who had to think about where to put my hands. She inhabited hers.

I wanted to look away.

I wanted her to turn around again.

_What is wrong with me?_

Plenty. A list. None of it was useful, and none of it stopped.

“Spade! Left!”

I turned.

A fourth man, past the doors.

Pistol up. Then I saw the rifle. Long. Heavy. Bulky housing on the stock. Copper at the muzzle.

_Charged rifle._

Briefing room. Grainy picture. Somebody joking that if you saw one pointed at an enhanced operator, you should already be looking for cover, because the joke was the last comfortable thing you were going to get. The anger hit before the fear did. They had brought a weapon for her specifically, and I had walked her into the bay talking about left and high ground like that made me useful.

“Voeren, DOWN—”

Blue-white light split the bay.

The report hit me in the chest. Ozone crawled up the back of my nose.

She stopped mid-stride. One foot still up. Mouth open, like the laugh hadn’t gotten the news.

Then the shudder went through her, violent, wrong, and her knees folded.

She hit the concrete. The bent rifle clattered under the sling.

“No.”

I fired.

He jerked.

I fired again.

The counting voice said _two left, stop, confirm_. My finger had already spent the second round. He turned halfway and dropped facedown into the oil.

I kept the pistol on him.

He didn’t move.

_Two._

My ears were a high, thin room.

_Two left. You had four. You have two. Don't spend them on a corpse._

I was angry. It sat in my jaw hard enough to ache. I did not spend the rounds. That was the logical part winning a small, late argument.

“No, no, no—”

I was beside her before I remembered crossing the floor. My knee complained. I didn’t care. Caring was for later, if later existed, if she existed in it.

On her back. Staring up. Little tremors in her arms and legs. Blood from a burned opening under her left ribs, spreading past the edge of the armor. The smell of it cut through the ozone.

“Voeren.” I touched her shoulder. Too light. I didn’t know where I was allowed to touch a person who had just been shot with a weapon designed to put her down. Her name felt too big in my mouth and I said it anyway. “Voeren, can you hear me?”

Her eyes moved.

Slow.

They found my face.

Relief hit so hard I almost laughed. She was in there. Staff Sergeant Voeren, who ate alone, who had said my name like it was easy, was still in there looking at me. I don’t know what the look cost her. I only know she managed it, and that I would have stayed on that concrete for the look alone, which was not a tactical assessment. It was the other thing. The thing I didn’t have a word for yet, except _don’t go_.

Then her jaw locked. She tried to speak. The sound died in her throat.

“Voeren?!”

_She’s dying._

I didn’t know that. I knew the wound, the tremor, the blood finding the low places the way water had in the pump house. Two fingers on her neck. Pulse, fast, hard to keep under my hand because my hand was useless. Breathing shallow. The certain body from a minute ago had become weight.

_Do something._

_Do something! You are the comms sergeant. You are not allowed to just kneel here and feel this._

Boots beyond the doors.

I turned.

Two men. Gray gear. Side by side. Not hurrying. One of them said something, and the other laughed, and the laugh was ordinary, which made me want to put the last two rounds into the sound. I didn’t. My hand stayed on the pistol and my face stayed blank and the want went nowhere, which is where most of my anger lives until it doesn’t.

Maintenance hatch by the wall. Rusted grate. Crawlspace.

I would fit.

She would not.

_Go._

Clear. Immediate. The same voice that counted rounds and read a jammer’s radius. Logical. Cruel. Correct, if the only mission left was me.

_You can still go._

Her fingers twitched beside my knee.

No.

I didn’t decide it in a speech. I put the pistol away, got both hands in the collar of her armor, and pulled. The split was clean this time. _Hatch, now, alone, alive. GO._ But my body didn't listen: already committed, already stupid. There was no way I could leave her.

If I lived, I was going to have to admit I’d chosen her over the exit. If I died, at least I wouldn’t die as the person who left her on the floor after she tore a door off to get to me.

---

She barely moved.

I reset my feet and pulled again.

Armor creaked. Boots slipped. She traveled inches. Enhanced, even half-dead, was a different category of weight. My shoulders already knew they were going to hate me.

Footsteps behind us. Still coming. Still not rushing. Men who thought the dangerous one was down.

_Come on._

Tool crib against the wall. Mesh, shelves, door half open. Not cover. A suggestion of cover. Enough to ruin a sightline if they were arrogant, and they were arrogant.

I hauled. Pain set up under my shoulder blades and started charging rent.

Again.

Her boot heel scraped a seam in the concrete. A small, ugly victory. I took it.

One of them spoke. Closer.

I backed through the crib door and pulled until her shoulders cleared it. Got her behind the shelves. Lowered her head, because slamming Voeren’s skull into a parts bin felt like a line I could still refuse to cross. My arms shook the whole way down.

Her chest moved.

I watched it twice. Superstition. If I saw it twice, it would do it a third time.

Then I looked up.

_Think._

Cable. Chain. Pry bars. Boxes gone soft with damp. None of it was a weapon I could use before those boots arrived. My pistol had two rounds. Two rounds was a plan only if I enjoyed losing.

Gray panel by the door. Green light behind a dirty cover.

I stared at it.

FIRE ALARM.

_Wired._

The want hit before the plan did. A live panel. Labels. A system somebody had left alone because it still worked. My head went quiet in the way it only goes quiet for this, and under it was something almost happy, which I hated myself for with a clarity I did not have time to use.

The cover came off in my hand. Dust. Warm insulation. Old labels, the kind of system nobody upgrades because it still works and nobody important walks through this bay.

EAST WING.

LOAD BAY.

ADMIN.

Simple. Thank God for simple. I could have talked about that panel for an hour. Zone test, strobe circuit, how a dirty cover meant the bay still had power even if the army net was dead. Voeren was on the floor behind me, and my brain was lighting up like I’d been handed a gift. I let it. For three seconds I let it, because the excitement was the only tool I had that was faster than fear.

East-wing test.

A horn went off somewhere across the depot.

The footsteps stopped.

I held my breath like that would help the electronics.

A voice barked in the bay.

I killed the test, started it again, then brought the loading-bay strobes up. If they wanted a direction, I would give them three. My fingers knew the sequence. That part of me was not shy. That part of me never is.

White through the mesh.

Dark.

White.

Their shadows broke apart on the floor. One set of boots turned toward the horn. The other didn’t.

Of course. One professional in every pair.

“Okay,” I whispered, which was a lie I told my hands.

Back to her.

Eyes half closed. I touched her cheek. I hadn’t planned the touch. My hand went, and then I was in it, and pulling away would have been another kind of leaving. Her gaze shifted to me, slow. Still here. Still finding my face when I don’t know what that cost.

“I’m not going anywhere,” I said.

I hadn’t planned the sentence either. It came out anyway, too honest for a loading bay, too large for what I was. The logical voice tried to shrink it to _the next minute_. The sentence had already left. I meant the next minute. I also meant it worse than that, and this was not the time to audit myself.

“Just stay with me. I’ll be right back.”

Her fingers moved on the floor. Not a grip. I took it as an answer. I needed an answer, so I made one. If that’s a lie I told myself, it’s one I’m still willing to keep.

Pistol out.

The one who hadn’t left came toward the crib like his shoulders had never met a consequence. Broad face. Hair cut close. Old scar dragging one side of his mouth down. He glanced through the shelves and saw her.

_No you don’t._

I got behind the open door.

Waited. Counted his steps because counting was cleaner than praying.

He came through.

I fired close.

He staggered into the mesh, and the sound he made was almost surprise, like the evening had been going so well.

Then he turned.

His hand came out. Enhanced, or close enough that the difference didn’t matter to my bones. I didn’t know which. I knew the hand was faster than mine, and certainly far heavier.

I ducked it and fired the last round.

He dropped to his knees. One hand hit the floor. Blood ran through his fingers. He sagged sideways.

Didn’t rise.

_Nobody gets up from that._

I needed that to be a rule. I said it like a rule. My finger tightened on nothing.

_Empty. You knew it was empty. Say it again until you believe it._

Footsteps hammered back from the east wing. The horn had bought me a minute, not a miracle. A man shouted a name. Once. Again. His friend’s name, probably. I had just made him grieve, and the small mean part of me was glad. The rest of me did not have time to be decent about it.

The pistol was empty. I kept it anyway, and ran for the ladder.

Rungs slick. My boot skipped one, and I caught myself hard enough to wrench the shoulder that was already filing a complaint.

_Up._

Gantry. Four meters over the bay. A walkway built for one person and a bad idea. Dead lamps. Loose cable. Hoist cab on the overhead beam.

Under it, a cargo net. Steel rail plates. Heavy enough to matter.

I looked down.

The second man had reached the crib. He stopped by his friend. Saw the blood. Then he looked up, and looking up was the first smart thing either of them had done in my presence.

I flattened behind the hoist cab. Hand on the release. I pulled, testing. It resisted. Of course it resisted. Nothing in this yard wanted to help me except, apparently, Voeren, who was on the floor because I had seen the rifle a half-second late.

He shouted again.

I braced a boot on the cab and got both hands on the lever.

_Not yet. If you drop it on the crib you drop it on her. You are not that kind of stupid. Be the other kind._

He stepped away from the shelves.

Strobe on his face.

Dark.

He walked under the load like the bay owed him a straight line.

_Now._

I hauled.

The lever gave. Chain screamed. The plates hit, and the gantry kicked under my boots hard enough that I flinched behind the cab like the impact might come back up for me.

A scream came through the crash.

Then nothing.

I stayed crouched. Hands still locked around the lever. Breath in short, ugly pieces.

Rifleman.

The man in the crib.

This one.

_That’s all of them._

I wanted it to be true so badly the want felt like a plan. It wasn’t a plan. It was me, counting bodies I could see and calling the number mercy.

I looked toward the corner where she was hidden. Still hidden. I couldn’t see her chest from up there. I told myself she was breathing because I had told her to stay, which is not how bodies work. I knew that. I did it anyway.

_I can get her. We can—_

A ladder rung rang behind me.

I turned.

Another rung.

A hand came over the edge. Bloody fingers. Tight on the steel.

The man from the crib pulled himself into view.

For a second my brain refused the delivery. I had watched him go down. I had spent the last round, **my last round**, on him, and the rules were simple: shoot a man twice at that range and he stays where you put him.

The man from the crib pulled himself into view anyway, gray-faced, his vest soaked black. One hand was clamped to his neck. The other hauled him up the ladder rung by rung, and his breathing was wet and wrong.

His eyes found me.

*He's dead. I watched him die.*

I stepped back. The empty pistol was dead weight in my hand, and the anger came in under the fear, aimed at me first. I had felt clever up on that gantry while he was still alive enough to climb.

He got onto the platform, stood, and put his free hand to his belt. Steel slid out of a sheath.

I ran.

The catwalk shook under my boots. It ended ahead at a vertical support, and past it there was only air: no second ladder, no door. There was a beam below the edge and a cable hanging beside the upright. It was a problem, a bad option, and the hope that I was slightly faster than the person trying to close my throat.

He didn't hurry. He knew where the walkway ended, and he could afford slow. I had already spent my speed.

I reached the support and caught the cable.

*Don't fight the knife. Fight the place he's standing.*

I swung out around the upright and dropped onto the lower beam, outside the rail. The cable burned through my palm, and the cold of the steel came up through my glove.

His knife struck where my shoulder had been, and the whole catwalk rang with it. The miss was close enough that I felt it as a cold line.

I crouched on the beam, one hand on the cable and one on the steel. The floor was far below me, and jumping was just a quicker way to lose.

He leaned out over the rail, one arm stretched toward me, with the rail pinned against his hips and his weight tipped forward.

*Stay low. Make him come to you. Don't spend the last of your balance on a kick you can't finish.*

But his boots were planted on the grating, and the rail was the only thing holding his hips back. I pushed off.

I swung wide on the cable and around the upright. I got a foot on a pipe and put both boots into his back.

He lurched. One boot slipped, and for an instant his weight went past the rail. The instant was so clean I believed in it.

*Yes—*

His knife hit steel, and his free hand closed on my ankle.

My stomach went first, and then the rest of me.

My shoulder clipped a ladder. Metal flashed past my face. Something caught my vest and tore free, and there was no time to learn what I had lost. It might have been the radio, or the knife. Not knowing made me angrier than the fall, which was ridiculous and also true.

I hit the salt sacks hard enough to burst them. Dust filled my mouth and eyes, white and gritty.

Then the floor.

My left knee folded under me, and the bay went white. I tried to breathe, with my chest up and my throat open. Nothing came. My body kept trying anyway, as if repetition might shame my lungs into working.

The light flickered, then went dark, then white again.

A small, horrible sound came out of me. Then air came in at last, scraping, and it tasted of salt and blood.

Across the bay, a boot found the bottom rung.

He came down slowly, one hand clamped to his neck, and stopped to look at me lying in the spilled salt. He laughed, and it sounded like something tearing.

I got a hand under me, and the shoulder buckled. I tried the other arm, and my knee sent a bolt of white up my leg that made the attempt pointless. Move.

He crossed the floor without hurrying and caught the front of my vest. When he lifted me, the pain eased for one absurd second. Being off the concrete felt like mercy. I knew better, and I was grateful anyway.

He slammed me down. Something flared across my lower back, and my mouth opened on a sound that never made it out.

He knelt on my chest, and the air I had just won left again.

"Look at you," he said, wet and rough. "All that running."

He took his hand off his neck. Blood ran freely down his collar, and a drop from his wrist landed on my cheek, warm. "All that for a little tech rat."

I almost laughed. **Available**. **Sit in the back**. **Fix what they break**. I'd heard some version of it my whole life, and it had never come with a hand on my throat.

The hand closed. I got both of mine around his wrist and pulled. It was like pulling on a pipe. I braced my heel against the floor, and my knee screamed. His weight didn't negotiate.

He watched me work, interested. "I could crush it now," he said. "One squeeze."

I let go of his wrist and drove the heel of my hand into his face. His nose shifted. He blinked and smiled.

"But you made me chase you." The pressure doubled. "So I think I'll take my time."

*I'm gonna die.*

The edges of my vision were closing in. I had one try left, and it was the thing he'd shown me himself. I dropped my hand to his neck and shoved my thumb into the wound.

He made a noise I would have been proud of in better circumstances. His whole body jerked, and the grip on my throat loosened for half a breath. I got a thread of air.

Then he hit my arm aside and clamped down twice as hard.

"That," he said through his teeth, "was rude."

I kicked. My boot scraped concrete and went nowhere.

*I'm gonna fucking die.*

The bay flickered, and his face came back, then Voeren's. I couldn't turn far enough to see the crib or hear her over the pulse in my skull. She was ten meters and a set of shelves away, and she might already be gone. I had told her I wasn't going anywhere. The sentence was still in my mouth with no air under it.

*Voeren. Sorry.*

My fingers slipped off his wrist.

*I'm so weak.*

It came with total certainty. The alarm panel, the hoist, the four rounds, and the door she had torn open were all mine, and none of it mattered. I was a person under a knee, unenhanced, out of tricks.

I wasn't ready. I wasn't done. I hadn't even gotten the small life: a job that wasn't this, someone who stayed, an evening with a guitar and a bad story and no one timing how long I talked.

*Damn it.*

The edges of the bay went dark.

*Damn it all. I didn't even get to—*
