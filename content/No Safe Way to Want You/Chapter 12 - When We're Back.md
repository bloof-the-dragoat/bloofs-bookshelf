
# Chapter 12 - When We're Back

> [!warning] Content Note
> Graphic nightmare imagery, grief, and aftermath of violence.

The hall was filling up.

I didn't know where the blood was coming from. It rose out of the drain first, slow and dark, then out of the cracks in the concrete, then out of nowhere at all, lapping at the feet of the empty frames. Ankles. Shins. It was warm, and it had the thick, patient weight of something that had all the time in the world.

I looked down. My shirt was gone, and so was the skin under it. There was a hole in my chest the size of a fist, wet and round and perfectly clean at the edges, and through it I could see the far wall. Nothing moved in it. Nothing hurt. That was the worst part. It simply was, as ordinary as a window.

*Count.*

I opened my mouth. *One.* The blood reached my knees. *Two.* It reached my hips, and the number slid out from under me, and I started again. *One.* Every frame in the hall stood empty, cuffs hanging open, cross-bars swung wide. I turned in a slow circle, looking for the one I needed, and there were so many of them. There were more than there had been. They went back into the dark in rows.

"Spade."

It came from very far away. It was low and rough and utterly steady, and I couldn't tell which direction it came from.

I tried to answer. The blood was at my ribs, my chest, pouring in at the hole, and I felt it fill me from the inside, warm, then heavy, then so heavy that I couldn't remember how to lift my arms. I tried to breathe. There was nowhere for the air to go. Somewhere above me, a lens was clouding red.

"Spade—"

*One.*

I woke with my mouth open on a scream that hadn't come out.

The ceiling was white.

It wasn't the flat, shadowless white of the Doctor's lab. This one had a water stain in the corner shaped roughly like a boot, an acoustic tile set slightly crooked, and a smoke detector whose green light blinked every few seconds. I knew this ceiling. I'd learned it last week. A monitor beeped somewhere to my left, fast, then less fast, and I understood that it was my own pulse.

My hand went to my chest before I'd decided to move it.

Gown. Thin cotton, dry, printed with tiny gray diamonds. Under it, my sternum, my ribs, the flat of my stomach. I pressed. Skin. Muscle. A deep, dull ache, as though every bone I owned had been struck once with a hammer and put back, but nothing open. Nothing missing. I pressed harder, as though the hole might be hiding, and found only the thump of my heart against my palm.

*Whole.*

It didn't feel like good news. It felt like evidence.

The hunger arrived a second later, and it arrived like a fist. My stomach clenched so hard that I curled around it, and my mouth flooded with the taste of old coins. When I tried to push myself up, my arms shook. They felt like they belonged to someone twice as tired as me, someone who had spent their whole savings and was being asked for the rest.

There was a rust-colored crescent under the nail of my right forefinger. Someone had scrubbed everything else. They'd missed that. My left thumb was bare, and I stared at it for a moment before I understood why. Effects, inventoried, put in a bag. The ring was somewhere in a drawer with a cracked jaw and no one to touch it.

*Voeren.*

I didn't think it as a word. It came as a shape, and then a sound in the back of my throat that never made it out, and then the memory behind it: her arms, the weight of me going out of my body and into hers. *I've got you.* And before that, my own voice, thick and thin at once. *Nine-thirty.*

The clock over the door said 1547.

I looked at it for a long time without understanding what I was seeing. The last time I'd checked a clock, it had read 0924, and I'd been counting what I had left. *Six hours.* Then, with a lurch: *Nine-thirty.* I'd gotten the warning out. I hadn't heard her answer. I had no idea whether it had been enough.

The chair by the window was empty. Someone had pushed it neatly against the wall, the way furniture is left when nobody is expected to use it.

*If she were fine, she'd be here.*

It wasn't logic. It was only the fear, and I knew it, and it did exactly what fear does. I looked at the empty chair and felt the floor go.

The IV ran into the back of my left hand. I peeled the tape and slid the line out in one pull, and a bead of blood welled up and stopped before I'd finished watching it. The monitor shrieked as the leads came off my chest. Beside the bed, on a tray, a pair of gray grip socks sat folded where someone had left them. I got them on with hands that wouldn't stop shaking and stood up.

The room tilted. I held the rail until it settled.

*Doyle,* I thought, with a small, guilty twist. *Doyle too.* I would ask. I would ask about him as soon as I knew.

I went out the door.

---

The corridor was long, bright and smelled of floor cleaner and boiled something. The monitor in my room was still screaming behind me, and the sound followed me down the hall, thin and furious. I stood there for a second with my hand on the doorframe. I could hear everything. A cart rattling two floors away. A phone, a pager, a pair of shoes on stairs, each as distinct as a coin on a table.

None of them was hers.

A nurse at the station looked up. She had the tired, level face of someone who'd been standing since the previous night. Her eyes went to the line I'd left in my hand, to the grip socks, to my face.

"Sergeant—"

I'd rehearsed a whole sentence in the room. It had been complete, polite, in order. Every word of it was gone.

"Voeren."

It came out as a croak. My throat felt as though I'd swallowed gravel and screamed it back up.

"You pulled your line, you need to get back—"

"*Voeren.*"

Something in her face changed. It wasn't surprise. It was a decision.

"Staff Sergeant Voeren?" she said. "Enhanced care. East wing, follow the green line, past—Sergeant, wait—"

I was already moving.

I meant it as a walk, a long, fast one. The first step went wrong. It wasn't a step at all. Something in my legs lit, a flat spasm of heat, and the corridor jerked past me in a blur of ceiling and wall and floor. I'd meant to cover ten meters. I covered thirty, and the end of the hall rushed up to meet me with a turn I hadn't planned for.

*Too far.*

My grip sock hit the waxed floor and slid. I turned too late. My shoulder struck the wall hard enough that something in my chest rang, and a cart of folded linens went over with a crash that echoed down the corridor like a dropped tray. Someone yelled. An orderly flattened himself against the wall with his hands up. I stayed there on one knee with the world gone gray at the edges and my heart slamming in my ears, and I understood, slowly and exactly, how little I had left.

*The first step. Only the first. Never the second.*

It had been a rule in the annex. In the annex, I'd had a reserve. Now there was a hollow scrape where it should have been, and every part of me knew it. I breathed through my teeth. I got a hand on the wall and stood up.

"Sergeant!" The nurse was behind me now, not running, only following, her voice sharp and pitched to carry. "You are going to hurt yourself—"

"I know," I said.

I walked.

It was the slowest thing I'd ever done. I made my legs obey, one stride after another, no bursts, no heat, just the old plain trade of bone for floor. The green line ran along the baseboard, and I followed it. Past the rehab room, with its bars and mats. Past a pair of doors marked PHARMACY, and a window onto a courtyard I didn't look at. Every step, I counted, because counting was the only thing that held. *Forty. Forty-one.*

I thought about the strike. I made myself think about it, because not thinking about it was worse. A column of smoke where a building had been. A shape in it. I thought of the way she'd crossed the floor in three strides, and the way she'd held me, and I told myself it had been enough time. It had been enough. It had to be.

*Sixty.*

The corridor narrowed. The walls changed. The paint went from pale green to a heavy gray, and the doors grew thicker, hinged with plates that looked meant for something other than doors. A sign on the ceiling read ENHANCED CARE in blocky letters. Past it, a short hall ended in a double door with a small window, propped open on a rubber wedge.

I stopped.

A slate hung on the wall beside the nearest room, the kind that nurses wrote on in marker. Someone had printed a name on it in tidy block letters.

**VOEREN, A. — S/SGT**.

The door beneath it stood a hand's breadth open. I could hear a sound from inside, quiet and steady, and for a moment I couldn't tell what it was. Then I could. It was a monitor. Someone's heartbeat, even and slow.

I'd run all the way here and couldn't make myself push it.

I put my hand on the door.

The door swung inward under my hand without a sound.

The room was smaller than mine. It had the same gray walls and the same wipeable floor, but the bed frame was welded instead of bolted, and the rail along its side was steel as thick as my wrist. The blind was half drawn. What light came through lay across the blanket in pale bars.

Voeren was awake.

She sat propped against the raised head of the bed, a line in the back of her right hand and a monitor ticking beside her. Her face had gone every color a face can go. The swelling had come down since the hall. One eye had opened again, a slit ringed in purple, and the other was still nearly shut. Both wrists lay on the blanket wrapped in white gauze from knuckle to forearm, with a thin brown stain showing at the edge of one dressing where nobody had caught it. Her hair was flat on one side. She was looking at the door. She'd heard the cart go over. Of course she had. She'd have heard everything.

"Spade," she said.

It wasn't a question. Her voice was a ruin, rough as a rasp, and it was hers.

I'd planned to say something. I'd run the whole way, one slow step at a time, and I'd had a sentence for this. My mouth opened. Nothing came out but air, and then a small, cracked sound I'd never heard myself make, like a hinge giving way.

My knees went.

I didn't fall. I folded. The doorframe was at my shoulder, and I slid down it until the cold tile met my knees, and I knelt in the doorway with my hand clamped over my mouth. I'd expected relief to be gentle. It wasn't. It hit the way the punch had, all at once, everywhere, and I couldn't stop shaking.

"Hey." The rail dropped with a clank. "Hey. Spade. Look at me."

I couldn't. I looked at the floor. A line of tile swam and doubled.

I heard her swing her legs off the bed. The monitor changed its tune as she pulled the leads, and then I heard the small, wet sound of the line coming out of her hand. She did it with two fingers, without looking, and pressed her thumb over the spot. The alarm began to shriek. She ignored it. She stood, slower than she should have, and I saw her free hand find the rail and her jaw tighten around a flinch she didn't let reach her face. Her ribs. She crossed the room barefoot, five steps, and each one cost her something I could see.

"Don't," I said. It came out in pieces. "You shouldn't—"

"Yeah." She went down on her knees in front of me, right there in the doorway, and I heard her breath catch. "Probably."

In the hall, she'd said *come here,* and I hadn't been able to. This time she didn't ask. She came to me.

She put her arms around me, bandaged wrists and all, and pulled my head down against her shoulder. The gown smelled of antiseptic soap, and under it, faintly, of her. Her hand settled at the back of my head, not tight, just there. I felt it tremble. Her voice, when she spoke, was perfectly level, and her breathing was too steady, as though she were managing it one breath at a time.

"I'm here," she said. "I've got— I'm here."

"You were—" I tried. "Nine-thirty. The strike—"

"You told me." Her mouth was against my hair. "I heard you. We got clear."

That was all. She didn't say how. I didn't ask.

I was crying. I hadn't noticed when it started. It ran down my face and into the collar of her gown, and I couldn't make it stop, and she didn't tell me to. Footsteps came down the hall, quick and sharp, and a voice said, "Staff Sergeant—" and Voeren said, without lifting her head, "Give us a minute."

"You're both— that monitor—"

"Turn it off. Please."

There was a pause. Then a hand reached past us, and the alarm cut out, and the silence was so complete that I could hear the blind ticking against the window.

"Five minutes," the nurse said. She sounded as though she'd spent her whole life saying it. "Then I'm putting both of those lines back in somebody."

The door eased partway shut.

My hand came up on its own. It stopped an inch from her jaw, over the worst of the bruise, and hung there. She turned into it carefully, the way you put weight on a bad leg, until my palm was against her cheek. Warm. Real. Her pulse beat in her jaw, slow and strong, against the heel of my hand.

*One.*

I counted it. I couldn't help it. *Two. Three.* It kept going. It didn't slide out from under me, and it didn't start over. I got to forty before I understood that I'd stopped being afraid of what number came next.

---

I heard them before I saw them.

Two sets of footsteps, and they didn't belong to the nurse. One was even and unhurried, a measured heel-and-toe that I'd have known anywhere. The other came a half step behind it, quicker, with a small catch at the end of each stride, like a man who'd practiced walking evenly and hadn't quite gotten there. They were still at the far end of the hall. They were coming straight toward us.

"Voeren," I said.

She'd heard them too. Her arms were gone from around me before I'd finished her name, and her hands came to my upper arms, careful, and held me at a distance where we both could see the other's face. Her eyes went over me once, fast, the way a medic's do. She didn't say anything. She stood, a little too carefully, her free hand flat against the doorframe, and then she bent and took my elbow.

"Up," she said, very low. "Slow."

I let her. My legs came under me one at a time, and my knees held. The gown had ridden up at the hem, and I tugged it straight with a hand that wasn't quite steady. My face was wet. I scrubbed it with the heel of my palm and didn't get all of it.

She didn't let go of my elbow until the door opened.

Major Ito came in first, tablet pressed to his chest, and stopped a pace inside the room. He looked tired in a way I hadn't seen before. The paper-colored face had gone gray around the edges, and there was a smear of something on his cuff that might have been ink. Colonel Stroud came in behind him and stood at the foot of the bed with his hands clasped at the small of his back. He looked exactly as he always did.

"Sergeant Spade," Stroud said. "Staff Sergeant. I'm told neither of you is where you're supposed to be."

"Sir," said Voeren.

"Sir," I said.

"Sit down, both of you, before you fall down." Ito's voice was quiet. He gestured at the bed, and Voeren eased herself onto the edge of it with her jaw set, and I took the visitor's chair someone had left by the window. I sat. I didn't realize how badly I'd needed to until I had. Voeren turned her head toward me and stayed that way.

Ito looked at his tablet and didn't read it.

"Corporal Doyle came out of surgery at one-fifteen," he said. "He's alive and he's stable. The surgeon is cautious about the word. He lost a great deal of blood, and there's damage to the lung that will take time. He hasn't woken yet. I'm told that's expected."

I breathed out. It came out as a sound.

"The strike landed at 0930," Ito went on. He was looking at a spot a little above my head. "On schedule. The annex is gone. The building, the yard, the perimeter wall. A crater, and a good deal of debris." He swallowed. "Staff Sergeant Voeren left the footprint at 0927 in an enemy vehicle with two men aboard. She had radioed on the Sergeant's set at 0926 to say all three of you were alive and moving. I took that call. I passed it up."

"And the strike proceeded," said Voeren, without turning.

She said it quietly. She wasn't looking at the Colonel. She was looking at me.

"The time on target belonged to the Directorate," Stroud said. "I relayed what I had. It was not mine to withdraw."

Nobody answered that. Ito's mouth tightened, and then relaxed, and he went on as though there'd been no pause.

"I had a helicopter on the pad by 0915, on the chance it would be wanted. I sent it to the rail bridge when I heard the call. The pilot described the margin to me. I'll spare you." A very small, dry breath. "He didn't think either of you would make it. He said the vehicle came over the bridge with the blast wave on its tail."

I thought of the truck I'd driven into the Salt Yards, and of a gray rectangle of tag receiver on a dashboard. I thought of the way the radio had wavered. I didn't know what to say. I didn't say it.

"Lieutenant Hale and Sergeant Okafor," Ito said, and then he stopped.

It was brief. He lowered the tablet an inch.

"They were dead when your team reached the annex, Staff Sergeant. You confirmed it in your statement. The strike took the building, and there is nothing left to bring home."

"I know," said Voeren.

"And Sergeant Haskins."

The room went very still.

"The corridor was inside the footprint. We have nothing," Ito said. He said it gently, the way you would say it to a child, and I understood that he was holding himself very upright. "I'm sorry. I should say it plainly. He is gone. We have the recording through 0606. That's all."

I'd seen it. I'd sat at a console and watched it on a monitor the size of a book. I'd seen the helmet roll, and the camera finish its turn toward the ceiling, and a gray rectangle go to snow. I hadn't known I was still carrying it. It arrived all at once, whole, and every part of it was in my hands.

"He asked me if I was being quiet on purpose," I said.

It came out small and strange. I didn't know I was going to say it.

"On the net. Around the fifth check-in. He said, is it always this quiet, or is the Sergeant doing it on purpose." My voice cracked in the middle of the sentence and kept going. "I said it was called radio discipline. He said it sounded boring. He was— he was twenty-two. He was just a kid with a mouth on him, and I—"

I couldn't finish it. My throat closed over the rest. I put my hand over my eyes and sat there in the chair with my shoulders shaking and didn't care who saw.

Nobody told me to stop.

A hand settled on my knee. It was bandaged, and warm, and it didn't squeeze or pat or try to say anything. It just stayed. I knew without looking whose it was. Through the gap in my fingers I saw Voeren's face turned toward me, the swollen eye and the open one, and she wasn't looking at Ito or Stroud or the floor. She was looking at me, steadily, as though everything in the room except me had gone out.

"Casualty notifications are in preparation," Stroud said.

I lowered my hand.

He hadn't moved. He stood with his hands behind him and his face smooth, and he was speaking in the voice he used for roll calls. "Lieutenant Hale and Sergeant Okafor will be listed as killed in action, remains not recovered. Sergeant Haskins likewise. The recordings have been secured. A review of the operation will follow."

There was a pause. I looked at him through wet eyes, and for a moment I thought I saw something move behind his face. Then it passed. Maybe it hadn't been there.

"Sergeant Spade."

"Sir."

"You left your post without authority. You removed a carbine, a utility vehicle, and three magazines from section stores. You disobeyed a direct order in front of two military police, and you did it knowing exactly what I'd said." He didn't raise his voice. "The truck is a loss. I'd be within my rights to ask for a reduction in grade and confinement to quarters pending review."

"Sir—" Ito began.

"I'm not finished, Major."

"Sir, with respect." Ito had straightened. The tablet was against his chest again, and his knuckles had gone white around the edge of it. "I'd ask you to look at what the Sergeant did. Two soldiers from a six-man element are alive in this building because they went in. A hundred and forty people in a garrison are not going to be told an annex was abandoned with living men in it, because it wasn't. The record will show that. I'll write it myself."

Stroud's eyes moved to him. It was a long look. I couldn't read it.

"Two weeks," he said at last, to the air between us. "Forfeiture of pay. It goes in the log."

"Understood, sir," I said.

"Don't mistake it for leniency, Sergeant."

"I don't, sir."

"You'd do it again." He didn't make it a question.

"Yes, sir."

I hadn't planned to say it. It was just the truest thing I had. Voeren's thumb moved once across my knee, very slightly, and stopped.

Stroud looked at me a moment longer. Then his gaze flicked to a point somewhere over my shoulder, and I saw him make a decision.

"Medical has questions about your chart," he said. "The surgeon reviewed your imaging this morning. They'll keep. I'd like them to wait until you're on your feet and have eaten something." A pause. "Not today."

"Sir."

"Major."

"Sir."

He turned and walked out. The sound of his heels went away down the hall, even and unhurried, and I listened to it until I couldn't.

Ito let out a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below his ribs. He pulled a clipboard out from under his arm, uncapped a pen, and began to write.

"I've signed you both out," he said, without looking up. "The surgeon objected. I overruled her. You're both off duty until you're cleared, which won't be before Thursday. Medical will see you both Thursday morning." He tore a sheet free and put it on the bed beside Voeren. "Your locker bag is by the nurses' station, Sergeant. I had it brought up. I'd get dressed if I were you."

"Sir." My voice was a ruin. "Thank you."

He stopped writing.

"For the hall, too," I said. "Last time. And for— just for—"

"Sergeant." Ito looked at me, and I saw how young he was under the tablet and the careful face. "I didn't do anything. I wrote down what happened." He capped the pen. His mouth moved, very slightly. "Eat something."

He went out. The door eased shut behind him.

The room was quiet. Outside the blind, the light had begun to go amber.

Voeren hadn't taken her hand off my knee.

---

I got my ring back at the nurses' station.

It came in a clear plastic bag with my piercings and a folded slip for my signature. No knife. I'd looked, and I'd known before I looked, and I still felt the small flat absence behind my right hip. I had put it down on the concrete with both hands, carefully, as though it might break. The concrete was a crater now. I signed the slip and said nothing. I slid the ring over my left thumb, and the crack in its jaw caught on my knuckle the way it always did, and when I looked up, Voeren was watching me do it.

She didn't say anything. I felt it all the way down my spine.

We left by the east doors at 1812. I knew the time because I'd read the clock over the exit twice, and I still couldn't have told anyone what it said. The air outside was cold and smelled of exhaust and wet gravel. The sky had gone amber at the edges and the garrison lamps were coming on, one at a time, as if somebody were checking them. Voeren's car sat in the lot where it must have sat for two days. She had the key in her hand before I'd found the door handle.

At the gate, the guard read Ito's sheet twice, looked at the two of us in our borrowed gray, and raised the barrier without a word.

Voeren drove.

She drove the way she did everything, with her whole body, even in sweatpants and a hospital hoodie with her ribs complaining. Both bandaged hands sat at ten and two, and her thumbs rode the wheel in small steady adjustments. She didn't turn on the radio. She didn't ask for directions. She'd learned the way on Saturday and had it already. The road ran between low gray buildings and then out past a stretch of scrub, and the light turned slowly from amber to rust.

I leaned my head against the glass and tried to think.

*Debrief.* It was the sensible thing, so I started there. I'd watched the knife-man too long. That was the first error, the half second that let the room close. I'd overshot into the chain because I'd been taught the burst and never taught the brake. The reserve had been a fraction of what I'd thought, and I'd spent it at the wrong end of the fight. I could build a whole list. I'd built lists my entire life, and they'd always held.

*She's fine.*

It arrived in the middle of the third item and knocked the rest of the list out of my head. I started over. *The chain. The reserve. What I'd do differently at the—*

Her wrists. The edge of the gauze where it met her knuckles, white against the black of the steering wheel.

*She's fine.*

I tried again. I tried the hall, because I owed it a proper accounting: the first man, the second, the brute, the leader. I'd done what I'd done. I would have to look at it. I knew I would have to look at it, in the dark, in a bed I didn't want to be in. I could feel it waiting behind a door in the back of my head, and I knew exactly how much weight was leaning on the other side.

A shadow crossed the windshield, an overpass, and for one second the cab went dark and the smell of the hall came up out of nowhere, hot and copper and thick. My stomach turned. I held my breath.

Then the light came back. Her profile was in it, the curve of her jaw and the bruise along it fading toward yellow, and the whole thing slid sideways out of my hands.

*She's here.*

It was almost embarrassing. I'd always been the kind of person who finished a thought. I didn't lose them. I laid them down in order, like tools, and checked each joint before I trusted it. It had never occurred to me that there was something that could cut through all of it, clean and without effort, and that it would be sitting in the driver's seat humming under her breath.

She wasn't humming. I'd only thought she was. I'd been listening to her breathe for four minutes.

At a red light, she looked over.

"You're staring."

"Sorry."

"Don't be."

The light stayed red a long time. Neither of us said anything else. She took one hand off the wheel, and I thought she'd adjust the heater. She put it over mine on the console instead. Her palm was warm through the gauze. Her thumb moved once across my knuckles and found the ring, and stopped there, tracing the edge of the cracked jaw, and then it moved on.

*All I can think about is you,* I thought. It wasn't a confession. It was an observation. It was exactly as simple as that.

The light turned green. She didn't move her hand.

---

She stopped in the doorway.

I'd had the key in the lock before I'd noticed that she wasn't behind me. I turned. She stood on the threshold with her hand on the frame, the light from the landing behind her, and she looked like she had the last time. She looked like someone waiting to be asked.

"You can come in," I said.

She stepped in.

The place was exactly as I'd left it at 0330 the morning before. The desk lamp, the dark monitor, the guitar on its stand by the window with the strings gone slack. My sketches were pinned along the far wall, and on the shelf above them, the candles I'd never lit, and the skull I'd bought for a dollar and didn't have a good reason for. The notebook sat closed on the desk. I looked at it. I looked at her looking at it. Neither of us said anything.

"Hungry?" I said.

It was the first full sentence I'd managed in an hour. It came out like a laugh.

I ate standing at the counter. I'd been ravenous for eight hours, and now that there was food in front of me, the hunger came up from somewhere beneath my ribs and didn't ask politely. Three eggs, fast. Half a loaf of bread. Leftover rice from a container I hadn't thought to check. I didn't taste any of it. Voeren sat on the stool across the counter with her chin on one fist and watched me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Do they not feed you in there?"

"Apparently not enough."

She laughed. It was short and quiet and it hurt her, I saw it catch in her ribs, and she did it anyway. She ate a little herself, a few mouthfuls of rice with a spoon held in her bandaged fingers. Then she stopped. She put the spoon down. She looked at her hands.

"Spade," she said. "I wanted to ask you—"

Her voice caught. It wasn't a break, just a hitch, a small crack in something that had been very carefully held together. She was looking at the gauze on her wrists, and I understood, with perfect clarity, that she'd been working toward this sentence since the hospital. She didn't know if she was allowed. She didn't know if after today she had the right.

I put down my fork.

"Hold that thought," I said.

I walked out of the kitchen. I didn't explain. I walked past the couch and the desk and the guitar and into the bathroom, and I shut the door and stood against it with my heart going.

It hung on the hook behind the door where I'd left it on Sunday night.

Black. Soft with age. A band's name across the chest, half cracked from washing. It was the shirt she'd given me on Saturday when I'd needed something to sleep in. It was enormous on me. I had slept in it again on Sunday, alone, after she'd gone home. I would deny this under oath.

I looked at it for a long moment.

*Don't.*

I took off the gray hospital hoodie. I put on the shirt.

It fell to the middle of my thighs. The sleeves came to my elbows and left the black ink of my right arm showing from the wrist up to where the cotton began. The collar hung off one shoulder. It smelled of her, faintly, still, under the smell of my own soap. I caught my reflection in the mirror and stopped.

I looked small in it. I looked like someone who'd been dressed by a person twice their size and sent to open the door.

I looked, God help me, adorable.

*No.* My ears had gone hot. *No, take it off, put the hoodie on, you're being—*

Behind my eyes, for one half second, there was a hall. A sheet of red across concrete. I looked away from the mirror before it finished forming.

I took a breath. I let it out. *It's a shirt. It's only a shirt.*

I opened the door.

She'd turned on the stool. She sat with one hand on the counter and her whole body angled toward the hall, and when I came out, she went absolutely still. It happened in stages. Her good eye widened. Her mouth opened a fraction and closed again. She looked at the collar, and the sleeves, and the hem, and the bare length of my legs below it, and I watched her gaze drop once and come back up as though she'd had to haul it.

"Oh," she said.

"I—" My voice cracked. I made it behave. "It's yours."

"I know it's mine."

"I've— had it a while."

"Two days."

"Two days." I shut my mouth. My face was burning. I could feel it down to my collarbone. "That's a long time."

The corner of her mouth moved. It came up slowly, the way it had in the hospital, the way it had in my doorway at dawn on Sunday, and I knew that I was in an enormous amount of trouble.

She slid off the stool. She came across the room, six steps, not fast. The gauze on her wrists was very white against the black of the doorframe as she passed it. She stopped in front of me, close enough that I could feel her warmth through the cotton, and she didn't touch me.

"You were going to ask me something," I said. My voice had dropped. I hadn't told it to.

"I was."

"You can."

"To stay?" she said.

"Yeah."

She didn't say it. She didn't say *I can do that.* She didn't have to. It was in her face, and it was enough.

I pushed through the next part before I could stop myself.

"Are you going to make me take it off?"

It came out barely louder than a breath. It was the stupidest thing I'd ever said, and I'd meant every word of it. I felt the heat go from my ears to my throat. Voeren's eyes went dark, and the smile slid off her face, replaced by something much more serious, and her breath came out in a slow, uneven line.

"Is that what you want?"

"I want—" I stopped. I started again. "I've wanted it for two days. I said I didn't want it rushed. I still don't. But there's nobody waiting. No briefing. No four a.m. I've got the whole evening, and I'd like to spend it with you."

Voeren made a sound.

It wasn't a word. It was small and strangled and came from somewhere high in her throat, and she turned her head away fast, as though the wall had said something. Color climbed her neck under the bruising. She pressed the back of one gauzed wrist against her mouth. For a second I thought she was laughing. Then I wasn't sure.

"Sorry," she said into the gauze. "Hold on. Give me a second."

I gave her a second. My heart was going like a dropped drum.

She took one long breath through her nose. When she turned back, she'd put her face in order, mostly. One corner of her mouth still wouldn't sit straight.

"Let me make sure I have this right." Her voice was rough and trying very hard to be dry. "You disobeyed a direct order. You took a truck off the board. You walked into an enemy-held annex alone, and you almost got yourself killed, and you did it—" she waved one bandaged hand at the whole fact of me, the shirt and all— "for one more evening?"

The refrigerator hummed in the silence.

It *was* insane. It had been insane when I did it, and I'd known that, and I'd done it anyway. But nobody had said it out loud in that voice, level and astonished and a little bit awed, as though she were reading me a list of my own crimes. Heat went up my neck. I couldn't hold her eyes.

"That's not why," I said.

"No?"

"It wasn't a trade. Nobody owes anybody anything." I looked at the floor. "I just didn't want you to be dead. Everything else was—" I stopped. I didn't know the end of that sentence.

She crossed the room.

It was six steps, and she took them slowly, and I watched her come. She stopped close enough that I could feel the warmth of her through the cotton. She lifted one hand. It stopped an inch from my cheek, and hung there, and I leaned into it before I'd decided to. The gauze was rough and warm. Her thumb came to rest at the corner of my jaw.

She drew me in, gently, until her forehead rested against mine.

A moan left me before I knew it was coming. It was small, low, suppressed, and entirely unintentional, a breath with a voice caught in it, but it was a moan all the same, and it escaped into the inch of air between us. I went rigid.

Voeren went still. Then I felt it more than saw it, the slow curl of her smile, close enough that I could feel the shape of it against my skin. Her thumb moved once along my jaw. She didn't mention it. She didn't have to. She was visibly, thoroughly pleased.

"You are such an idiot," she said.

It came out warm and a little breathless, with something satisfied underneath. It didn't sound like an insult. It sounded like something she'd been holding for hours and had finally been allowed to set down, and she was enjoying putting it down.

My face was on fire. I looked away. I looked at the buttons of her hoodie, the hollow of her throat, anywhere else. "I know."

"Don't do it again."

"I can't promise that."

"I know." Her breath moved against my mouth. "I wouldn't believe you if you did."

I almost laughed. It caught somewhere in my chest and came out as a shaky exhale, and she felt it, and her thumb moved once along my jaw.

"I'm not here because you came for me," she said. "I want you to know that. This isn't me paying you back."

"I know." I made myself look at her. "I'd hate that."

"Good." Her eye was dark and steady and very close. "Because I'd be standing here either way."

I believed her. It was a strange thing to feel, how completely I did.

"Your ribs," I said.

"I'll tell you if anything hurts."

"You'd better."

"You too."

"I will."

"Okay."

"Okay."

I don't know how long we stood there. A minute, maybe. Her forehead stayed against mine, and I breathed the same air she did, and the light through the window went slowly from rust to dusk. Under my hands, I could feel her pulse, slow and strong, in the wrist she'd braced against my shoulder.

Neither of us moved.

Then both of us did.
