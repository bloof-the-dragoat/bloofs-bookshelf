
# Chapter 8: Dead Air

> [!warning] Content Note  
> Military violence and loss, panic and emotional distress, institutional dehumanization, and discriminatory remarks.

By 0345, I had been at the console for fifteen minutes. The check on my badge only required five.

The operations room at the forward support location was a converted freight office with a long table down the middle and one window painted over from the inside. Someone had hung a wall display where the shipping board used to be, and the Salt Yards sat on it in gray relief: the canal, the rail spur, the old pump station, and the bridge. At the far north end, an amber box marked the containment annex. It was a smaller footprint than I'd expected. It looked like something you could cover with your hand.

I had my radios lined up in order. Kestrel's net, the squad frequencies, and a backup band nobody had asked me to set up. Beside them were three small monitors, dark for now, each labeled in my own handwriting on a strip of tape: ANVIL 2, ANVIL 6, ANVIL 7. The helmets were new issue, the first lot with cameras built into the brow, and they had arrived on the supply run too late for the Salt Yards operation. Stroud had signed them out to the recovery element the moment they cleared inventory. The video would ride the secondary net, which was the only reason I'd had room to give it.

Beside the monitors sat the repeater list, five lines long, every one ticked in pen, because I'd packed the units myself and checked each battery twice. The fifth tick was new. I had added a repeater on the rail bridge, since it was the only structure out there tall enough to give me a clean line down the canal.

I knew exactly why I had picked it. I tried not to think about that either.

Marlowe's reply had come in at 0312. _Receipt confirmed. Light duty means light duty. Sit down whenever the console lets you._ I had read it three times and still couldn't decide whether it was reassuring.

_Rested and fit for duty._ I was neither. I had slept four hours in the shape of a question mark.

"Sergeant." Specialist Pruitt set a paper cup beside my elbow. He ran the telemetry desk and had the quiet competence of someone who'd learned early that the loudest person in a room is rarely the one who knows the answer. "Real coffee. Don't tell Major Ito."

"I'm not telling anyone anything."

"Good policy."

Major Ito sat at the head of the table with a tablet and a face like an unopened letter. Behind Pruitt, a civilian in a gray jacket was crouched at the back of the telemetry rack with a handheld diagnostic, tracing a cable. I'd registered him the way you register a coat stand, as part of the room and not worth a second look, and I turned back to the board.

The team arrived at 0350.

Haskins came first, already half-geared, with his rifle slung and his helmet hanging off two fingers. Doyle followed a step behind, adjusting the collar of his armor. They were both enhanced, and they took up the room the way enhanced people do, without trying, as if the furniture had been placed a little too close together. Haskins dropped his helmet on the table and looked at the wall display.

"Ah," he said, to Doyle, at a volume that wasn't quite meant for anyone else. "So we're heading in with the battle ape, huh."

I kept my eyes on my radios.

Voeren was standing in the doorway.

I hadn't heard her arrive. She wore full kit, the black armor sitting across her shoulders as if it had been cut for her, her rifle racked against one hip and her hair pushed back under a cap. She'd heard it. I knew she'd heard it, because her expression didn't change at all, and that was how I could tell.

"Morning, gentlemen," she said, evenly.

She crossed to the table and set her helmet down beside his. A small matte lens sat in the brow of each. She started working through her kit without looking at either of them: sling, magazines, a quick tug on each strap. She did it slowly and deliberately, the way you'd check a parachute. The silence stretched until Doyle shifted his weight.

Then he looked past her and found me.

"At least the small one's hanging back," he said, with a grin that tried to be friendly. "Wouldn't want you turned into a fine mist, Sergeant."

I opened my mouth to say something dry about my mist budget.

Voeren turned her head.

She didn't speak. She didn't move anything else. She just looked at him, and I watched the grin come apart a piece at a time. Doyle lasted maybe two seconds. Then he found something interesting to adjust on his sleeve, and Haskins very carefully examined the wall display.

I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding.

Voeren went back to her straps as though nothing had happened. She caught my eye across the table, one fraction of a second, and the corner of her mouth moved. It was gone before it could be called a smile.

"Radio check, Echo Three-One," she said, in her work voice, thumbing the handset on her vest.

"Reading you fine, Anvil Two."

"Likewise."

That was all. I looked down and found my pen was doing something unnecessary to the repeater list.

---

Colonel Stroud arrived at 0358 and spoke the way other people checked their watches.

"Anvil Two, you'll lead. Haskins, Doyle, you're with her." He didn't sit. "Objective is the north annex of the Salt Yards complex. Two recovery targets. Lieutenant Hale, Anvil One. Sergeant Okafor, Anvil Four. Both enhanced, both missing since the original operation. Their medical-status tags have been transmitting intermittently since last Monday. As of 0340, both show stable and stationary inside the annex."

He tapped the board. The amber box brightened, and two small markers appeared inside it, a short distance apart.

"Insertion by ground, outside the old jam radius. You'll approach along the rail spur from the south. Expect resistance. Assume it's the same people as last time."

*Same people.* In a week of debriefs and hospital corridors, nobody had given me a name for them. They were *hostiles*, *the opposition*, *the other side*, as if a name would have made them easier to remember.

"Anvil Two knows the ground." He said it with no emphasis at all. "Kestrel Control will manage the operation. Sergeant Spade is your communications. Sergeant?"

I stood. My ribs reminded me why I'd been told to sit.

"Five repeaters along the route, sir. Spacing is about two hundred meters, with the last on the rail bridge. Voice on the primary net, check-ins every ten minutes. Helmet cameras stream on the secondary. If you lose me, fall back to Band Two, which is voice only, and stay on it. I'll be there."

"Cameras," Haskins said, and tilted his helmet toward him on the table. "We're really wearing these?"

"New issue," Stroud said. "Cameras go live when the helmets go on. Everything on the secondary is recorded at this end. Command would like to see what the last element couldn't."

Nobody said anything to that. I looked at the small dark lens in the brow of Voeren's helmet and tried not to think about what it would mean to watch.

"And if you lose us?" Haskins asked, after a moment.

"Then I'll be calling you on Band Two."

That got a short laugh from Pruitt, and nobody else.

"Questions," Stroud said. It was not an invitation.

Nobody asked whether three was enough. That conversation had happened already, in a hospital room, and been lost. I looked at Voeren and found her looking at the board with the stillness of someone filing something away.

"Sergeant Spade's duty restrictions have been accounted for," Stroud added, to the room in general. "Carry on."

"Sir," I said.

I sat. I did not argue. There was nothing to argue with.

The briefing ended at 0415. Haskins and Doyle went out first, loud about nothing, their helmets under their arms. Major Ito followed, then Pruitt, who took his tablet with him and left the telemetry rack humming. The civilian had finished with his cable. I noticed that only because he was the last one in the room besides us, gathering his handheld with his back to the table.

Voeren stopped beside my console.

She was still in full kit, helmet in one hand, and she stood at what the regulations would have called a correct distance. Someone walking past would have seen a staff sergeant waiting for a final comms check.

"Anything else I need from you, Sergeant?"

"Channel's yours, Staff Sergeant." I kept my voice level. "I'll be on the other end the whole time. Come back safe."

"Counting on you to make sure I do."

She hadn't moved. Neither had I. For half a second, longer than the sentence needed, her gaze held mine, and I remembered the weight of her forehead against my own, her hand at my elbow, and her voice in my apartment saying _I can do that._

Neither of us said any of it.

She nodded once. Then she put her helmet on, and the middle monitor flickered and came up gray.

It was my own console, seen from a standing height. My headset, my hands, a woman at a desk who looked smaller than I'd have guessed, with a face she was working hard to keep neutral. The picture held on me a second longer than a camera check required.

"Echo Three-One, camera check," Voeren said.

"I've got you, Anvil Two. Picture's clear."

"Good."

The view swung to the door. Her boots went out into the corridor, and the image shrank down the hall until it was a gray rectangle with a bright one at the end.

I looked at the board. The two small markers inside the annex were steady. I watched them for a moment, then reached for my headset.

---

The team rolled out at 0430.

By 0455, I had them on the primary net. Voeren's voice came through clean and flat, the way it always did when she was working, with the faintest trace of amusement she wouldn't have admitted to.

"Kestrel, Anvil Two. Insertion complete. Moving to the first waypoint."

"Anvil Two, Echo Three-One. Copy. You're reading five by five."

"Anvil Two copies."

The repeaters came up one at a time. I watched each light turn green on my board, and each one felt like a small, private victory. _Repeater One, green. Repeater Two, green._ The three monitors filled in with it: gray, grainy, nine frames a second whenever someone turned their head. Voeren's showed the road ahead and the backs of Haskins's and Doyle's armor, small and jerking in the lower corners. Every ten minutes, the check-in came through on schedule, in the same calm voice, and I ticked it off.

"Echo Three-One, Anvil Six," Haskins said, around the fifth check-in. "Question. Is it always this quiet, or is the Sergeant doing it on purpose?"

"Anvil Six, it's called radio discipline."

"Sounds boring."

"It's what keeps you alive, Anvil Six."

A pause. Then Doyle, from further back: "Hey, he's got a mouth."

"Anvil Seven, Anvil Two," Voeren said. It was the voice she used for nothing. "Eyes forward, mouths shut."

They shut. On the monitors, both helmet views snapped straight ahead, and I almost smiled and caught myself.

By 0520, the team was moving through the southern approach. The old rail spur ran beside the canal, and the first sections of the complex came up in the grain of Voeren's view: broken fencing, a dead gantry crane, the long rectangle of the pump station. I knew it. I'd walked it before, though not in this direction, and not on my own feet.

I checked the tags. Anvil One and Anvil Four sat in the amber box, steady and stationary. I pulled up the numbers beside them. Anvil One's heart rate read sixty-two. It had read sixty-two when I checked at 0430. And at 0500. I frowned and scrolled back through the log. Sixty-two, sixty-two, sixty-two, across more than an hour.

"Specialist Pruitt."

"Sergeant."

"Anvil One's heart rate hasn't moved in an hour."

Pruitt leaned over his own screen. "Tag's on compressed telemetry. It sends averages, not raw. Probably in low-power mode, since they've been transmitting for a week."

"Or he's sedated."

"Or that." He shrugged. "Either way, steady's good, Sergeant."

It was a reasonable answer. I accepted it. I added a note at the bottom of my log anyway: _AN1 HR flat, 60+ min._ I didn't know why.

At 0538, Voeren's voice came through.

"Kestrel, Anvil Two. Approaching the eight-hundred-meter mark."

I sat forward. I'd been waiting for this one.

The first time, the jammer had wiped every channel in that complex within seconds. It had cut my relay, silenced Voeren's radio, and left me alone in a flooded room with an empty carbine. They hadn't worn a patch, a flag, or a number. Nothing to say who was shooting at us. I knew exactly what the edge of that jamming felt like. I held my breath and watched the middle monitor for the tearing, the stutter, the picture folding into snow.

"Anvil Two, Echo Three-One. Say your status."

"Mark. Crossing." A pause, and the soft sound of her breathing. "Nothing, Spade."

I realized I'd stopped moving.

"Say again?"

"I've got nothing. Net's clean. No jamming, no interference. Kestrel, are you seeing this?"

The picture hadn't so much as flickered. I looked at it, a gray road under a gray sky, and could not find a thing wrong with it.

"We have you five by five, Anvil Two," said Stroud, from behind my shoulder. I hadn't heard him come to stand there. "Excellent. Proceed."

"Sir," I said. I kept my voice careful. "The jammer was active here last time. It's not now. That's either a failure or a withdrawal."

"Or they didn't expect us." Stroud's voice stayed even. "It's good news, Sergeant. Don't talk yourself out of it."

"Sir, with respect—"

"They've moved on, or their equipment failed. Either way, it works for us." He turned to the room. "Textbook so far."

Major Ito looked at the board, then at the colonel, then at nothing in particular. Pruitt kept his eyes on his tablet. I turned back to my console and told myself that a good day was a good day.

My hands didn't believe it.

At 0549, the rail bridge came up on my relay, and then on the middle monitor.

"Kestrel, Anvil Two. Crossing the rail bridge." Voeren's voice was perfectly professional, and it still went quiet in the middle of the word _bridge_. The view showed two rails running ahead over gray water, and I knew the exact length of that structure. I knew how it had felt, under someone else's boots, over the same canal. "Bridge is clear. Still holds."

"Copy, Anvil Two," I said. My voice sounded all right. "Repeater Five is on the far side. You'll have a clean line down the canal."

"Appreciated, Echo."

_Echo._ Not _Spade._ I let the word stay where she'd left it.

The next ten minutes passed in a blur of green lights and crisp check-ins. At 0557, the picture turned off the canal and came up toward the north end of the complex, where a low concrete building stood against the gray sky. I had seen it a hundred times that morning as an amber rectangle with two steady dots. In the frame it was only concrete, stained and ordinary and much too close.

"Kestrel, Anvil Two. Annex in sight. Approaching the south door."

"Copy, Anvil Two."

A pause. Then, in a different voice, lower, and no longer quite working:

"Kestrel, Anvil Two. Door's open."

It was. I could see it. A dark rectangle in the face of the building, the steel door standing back against the wall as if somebody had just walked through it. I looked at the two heartbeats on my board, each as even as a metronome.

"Say again, Anvil Two."

"Door's open. Unlocked. No guard. No markings, either." The faintest sound, a boot on concrete. "Echo, tell me this is a good sign."

_It's too good._ I didn't say it. I didn't have the right words, only a knot in my stomach and a note at the bottom of my log.

"Anvil Two," I said, "proceed with caution."

"Always do." There was a thread of her usual humor in it, thin and a little strained. "Anvil Two, entering the annex."

The doorway came up in the frame and swallowed it. The picture went black, then gray, then slowly resolved as the sensor caught up with the dark. I sat very still, one hand flat against the console, and listened to the sound of her breathing change as she crossed the threshold.

---
The picture finished resolving into a long concrete corridor.

Emergency strips ran along the base of both walls, a weak amber that turned everything the color of old tea. The lenses struggled with it. Every time someone turned their head, the image breathed, brightening and dimming and sliding back into focus. Haskins's monitor showed the left wall and the back of Voeren's armor. Doyle's showed the way they'd come, a gray rectangle of daylight getting smaller behind them.

"Clear left," Haskins said, barely above a whisper.

"Quiet, Six," Voeren said.

I leaned toward the middle monitor. At the far end of the corridor, a pressure door stood closed under a single working light. The tags were somewhere past it. I could see the number on my board, fifty-one meters, and the two small amber dots as steady as a held breath.

"Kestrel, Anvil Two," Voeren murmured. "Moving to the inner door."

"Copy, Anvil Two," I said.

A boom came through the audio.

It was the flat, final sound of something very heavy meeting its frame. On Doyle's monitor, the rectangle of daylight was gone. A smooth steel face filled the picture where the door had been, with no handle and no seam on the inside.

"Door," Doyle said. His voice cracked on it. "Staff Sergeant, the door's shut, they shut the door—"

"I see it." Voeren had stopped. "Kestrel, Anvil Two. South door's closed behind us. Say again, south door is—"

The strips went out.

All three monitors dropped into the dark and flickered up again in the helmets' low-light mode, a grainy white-green world the color of bad ice. Haskins's breathing filled the line. Somewhere up in the dark, metal creaked.

"Contact," Voeren said, very quietly. "Up. Left—"

The first crack went through my headset like a nail through the bone behind my ear.

I knew that sound. I'd heard it once before, on a loading dock, and it had put her on the floor. On the monitors the whole corridor flashed white, and in the white I saw them: a line of shapes on a catwalk above the hall, shoulders squared, long barrels leveled. Then the dark came back, and an afterimage of ozone and static burned across the screen.

"Ozone!" Voeren shouted. "Charged rifles, above, five, six—"

Haskins's feed jerked.

He'd been hit. I knew it before I understood it, from the way the picture locked at an angle and began to tremble. The ceiling grating hung in the frame, shaking in time with a sound I couldn't place until I realized it was his teeth. His rifle clattered. His breath came out of him in short, wrenched bursts, as though something had a hand around his ribs and was squeezing.

"Anvil Two, Echo Three-One," I said. "Anvil Two, say again, I—"

**Nothing**.

"Kestrel, Anvil Two," she was saying. "Kestrel, do you copy? Kestrel, I'm not getting you—"

_She can't hear me._ It landed like a stone. Her voice was clean in my headset. I was on the net. I could see the repeater lights. And she was shouting into a dead line, because something in that building had stuffed her receivers with noise.

"Local jamming," Pruitt said. "It's on their end, Sergeant."

"I _know_."

Something dropped into Voeren's frame.

It came off the catwalk, forty feet or more, and landed on its feet without a sound. A man. Gray gaiter, black visor, a long coat of plates over his chest. He didn't stagger. He crossed the gap to Haskins in what felt like one step.

"Staff Sergeant," Haskins said, thin, wet and young. "Voeren. I can't feel my—"

The man took Haskins's helmet in both hands.

I watched his thumbs find the seam of the visor. I watched the plastic whiten, then web. There was a pop, a small, ugly sound, and then both thumbs went in.

On the left monitor, Haskins's own picture went white, then red. It filled with dark that was thick and quick and strung with something pale. A sound came through the audio that I didn't know a person could make. The fighter's hands turned. There was a long, wet crunch, like a boot going through a rotten melon, and the red on the lens bulged and slid.

He dropped what he was holding.

The helmet hit the floor and rolled, and Haskins's camera finished its turn by staring up at the ceiling, at the strips and the steel and the long dark ceiling vents, until the picture dissolved into gray snow.

I couldn't breathe.

My hand had come up over my mouth, though I didn't remember lifting it. My eyes were so wide they hurt. Behind me, someone said _Jesus_ quietly, and someone else made a small noise and turned away.

_Haskins._ It was the only thought I had. _He was just standing in the room. He was standing in the room an hour ago, being an idiot about a battle ape—_

On Voeren's feed, the picture lurched forward as she charged him.

She hit the fighter at full speed and drove him back into a steel rail with a crack I felt in my teeth. He was enhanced, and he hit her back hard enough that her frame rolled sideways. She didn't stop. I watched her drag him by the plates and put his head through the rail, once, twice, three times, until his helmet buckled and the movement under it went wrong. His shoulder came into the center of her picture as he folded. A patch was stitched there, pale gray against black: a ring cut by a vertical bar.

"Ostrand," Stroud said.

He'd said it flat, like a roll call, from somewhere behind my chair.

"The Combine," he added. "Same outfit as the Salt Yards."

It took me a moment to understand what I'd heard. "They didn't wear it last time," I said.

"No."

It was all he said. I turned my head, and the Colonel was standing with his hands behind his back, watching the screens with the face of a man reading a schedule. Next to him, Major Ito had gone the color of paper.

On the right monitor, Doyle was firing.

His rifle made short, ragged bursts in the dark, and the muzzle flash lit the walls in white pulses. _Where's the, where's the—_ he was saying, in a voice that had no mouth-guard left in it. A crack. His picture lurched hard to the left and fell. The camera ended up on the floor, looking at concrete and the boots coming toward it. A scream. A second crack, very close.

"Voeren," Doyle said, to no one. "Staff Sergeant. Staff—"

A blow hit the helmet. The picture spun, filled with white, and went to snow.

I wasn't breathing right. I could hear it, a quick tight pull through my nose that did nothing. My hands were on the console, and I realized I'd been typing, one frequency after another, because my brain had grabbed the only tool it knew.

_Band Two._ I switched. "Anvil Two, Echo Three-One, Band Two, Band Two, do you read—" Nothing. The hiss on that band was flat and endless.

_Burst._ I keyed the burst packet, the one I'd built for a day like this. The light blinked green. Acknowledged by the repeater. Not by her.

_Repeater Five._ I flipped to the bridge repeater, then to the standby. They were green. Every one of them. Every light on my board was green. Everything I had built that week was working perfectly, and I was listening to her through it.

"Anvil Two," I said, and my voice had begun to shake. "_Anvil Two._ Please. Come back, I'm— Voeren, I'm here, I'm _right here_—"

"Sergeant," Stroud said.

"Sir, they can't hear me, I can't—"

"Noted. Continue."

I didn't know what _continue_ meant. I turned back to the middle monitor.

She was still fighting.

I couldn't have said how. The corridor ahead of her had become a sea of green-white shapes, and she moved through it the way she'd moved through the Salt Yards, with her whole body behind every strike. I watched her break an arm backward over a knee. I watched her take a man's rifle and use it as a club until the stock came off in splinters. Two went down and didn't rise. A third scrambled away dragging a leg.

Then the charged rifles spoke again, together.

The first slug took her in the left thigh. Her picture dipped and snapped back. The second hit her shoulder, and I saw her arm lock straight, her gloved fingers spread and trembling against a wall of light. A third cracked into her armor, and she went down on one knee with a sound I'd only heard from the people I'd pulled out of the dark.

"Kestrel," she said. The word came out between her teeth. Her camera shook with a tremor she couldn't stop. "Kestrel, Anvil Two. Trap. It's a trap. Six is down, Seven's— I can't—"

_Please. Please say it. Say something I can use._

"I can't hear you," she said. "I don't know if you can hear me."

"I can," I said. I knew she couldn't hear it. "I can, I'm here, I can hear you."

"Spade—"

A boot came into the bottom of the frame.

It was black and heavy, set down with no hurry at all. Then another beside it. The picture tilted as she tried to lift her head and failed. A gloved hand rose into the center of the screen, wide and open, reaching for the lens as if it wanted to touch something it had been told was fragile.

The picture went black.

The audio went with it. No static, no hiss, only a carrier tone, steady and empty, the sound a radio makes when nobody is on it.

I stared at the middle monitor.

I stared at the left. At the right. Three gray rectangles, and nothing in any of them.

On the board, the two small amber dots inside the annex still beat, sixty-two and sixty-two, as steady as a metronome. Under them, Voeren's marker flickered. It dipped to a thin unsteady line and held there, barely.

The channel stayed open.

There was nothing on it. 

**Nothing.**

---
For several seconds, nobody in the room moved.

The three monitors showed gray static, the flat, patient kind a screen makes when nothing is feeding it. The wall display showed the amber box and its two steady dots. Somebody's chair creaked. Somebody exhaled.

"Anvil Two," I said. My voice came out hoarse. "Anvil Two, Echo Three-One."

Hiss.

"Anvil Six. Anvil Seven. _Anyone._"

Hiss.

I keyed the channel again and again, one hand on the console, as if the problem were the button. When I looked up, the Colonel had hardly shifted. He had one hand behind his back and the other at his side, and he was looking at the wall display with the face of a man checking a train timetable.

"Time," he said.

"0606, sir," said Pruitt. His voice wasn't quite steady.

"Log it."

"Sir," I said. I'd stood up without noticing. My ribs screamed and I ignored them. "Sir, we need to do something."

"We are doing something, Sergeant. We are logging."

"She's alive. Her tag's still up. They're alive, two of them could still be—" I couldn't make the sentence hold. "What's the second element? What have we got? Anyone. Anything. I can have a relay up on the north side in ten minutes if—"

"There is no second element."

"There has to be."

"The Directorate released three enhanced personnel for this operation, and I used them." He said it in the same even tone. "The nearest available team is three hours out. Eight, if I want them staffed and briefed. Neither is useful."

"Then send what we have."

"What we have is a communications section and a telemetry desk."

The calm of it went into me like cold water.

"**Sir**," I said. "WE HAVE TO DO SOMETHING. THEY'RE GONNA DIE OUT THERE."

The room went still.

It was louder than I'd ever been in a room like that. I heard it come out of me, and my face went hot, and somewhere behind my ribs something I'd kept shut for a very long time rattled its latch.

"Sergeant," Stroud said, without raising his own voice. "Control your volume."

I should have. A dozen years of habit told me to. That was what I did. I took the order and put it in the place where I put orders, said _yes, sir,_ and found a task to hold on to until the feeling went away.

I looked at him instead.

He had a card in his hand.

I hadn't seen it until then, because I hadn't been looking. It was a small laminated rectangle with a colored tab on one edge, the kind of thing a person carries when they have a plan for every outcome. The corners were soft from use. He'd been holding it at the briefing, in his left hand, while he tapped the board with his right.

_He had it ready._

"Major Ito," Stroud said. "Contingency Delta."

Major Ito's face did something small and unhappy. "Sir. Anvil Two's tag—"

"Is degraded and tells us nothing. The element is lost. Standard denial of the site is the correct response." He turned a corner of the card against his thumb. "Request a fire mission on the annex. Grid as briefed. Request the earliest window from the Directorate."

"Sir, the earliest window with airspace clearance will be 0930," Ito said. He did not look up. "At best."

"Then request 0930."

"Sir, if Staff Sergeant Voeren is still—"

"Send it, Major."

Ito's hands moved over his tablet. They moved slowly.

I stared at the card in Stroud's hand and understood something I'd spent my whole career not understanding.

_Everyone in this building has a tab._

Haskins had a tab. Doyle did. A lieutenant and a sergeant somewhere under that concrete had one, and somebody had drawn a line through each name and gone on with their morning. Voeren had one. She'd signed her papers and taken the serum and put her body between the line and everyone else, and the people she did it for had looked at her and seen a number that could be spent.

I'd always known. I'd known the way you know the weather. I'd never had to stand inside it.

_She isn't a fucking tab._

The thought arrived with a clarity so total it frightened me. It was not an opinion. It was a fact, the way gravity was a fact. Whatever Voeren was to the Colonel, to the Directorate, to the people who filed the cards, she was not that to me.

"Sir," I said. My voice had gone quiet and strange. "Give me the serum."

Somebody laughed.

It was a short, startled sound, quickly smothered. Then another, from the far end of the table. A staff officer put his hand over his mouth and shook his head. It rolled around the room in a ripple, tired and incredulous, the kind of laugh people give when someone has said something they can't believe.

Pruitt didn't laugh. Major Ito didn't either. I noticed that and didn't know what to do with it.

Stroud looked at me for the first time since the cameras died.

"You are out of your mind, Sergeant."

"The serum. Bastion. Whatever it takes. I can get there faster than a second element, I can—"

"You'd die." He said it with no heat at all, as if reading a number off a card. "People die on that table. In a hospital bed, with a team, with every resource this garrison can spare. You would die in a corridor. Painfully."

I had nothing to say to that. I was still looking at him.

"Sit down, Sergeant."

"Sir. You're already calling in a strike. You're going to _bomb_ them."

"I am going to deny the enemy a site and its contents."

"She's in there."

"We don't know that."

"WE DON'T KNOW THAT SHE'S NOT."

"Sergeant—"

"DON'T YOU DARE. YOU DON'T KNOW IF THEY'RE DEAD."

"Sir," Pruitt said.

It was very quiet, and it cut through everything.

"Specialist," Stroud said.

"Sir, both recovery tags just dropped." Pruitt was staring at his screen. His face was gray. "Anvil One and Anvil Four. They went out mid-cycle, both at once. And, sir—"

"Say it."

"The last six seconds are identical. Both tags. Same waveform, same interval, to the sample." Pruitt swallowed. "It's the same recording, sir. It's been looping. I flagged the compression, I thought—"

The wall display flickered. The two amber dots inside the annex went out together.

Nobody spoke.

Stroud stood very still. For a moment I thought something might actually cross his face. Then it smoothed over, and he lowered the card.

"They were never alive," he said.

It was the first time I'd seen him say something he hadn't prepared.

I didn't understand. And then I did.

The cold went through me from the base of my skull. _They were never alive._ Two dots on a board, a heartbeat that never changed, a steady and stationary pair inside a building with the door left open. A week of intermittent signals. A hospital room, and a colonel with a folder, and Voeren asking how current the transmissions were.

_It was bait._

It had been bait the whole time, and they'd sent her in anyway.

"Her tag," I said. My voice shook. "Hers is still up."

"Anvil Two's tag is degraded," Stroud said. His composure had returned. "It tells us nothing."

"IT TELLS YOU SHE ISN'T DEAD."

"That is _enough_."

He took one step toward me. Then he did what I'd never seen him do: he raised his voice, and it came out of him as if it had been waiting.

"What the hell can YOU do? What can you do against people who can fight enhanced? NOTHING. You couldn't hold a pistol steady against what was in that building. You are a communications sergeant on light duty with a medical hold, and you will SIT THE HELL DOWN, SERGEANT."

Something went white in my head.

It was a quiet, clean white. A voice somewhere behind it said, very calmly, _Stop. Stop now. This isn't you,_ and I knew it was right. I knew every word it said was right.

I couldn't make it matter.

"YOU SENT HER ON THIS SUICIDE MISSION."

It rang off the painted window and the concrete floor and every face in the room.

Nobody moved.

Major Ito's tablet hung in his hand. Pruitt had gone as still as a photograph. The staff officer who'd laughed was staring at the table. At the back of the room, by the telemetry rack, a gray jacket hadn't moved at all, and I registered it the way you register the weather on a bad day: it was there, and it didn't matter.

Stroud's eyes held mine. The calm had cracked across the middle, and under it was nothing I could name. It wasn't guilt. I didn't think it was even anger. It was closer to the way you look at a tool that has slipped.

He didn't answer. He turned his head.

"Major Ito," he said, in a voice like a closing door. "Get me the Directorate."

I stood there with my pulse in my ears and my hands open at my sides, and the room kept not looking at me.

_Think._

I turned and walked out.

---
The door banged shut behind me, and for the first ten steps I didn't feel anything at all.

The corridor outside the operations room was an old freight passage: cinderblock painted institutional green, a yellow safety line down the center of the floor, a single tube of fluorescent light that buzzed and flickered over a poster about proper lifting technique. LIFT WITH YOUR LEGS. Someone had drawn a small mustache on the figure in the poster, a long time ago. I stared at it as I walked.

Then I stopped, because my legs stopped, and everything arrived at once.

The sound first. The wet crunch, the lens clouding red. A man who'd been joking about a battle ape an hour ago, saying _I can't feel my_—. I put my hand flat against the wall and my stomach turned over, hard. I bent at the waist and breathed through my teeth. Nothing came up. That was almost worse. If I'd been sick I might have felt something close to clean.

_Think._

It came in my own voice, the one I used when a console lit up red and the only way through was a list.

_Think. Inventory._

I straightened. I started counting.

_Transport._ The motor pool needed a signature, and I had no signature. There was a checkpoint on the road and military police at the gate. They'd stop me before the second turn. I wasn't cleared for a vehicle, a weapon, or most of the building.

_Weapon._ A knife behind my right hip. The armory behind a cage. A carbine would have a magazine, and a magazine would have thirty rounds, and I had spent a very long time learning how thirty rounds went.

_Radio._ I had a radio. She couldn't hear it.

_Distance._ I didn't even know how long the drive was. I only knew it was far enough that the sun would be high before I got there, and by then—

_Time._ I checked my watch. 0631.

The fire mission's earliest window was 0930. Three hours. Three hours and a handful of minutes, and then a block of concrete and everything under it would be a hole.

_Her tag's still up._

_Her tag is degraded._ That was Stroud's voice, level and sure. _It tells us nothing._

I did the math again. Then again. Every time, the answer was the same and I kept refusing it, like a man who keeps carrying the same wrong number into a column.

_Think._ I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes. _THINK._

Nothing came.

I could picture the building. I could picture every corridor and door I'd seen on her feed, every catwalk, every black barrel pointed down. I could see the man who'd come off the rail, how he'd crossed the floor, how he'd taken Haskins's head in his hands. I could see Voeren's gloved fingers spread against a wall of light.

_What can YOU do against people who can fight enhanced?_

He was right. That was the thing. It was the thing I couldn't stand.

I'd fought a drunk in a bar with a knife in my hand and a stupid amount of luck. I'd survived a man at the Salt Yards because he'd been too pleased with himself to finish the job. Everything I'd ever done, I'd done by being quick and small and difficult to hit, and it had always depended on the other person making a mistake. Those men did not make mistakes. They'd built a trap around a woman who could throw a man through a wall, and they'd _won_.

And I was a communications sergeant with a bad knee and good ears.

"Stupid," I said, aloud, into the empty hall.

My voice came out wrong. It cracked in the middle.

"Weak. _Weak._ Slow, small, normal, _useless_—"

I hit the wall.

I hit it with the flat of my hand, the way you'd hit a jammed door. The block didn't give. Pain went up my wrist and into my elbow, and something small and plastic cracked against the cinder.

I stared at my hand.

The skull ring was split across its brow. The chip in the eye socket had spread into a hairline fracture, a thin black line running down toward the jaw. It was a stupid piece of arcade junk, not even worth the tickets. I held my thumb in front of my face and could see Voeren's fingers settling it there, her thumb moving once across my knuckle.

_Terrible taste,_ she'd said.

I made a noise I didn't recognize.

It wasn't a sob. I wouldn't have called it one. It was something more like what comes out of an animal that's been shut in a space too small to turn around in. I put the back of my wrist against my mouth and bit down on it, and I stood in that green hall and shook.

_Say something. Do something._

I'd spent my whole life being the one who stayed calm. I'd done it in school, in the barracks, on the first day I signed my name to something I hadn't wanted to sign. Every time someone told me to sit down, I'd sat. Every time the answer was _no_, I'd said _yes, sir,_ and found a task and gone on. It had always felt like strength.

It had been the opposite of strength. It was a long habit of making myself small enough that nobody had to notice, and now the one thing I'd ever wanted to be loud about was sitting in a building I couldn't reach.

_I hate this. I hate me. I hate every single decision that ever put me in this hall._

"Stop," I said, to myself, and my voice cracked again. "Stop. _Stop._"

I couldn't.

"You want to help her?"

I went rigid.

The voice came from my left, quiet and mild. It wasn't the Colonel. It wasn't anyone from the room. It sounded like someone reading the instructions off a bottle.

I turned.

A man stood at the far end of the corridor, just beyond the reach of the flickering light. He was narrow and gray-jacketed, with a face I would never have been able to describe an hour ago, mild and lined and ordinary, the kind of face that slid out of your memory the instant it left your sight. A credential hung from his lapel, flipped inward so I couldn't read the name. In the glare I made out a single letter on the back, white on blue. A _V_.

I'd seen him twice that morning. I'd registered him the way you register a coat stand.

He'd been in the room. That was the first thing I understood. He'd been at the telemetry rack, winding a cable around his hand, through all of it, through every word.

I had no idea how long he'd been standing in the hall.

"What?" I said.

"Do you really want to help her?"

His voice hadn't risen. His hands were folded in front of him, and there was nothing in his face I could hold on to. No sympathy. No judgment. He was watching me the way a man watches a gauge.

Something inside me broke loose.

"_Yes!_" It tore out of me, raw and cracked and louder than the hall could hold. "Yes! No shit! What do you think I've been—were you in the _room_? Did you hear what I—"

"I heard all of it."

"Then you _know_—"

"I know what the Colonel told you." He took a step forward. The light caught his face, and it was still ordinary, still mild. "He's a careful man. Carefulness has its uses. It isn't going to help you, or her. He's already filed her under _Delta_, Sergeant. He won't help you."

I didn't answer. I couldn't. The word _filed_ stood in the middle of the air like a hand on my throat.

"So I'll ask you again." He tilted his head, a small, curious motion. "What are you willing to do?"

"Whatever it takes."

"That's easy to say in a hallway."

"_Whatever it takes!_" I took a step toward him, and my knee shouted, and I didn't care. "Whatever I can do, I'll do! I don't care what it is! I don't care what it costs! If I have to walk in there with my bare hands—"

"You wouldn't survive that."

"Then tell me what _will_—"

His mouth twitched.

It was a small thing, barely a movement at all, a flicker at one corner like a nerve catching. For half a second, something showed in his face that I might, on any other morning, have noticed. It was bright and attentive and very quiet. It looked like a man who'd been waiting a long time at a locked door and had just heard the first tumbler turn.

I noticed. I didn't care.

"There might be something you can—"

"Then I'll do it."

The words left me before he'd finished, as quick and certain as a hand closing around a rail. Some distant part of me registered that he hadn't said what it was. That I didn't know. That I'd given my answer to a man I had never spoken to, before he'd said a single word about the price.

It didn't occur to me to ask.
