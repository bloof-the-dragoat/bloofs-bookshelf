## Chapter 3: The Harbor Master's Daughter

> [!warning] Content note  
> Aftermath of combat, traumatic recollections, alcohol misuse, racist and sexist harassment.

The sound was too regular to belong to the yard. A soft tone, a pause, another. No alarm underneath it. No rotors.

I kept my eyes closed and tried to work out where my hands were. One rested on my stomach. The other lay beside me on something smooth and cool, palm up and empty.

My fingers curled. I remembered a thumb moving across my knuckles.

Then I opened my eyes. White ceiling, daylight through an adjustable blind, a monitor beside the bed keeping time with the sound.

_Hospital._

I turned my head, and Voeren was sitting by the window.

For a moment I only looked at her. No blood on her face, no armor. A clean shirt stretched across her shoulders, a white dressing over the cut on her arm, and hair that had dried into a shape she seemed to have given up arguing with. The scar through her right brow looked paler in daylight. She was staring down at her hands, and one boot kept shifting against the floor, stopping, starting again.

“Voeren?”

Her head came up and she leaned forward so fast the chair creaked. “Hey.” It came out almost on a breath. Then she smiled, and I had to look at the blanket. “You back with us?”

I tried to answer. My tongue had stuck to the roof of my mouth.

“Water?” She reached for the cup and waited for my nod before she held it out.

I drank too quickly and my throat caught. “Easy,” she said, and I lowered the cup. Her chair was close enough that one leg had disappeared under the bed.

“How long?”

“It’s Friday.”

“What?”

“Late morning.”

“No, since—”

“Monday,” she said, more quietly.

Four days. I looked down at my hands. Pink lines crossed both palms, and a bruise beside one thumb had faded yellow at the edges. I turned the hand over and saw webbing cutting into skin, heard the scrape of the sheet behind me, felt a knee on my chest.

My breath stopped. The room flashed white. I knew where I was. I could see the blind and the cup and the clean sheet under my hands, and I still couldn’t pull in enough air. The cup hit the table hard enough to spill.

“Spade?”

Reyes on the radio. Chen—

My stomach heaved. Voeren put a basin within reach and I bent over it with a hand across my mouth, waiting for something to happen. Nothing did. I swallowed, and it hurt. The monitor kept sounding. I watched a drop of water gather at the base of the cup because it was something to watch.

“Take your time,” she said.

“The team.”

Her smile was gone. “We don’t have everyone accounted for.”

“Reyes?”

“I don’t know.”

“And Chen.”

She held my gaze and didn’t answer, which was an answer.

Everything in the room was clean. My hands, my clothes, the sheets. It had happened anyway.

“I almost—” I touched my throat.

Her hand left the chair arm and stopped on the edge of the mattress, close but not touching. “You’re here.”

I looked at her hand, then at her. “You saved me.”

“Had some help.”

“No. I mean—”

“I know.”

I wanted to say it properly, in a way that could hold the man in the bay, the pier, her hand still there when the rest of me had stopped working. I had nothing. “Thank you.”

She dropped her eyes and rubbed a thumb across one scarred knuckle. “You didn’t do so bad yourself, Sergeant.”

The rank should have helped. It didn’t. I looked at her side. “How’s the wound?”

“Better.”

“That’s not an answer.”

Her eyebrows rose, and I heard myself. “Sorry. I just—”

“They closed it. Checked it. Checked it again.” She eased back. “I’m sore. I’m getting tired of people asking whether I’m sore.”

“You could try telling them.”

“Dangerous precedent.”

A laugh got out of me. It pulled at my throat, but the sound was mine, and her smile came back. I watched it for a second longer than I should have.

Captain Marlowe arrived with a tablet under one arm and a coffee that looked abandoned even while being carried. “Sergeant Spade. Good. A conversation I might not have to repeat.”

I straightened. “Captain.”

“Stay comfortable. How much do you remember?”

“The pier.”

“And afterward?”

Light through my eyelids. Someone asking me to squeeze their fingers. Hunger. “Bits.”

“That fits. You’ve been awake. Answered questions, eaten, complained about the food.”

“I did?”

“An encouraging sign.”

Voeren turned toward the window, and I caught her smile before she hid it.

Marlowe checked the monitor, then asked about my throat, back, and knee. I tried to separate what hurt now from what I remembered hurting. “Sore,” I said eventually. “Not like before.”

“No fractures on imaging. No unstable injury to your spine. Your knee was badly sprained, but the major structures held.”

I remembered trying to stand on that knee. “The treatment did all that?”

“The treatment, rest, and a substantial amount of food.” Marlowe checked the chart. “You responded very well to the clinical course.”

“How well?”

“Better than I expected from the condition you arrived in.”

My fingers tightened on the sheet. “Is that bad?”

Marlowe looked up. “No. You’re allowed a good result, Sergeant.”

“Oh.” _Okay. Cool. Normal. Nobody is looking for a reason._

“But a body that looks repaired isn’t necessarily ready for everything you intend to do with it. We’ll assess your walking after lunch.”

“And the sleeping?”

“Exhaustion. Medication. The Recovery Aid course. You weren’t unconscious for four days straight.”

I let go of the sheet, and Voeren noticed. “You’ve been terrible company,” she said.

“How often have you been here?”

The question was out before I could make it casual. She shifted in the chair.

“Often enough to know which chair doesn’t squeak,” Marlowe said, picking up the tablet.

“Captain.”

“What? Useful information.”

I looked at Voeren. She folded her arms. “I had appointments.”

“All four days?”

Her eyes came back to mine. I hadn’t meant to push. Then one corner of her mouth moved. “It’s a small hospital.”

Marlowe carried on as if neither of us had spoken. “If the assessment goes well, discharge this afternoon. Light duty. Follow-up. Instructions you will read and obey.”

“Yes, Captain.”

“Excellent.” Marlowe collected the coffee, paused at the door, and pointed at the tray. “And eat lunch.”

The door closed. Voeren was still looking at me, and I tried not to smile and failed.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“Convincing.”

I picked up the cup before I could say something worse. I was still deciding whether toast counted as the lunch Marlowe meant when the knock came. Voeren was on her feet before the door finished opening.

I saw the rank first. _Colonel._ I started to straighten.

“At ease,” Colonel Stroud said. He glanced at the tray, then the tablet by the door. “Sergeant Spade.”

“Sir.”

“Staff Sergeant.”

“Sir,” Voeren answered. Her voice had changed. Not colder, just more carefully placed.

Stroud stayed standing. “We’ve received intermittent medical-status transmissions from two members of the missing element.”

I gripped the edge of the tray. “Who?”

“Identities and locations are being verified.”

“Current transmissions?” Voeren asked.

“Recent enough to warrant recovery.”

She waited.

“You’ll lead the return element. Two additional enhanced personnel. Monday, pre-dawn.”

I looked at her. “Three?” she asked.

“On the recovery element. Support arrangements will be covered in the brief.”

“The depot was prepared for the first operation.”

“Yes. I’ve read the reports.”

“They knew we were coming, sir.”

Stroud’s gaze stayed on her. “And your familiarity with the site is one of the few advantages we retain.” Her jaw tightened. “The relay failed,” he went on. “The element fragmented. Before we commit another force, I expect the survivors to be useful in correcting those failures.”

“The relay was hit,” I said.

Both of them looked at me. My hands were still on the tray, and I could have sworn I’d planned to stay quiet. “It held for eleven minutes after the interference started.”

“Then you’re familiar with its limitations.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You’ll establish communications support at the nearby support headquarters.”

I stared at him. Monday. I had learned it was Friday about forty minutes ago. “I’m on light duty.”

“Your restrictions will be accounted for. You won’t accompany the entry team.”

Voeren glanced at me. I couldn’t tell whether that made her feel better. It hadn’t helped me.

“The packet will come through the usual channel,” Stroud said. “I expect both of you prepared.”

“Yes, sir,” Voeren answered. I found the words a moment later, which was a moment too late, and he was already gone. The door clicked shut.

Voeren looked at it. “Fucking generous.”

“Three people?”

“Apparently.”

“They know what happened.”

“They know what came back.”

I stared at the toast. The butter had melted into it. Monday had been a word a minute ago. Now it was her, going back into the yard, and me listening from a room somewhere while the net did whatever it did.

She lowered herself into the chair. “Hey.” I looked up. “Not doing the mission in here.”

“What?”

“Not right now.”

I rubbed my palms against the blanket. “All right.”

She stayed until I had finished most of the tray. We talked about the food, the window that didn’t open, and the chair, which apparently did squeak if she leaned too far left. Ordinary things, and they were somehow the best part of the week.

Then she reached for her jacket. “I should let you rest before they march you up and down the corridor.”

“Oh.”

She paused, and I looked at the empty cup.

_Say something._

“Hey.” She turned. “Need a drink?”

For a second neither of us moved. “Later,” I added. “After discharge. If they discharge me.”

“You asking me?”

“No, the—yes. You.”

Her hand stayed on the jacket. I couldn’t read her face. “You probably have things to do. I just thought—”

“Spade.”

I stopped.

“I’d like that. Very much, actually.”

My ears were hot. “All right.”

“All right.” She put the jacket on, then adjusted a sleeve that didn’t need adjusting. “Where do you want to go?”

“I hadn’t planned that far.”

“Good. I was starting to feel inadequate.”

I laughed, and she smiled at the sound, as if it were something she’d been waiting to hear.

“Get cleared,” she said. “We’ll work out the rest.”

---

Marlowe did not consider “I can walk” a complete assessment. There was a corridor, and there were questions, and about halfway back I realized that standing still had been doing an excellent job of disguising how tired I was. By the time I returned to the room, my knee was stiff and my back ached.

Marlowe looked unsurprised. “Light duty,” the captain said. “Not light duty until you get bored.”

“Yes, Captain.”

“Rest. Follow-up as scheduled. No alcohol for seventy-two hours.”

I stopped looking at the paper. “Right.”

“That wasn’t decorative.”

“I know.”

“And no additional reknit without review. Field injector included. You’ve finished a clinical course, not developed immunity to consequences.”

I repeated the instructions, and Marlowe waited until I had them right.

My effects arrived in a labelled bag: piercings, clothes, personal items. My knife. I touched the sheath through the plastic and got rust against the blade, a cold railing, Voeren on the bricks. I put the bag down, read the discharge instructions again, folded them, and took them with me.

In the locker-room mirror my septum ring sat slightly crooked. I straightened it, then touched it again. _Leave it alone._ The rust-brown overshirt covered most of the fading bruises on my neck, with cream underneath, olive trousers, and brown boots. Clothes I liked. I pushed the right sleeve up to show the tattoo, pulled it down, and pushed it up again. This was getting embarrassing even without an audience.

I secured the knife behind my right hip and let the overshirt fall over it. I checked the mirror once more. Same person.

I went to meet her.

---

Voeren was leaning against a charcoal coupe at the far side of the lot, and I slowed down without deciding to. The leather jacket was worn at the cuffs and tight across her shoulders, with band patches I didn’t recognize and a chain at her hip. Heavy boots, ripped black jeans, a small hoop in one ear and three studs in the other. The eyebrow barbell caught the light when she looked up.

Her grin arrived before I did. “There you are.”

“Hey.”

She straightened off the car. 6'3 with the boots, and close enough now that I had to tip my chin to keep looking at her face. Her gaze moved over me and came back. “What?” I asked.

“Nothing bad.”

“That’s specific.”

“Didn’t know what to expect under the uniform.”

I looked down at myself. “More clothes, mostly.”

She laughed, and some of the tension left my shoulders. “We look like we’re headed to different places.”

I took in her jacket, then my overshirt. “I can change.”

Her smile shifted. “Why?”

“I don’t know. You said—”

“Different.” She stepped closer. “Not worse.” I looked up. “Those colors suit you.”

“Thanks.” Her eyes dropped to my sleeve. “Didn’t know you had that, either.”

“The uniform hides most of it.”

“Apparently it’s been hiding quite a lot.”

I touched the edge of the cuff. “Your jacket’s cool.”

“Thank you.”

“The whole thing, actually.” Her eyebrows rose, and I made a gesture that unfortunately took in most of her. “It works.”

She looked down at herself, then back at me. “Glad you approve.”

_Stop helping._ I’d have loved to say that to my mouth out loud.

She tipped her head toward the passenger side. “Come on. Before somebody finds another form for you.”

I lowered myself into the seat carefully. The sheath pressed against my back, so I shifted it, pulled the overshirt clear, and fastened the belt. Drumsticks sat in the door pocket and cables filled the back seat. Voeren glanced over as she got in. “All right?”

“Just stiff.”

She waited until I’d settled before she started the engine. The growl filled the cabin. I looked at the dashboard, then at her, and she was trying very hard not to look pleased.

“Does it do that every time?”

“Better.”

“Better than what?”

“The last one.”

I opened my mouth, caught her grin, and shut it. “Oh, you’re fucking with me.”

“A little.”

She pulled out of the space. Outside the gate people were doing ordinary things, carrying groceries, waiting at lights, arguing into phones outside takeaway windows. I watched someone balance a bag on one hip while searching for their keys. They had probably done exactly that on Monday too, while I was learning what a knee on my chest felt like. The thought sat in my stomach for a second, and I let it.

Voeren turned the music down. “You’ve gone quiet.”

“Sorry.”

“Wasn’t a complaint.”

I smoothed a crease in my trousers. A few seconds passed. “We don’t have to do the bar,” she said. “There’s food elsewhere. Or I can take you home.”

“No.” She glanced over, and I made myself keep going. “I want to go. I’m just not very good at this.”

“Car journeys?”

“Talking.”

“You’ve been talking to me fine.”

“That was different. That was a hospital. People expect you to be weird in a hospital.”

The smile left her mouth, and I sat up straighter. “I don’t mean I’m uncomfortable with you.”

“Right.”

“I asked because I wanted to.”

Her thumb stopped moving on the wheel. Then she looked over again. “Good. I said yes for the same reason.”

I turned toward the window before my face could make that difficult.

“You don’t have to keep me entertained,” she added.

“I’m trying to contribute.”

“‘More clothes, mostly’ was a strong opening.”

“I can’t promise another one.”

“We’ll ration it.”

The silence got easier after that. She turned the music up a notch and I watched the shops go past, and when somebody attempted a turn that involved three lanes and a lot of faith, we had something easy to agree on.

Then she asked, “What would you have been doing tonight?”

“Probably playing guitar.”

“You play?”

“Yeah.”

“What kind of stuff?”

“Mostly alternative. Punk, goth. Depends what I’m in the mood for.” Her mouth lifted, and I glanced at the patches on her jacket. “I suppose you might have opinions.”

“A few.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

“Only if yours are bad.”

I smiled down at my hands. She turned the music down another notch. “What are you working on?”

I told her about a song I kept going back to. The opening was fine, and so was the section after it, but getting between them was apparently beyond the abilities of my hands. “It’s the change,” I said. “I can play both parts separately. Put them together and suddenly I’ve never held a guitar.”

“Slower?”

“Insultingly slow. I’ve tried.”

She laughed. “I hate when that works.”

“It hasn’t worked yet. That’s the insulting part.”

“What happens?”

I tapped the rhythm against my knee. “Here. I keep carrying this into the next section, but it changes underneath—”

She marked the next beat on the wheel, and I stopped. “That.”

“Yeah.”

“You know it?”

“Know it? I’ve annoyed people with that song.”

I turned to face her. “On what?”

“Drums.”

My eyes went to the sticks in the door pocket. Her grin widened. “Usually a clue.”

“I saw them.”

“Mm.”

“I was distracted by the engine.”

“Of course you were.”

She checked the road, still smiling. “Play it for me sometime.”

The tapping stopped. “The part I can’t do?”

“The rest of it too.”

I looked down at my hand. “Yeah,” I said. “I could do that.”

For a little while we talked about the song, the part she liked and the part I kept getting wrong, and whether I was making the transition harder by thinking about it too much. “You sound very certain,” I said.

“I haven’t heard you play yet. Excellent time to be certain.”

I laughed. “What else?” she asked.

“Besides failing to play guitar?”

“Can’t take up the whole evening.”

“You underestimate how annoying it is.”

“Beginning to understand.”

“Drawing,” I admitted. “Writing sometimes.”

“Stories?”

“Yeah.”

“What kind?”

“Fantasy.”

That was a safe answer. A manageable one. It was _not_ the other stuff, which was nobody’s business and definitely not hers, in a car, the same day I’d woken up.

She waited, so I added, “Mostly worldbuilding. Places. How people live. Things like that.”

“Do you draw the places?”

“Sometimes.”

“Would you show me?”

My fingers went still. “If you wanted.”

“I’m asking.” I looked across. She was watching the road, but the corner of her mouth had lifted.

“There’s a city,” I said. “It used to be a port.”

“Used to be?”

“The sea moved away.”

“What happened to the people?”

I turned toward her. _Oh no._ I could feel it starting, the whole eager machinery of me getting ready to run. “That’s the interesting part.”

---

The Low Tide sat between a shuttered shop and a takeaway window that breathed heat onto the pavement. The doorman checked our identification and waved us through. Music met us first, with conversation underneath it, glasses touching, and a complaint from the pool table at the back. The room was longer than the frontage suggested, with the counter along one wall and booths opposite. A woman behind the counter was drying a glass, a towel slung over one shoulder. She gave the room one flat, practiced sweep as we came in and went back to the glass.

Voeren glanced toward the rear passage before choosing a corner booth. “This work?”

“Definitely.”

She let me sit first, then took the seat across from me with her back to the wall, facing the room. Her jacket came off. The sleeve on her right arm ran from beneath her tank to her wrist: a cracked bell, torn speakers, cables turning into thorned lines. I looked at it, then realized she was watching me look.

“Better than the uniform?”

“The uniform’s harder to read.”

“Very diplomatic.”

“I’m trying.”

She rested her forearms on the table, and the booth seemed to get smaller. We ordered drinks and food, and then she said, “So?”

I looked up.

“The city. What happened to everyone?”

She had kept the question through the parking lot, the doorway, and the drinks order. I leaned forward. “Some left. The ones who could afford to. But roads crossed the old bay afterward, so the city started becoming something else.”

“Does it know?”

“What?”

“That it’s something else.”

I thought about that. “No. Not really. It still calls itself a port.”

“Of course it does.”

“It has a harbor master.”

Her mouth opened, and then she laughed. “Of course it fucking does. Boats?”

“Yes.”

“Water?”

“Not where the boats are.”

“Excellent. Fully functioning government.”

“They’re very proud.”

“I’m sure.”

The bartender brought our drinks over herself, towel still on her shoulder, and left without comment while I was mid-sentence about the old harbor walls. I stopped and looked at her. “Sorry. That got away from me.”

“Did it?”

“A bit.”

“Because you haven’t explained why they still pay a harbor master.”

“That’s complicated.”

She lifted her glass. “We’ve got time.”

Marlowe’s instructions were folded in my pocket. I could have ordered something else. The booth would still have been there, and so would she. I picked up the glass anyway, and Voeren touched hers lightly against it. “To getting out.”

“Of the hospital?”

“For a start.”

The first mouthful burned. Then I told her about the harbor master, and she interrupted twice, both times with questions I wanted to answer.

---

By the second drink I had stopped checking her face after every sentence. I was drawing the harbor on a napkin with a borrowed pen. “No, this is the old seawall,” I said. “They’ve built over this part.”

“Which seems wise.”

“It was, until the predictions started changing.”

“What predictions?”

“The tides.”

She looked at the napkin, then at me. “The sea’s gone.”

“Not all the way gone.”

“Spade.”

“I know.”

“Are they going to drown?”

“That depends.”

“On?”

“How much of the prediction system is a scam.”

She sat back, and I grinned. She pointed at me. “You enjoyed doing that.”

“A little.”

“There you are.”

I looked down at the drawing. My hands had gotten involved without permission, the right holding the pen while the left outlined something the napkin hadn’t been designed for. Voeren watched them. “What?” I asked.

“I like this version.”

My hand stopped. “What version?”

“The one arguing with me about a harbor that doesn’t exist.”

“Oh.”

She moved a glass away from the napkin. “I’m not complaining about the other one.”

“No. I know.”

I didn’t know, like, at all. I wanted to, though, and the wanting was loud.

Someone at the next table laughed loud enough to drown out my next sentence. Voeren leaned forward. “Missed that.”

“It wasn’t—” She waited, so I tried again. “The harbor master’s daughter knows.”

“About the scam?”

“About the sea.”

Her gaze stayed on mine. I had leaned in to be heard, and now I could see the amber ring around her pupils, a thin bright line in all that gray-green.

“What does she do?” Voeren asked.

An excellent question. I’d been thinking about her mouth. “She—sorry.”

“You do that a lot.”

“What?”

“Apologize.”

“Sorry.”

Her laugh came immediately. I shut my eyes. “Oh my God.”

“No. Keep that one.”

“I’d like to leave my body for a minute.”

“I’ll watch the drink.”

When I looked up, she was still leaning close, still grinning, the crooked edge of it lifting the scar slightly. I started explaining what the daughter planned to do. Then our food arrived.

---

I stole something off the shared plate while she was talking, and then had to make her repeat herself because I’d been concentrating on not dropping it.

“You all right over there?”

“Excellent.”

“Convincing.”

She nudged the plate closer. When the cuff of my sleeve caught on the table edge I pushed it back, and Voeren’s gaze followed. “Can I see?”

I held out my arm. Her hand stopped short of touching, so I turned my wrist up. She settled her fingers lightly underneath it, careful to avoid the healing skin across my palm, and the contact was so ordinary that I hadn’t prepared for it at all.

“The whole arm?”

“Shoulder to wrist.”

“Hidden depths.”

“It’s a tattoo.”

“Let me be impressed.”

I smiled. Her thumb rested on the inside of my wrist, right where my pulse was, and I hoped to God it was behaving. She studied the sheet music breaking apart toward my hand. “Yours?”

“The design?”

“The music.”

“Oh. No. It isn’t a full piece.”

“I was going to add it to the requests.”

“You’d be disappointed.”

“Very confident prediction.”

“It stops.”

“So do most songs.”

“Not usually halfway through a bar.”

She grinned and turned my arm slightly toward the lamp, and her own tattoo shifted against mine. I pointed at the bell with my free hand. “Your turn.”

“My turn?”

“You’ve had mine for a while.”

Her eyes lifted, and I heard it a beat too late. “The tattoo,” I added.

“Of course.”

But she laid her forearm next to mine. For a moment both sleeves lay across the table, mine dense and quiet and hers all sharp edges and noise. “That’s unfair,” I said.

“What is?”

“Even your ink looks loud.”

Her laugh shook the table. I caught the nearest glass and she caught the other, and we looked at each other over the rims before we started laughing again. When she drew her arm back I nearly followed it. I picked up my food instead, which felt like a very adult decision.

---

She shifted in the booth, a small adjustment, and my eyes went to her side. For a second I saw the dressing on the quay with the blood working through it. I reached for my glass, and Voeren noticed. “Monday?”

“Yeah.”

Her smile faded. I turned the glass against the table. “You shouldn’t have to go back yet.”

“Neither should you.”

“I’m at headquarters.”

“You’ll still be on the net.”

“That’s what scares me.” Her hand stopped beside her glass, and I watched the wet ring under mine instead of her face. “I don’t want to listen to it happen.”

The music filled the booth for a while. Then she said, “I don’t want that either.” I looked up. She held my gaze with no joke ready and no promise that she’d be fine. “I’d rather it was you.”

“On the net?”

“Yeah.”

My fingers tightened around the glass. “You haven’t heard how annoying I can be.”

Her mouth lifted a little. “I’m getting an excellent preview.”

I smiled. She did too, but hers took longer. I finished what was left in my glass, and when we ordered again, I didn’t look at the instructions in my pocket. Voeren ordered hers the same way.

---

By the third drink, Voeren was matching me glass for glass, and showing it far less. I was no longer drawing on the napkin. I was drawing in the air, correcting myself halfway through sentences, going back to something she’d asked ten minutes earlier because I’d finally found the right answer. Voeren followed all of it. Sometimes she made me go back. Sometimes she argued.

“Why would they stay?” she asked.

“Because it’s home.”

“Even without the sea?”

“Especially then. If you leave, someone else decides what happened to it.”

She looked at the drawing. I lowered the pen and waited, and then she pointed to a road I’d nearly erased. “So who gets this?”

I leaned forward again. By the time I’d finished explaining, she had moved the napkin clear of her glass and smoothed it flat beside her elbow, as if it were something that mattered.

“You finish any of these stories?”

“A few.”

“Would you let me read one?”

“Maybe.”

“Actual maybe?”

I looked up. “Actual maybe.”

“Good.”

I turned the pen between my fingers. “They’re not all like this.”

“What are they like?”

I could have given her another safe answer. I had a whole shelf of them. Instead I said, “Some have more kissing.”

Her eyebrows lifted. I looked at the table. _Excellent. Very subtle. Truly a master of the craft._

“Right,” she said.

“Not exclusively kissing.”

“I assumed there’d be a plot.”

“There is a plot.”

“I believe you.”

“You look far too pleased.”

“I’m trying very hard not to ruin this.”

I laughed, and she smiled into her glass. I watched her for a moment, then looked back at the drawing. “Most people ask what I write and look relieved when I give them a short answer.”

The words came out whole. I waited for the urge to turn them into a joke. It arrived on schedule, and I left them alone.

Voeren glanced at the napkin. “I’m still waiting to hear about the daughter.”

“You stopped when the food came.”

“Oh. Right. She knows the sea’s coming back.”

“Maybe.”

“Spade.”

I smiled. “All right. She knows.”

“So what does she do?”

I told her. Not the short version, either. Voeren interrupted to disagree with one of the daughter’s decisions, then disagreed with my defense of it, and I found myself leaning over the drawing again and pointing out the route she couldn’t take, talking with both hands, _not_ apologizing. When I finally sat back she was smiling.

“What?”

“You get very particular about people I haven’t met.”

“You asked.”

“I did.” She touched the edge of the napkin. “Would you ever write a story about me?”

“Yeah.” The answer came easily. “I’d love to.”

Her glass stopped halfway to the table.

“I’d need to ask you things,” I continued. “Otherwise it would just be someone who looked like you. Would you want it to be fantasy, or—”

She lowered the glass. “That wasn’t quite what I—” I looked up. Her mouth opened and closed. “Never mind.”

“No. What?”

She rubbed her thumb against the side of the glass. “I was wondering which kind of story I’d get.”

I looked at her. At her mouth. Back at her eyes.

“Oh.” _OH._

She leaned back a little. “You don’t have to answer that.”

“No, I—” Someone at the next table shouted over a game, and I barely heard it. “I wasn’t saying no.”

Her thumb stopped. My face heated.

“I don’t know what I’m saying very well.”

“You’re doing all right.”

“No, because then you say that, and I have to start again.”

She laughed softly. I put the pen down before I wore through the napkin. “If I miss something,” I said, “you can just tell me.”

Her smile eased. “Something?”

“Something you’re doing deliberately.”

She looked down at her glass, then back at me. “All right.”

That was somehow worse than another joke. I took a drink.

_Okay. Okay. She’s flirting with me. She’s been flirting with me. For hours. Possibly since the hospital. Possibly since the chair._ I took another drink.

---

“You write romance,” she said after a while.

“Among other things.”

“And I had to work this hard?”

“I know when characters are flirting.”

“Useful distinction.”

“They give me advance notice.”

She laughed, and I leaned back, pleased with myself. “You could have warned me you were secretly charming.”

“I thought I’d been warning you all evening.”

“Poorly.”

“I’m not much of a romantic lead.”

“You keep saying things like that.”

I turned the pen against the table. “Usually people are still trying to work out what I am.”

Her smile faded a little. “What do you want them to know?”

I looked at her. The answer was out before I’d checked it, which was either the drinks or her. “Non-binary. They/them.”

“All right.”

She didn’t look me over again. She didn’t glance at my clothes or the piercings or anything else I’d apparently offered for interpretation. She just waited while I found somewhere to put the pen.

“And you can still be a convincing romantic lead,” she added.

I smiled despite myself. “You’re very committed to this argument.”

“Strong position. Good evidence.”

I looked down, then back up. “What about you?”

“What about me?”

“Who do you date?”

“Whoever I like.” Her mouth curved. “I’m pan.”

“Oh.”

“Disappointing?”

“No.” That came out fast. Her smile widened, and I repeated it, quieter. “No.”

I rubbed my thumb along the pen. “I’m demi.”

“Good to know.”

I looked at her. “That wasn’t me asking you to stop.”

Her fingers went still on the napkin. “Good to know,” she said again, more quietly this time. My ears were hot. I reached for my drink, changed my mind, and left my hand on the table instead. She was still looking at me, and I let her. _Honestly? Great. Look all you want._

---

“Your turn,” I said.

She raised her eyebrows. “I’ve been talking about myself for ages.”

“I know.”

I stared. She lasted a second. “I’m enjoying it.”

“That’s not fair.”

“What isn’t?”

“You keep doing that.”

“Listening?”

“Saying things.”

“I’ll try to recover from the accusation.”

I shook my head. “Tell me something.”

“About?”

“You.”

She leaned back, considering. “Gym. Boxing. Sparring. Climbing.”

“I’m noticing a pattern.”

“Boring, am I?”

“No. I’m wondering whether you ever sit down.”

“I’m sitting down now.”

“Under protest?”

“Depends how this questioning goes.”

I smiled. “Fine. Drums.”

“What about them?”

“You gave me one song and then got me talking about a city for an hour.”

“You seemed to have quite a lot to say about the city.”

“I have quite a lot to ask about the drums.”

Her smile changed. She put her glass down. “All right. Ask.”

I had questions ready now: what she liked playing, what she did when she couldn’t get something right, whether the songs I liked on guitar were the ones she liked behind a kit. Not always, apparently, and she explained why. I interrupted halfway through. “Wait. That’s the part I like.”

“On guitar.”

“Yes?”

“Try counting what I’m doing underneath it.”

She tapped the pattern against the table, her rings clicking on the wood, and I counted along. She watched my face, and I got there. “Oh.”

“There you go.”

“That’s rude.”

“Take it up with the drummer.”

“I am.”

She laughed and played it again, and this time I joined in against my knee. Her fingers stopped. “Lost you?” she asked.

“No. Again.”

She started over, and this time I caught the change. Her grin spread. “There.”

“I do listen.”

“I’m noticing.”

I missed the next beat when she looked directly at me. She laughed but kept playing, and I found my way back in.

“Better,” she said.

“Condescending.”

“You asked.”

“You’re enjoying this.”

“Immensely.”

I laughed. She let the rhythm slow, then stopped with her fingers resting on the table. “Do you like playing with people?” I asked.

“Depends who’s there.”

“That bad?”

“No. Just—” She tapped once. “You ever play something and someone joins in, and suddenly you don’t have to explain what you were trying to do?”

I looked at her. “Not often.”

“Yeah.”

Her gaze dropped to my hand against my knee, so I moved it onto the table. She started the pattern again, softer this time, and the nearest glass trembled until she stopped it with her palm. “Sorry,” she said, and then she saw me pointing at her.

“Don’t.”

“You do that a lot.”

“Unbelievable.”

“You started it.”

“Could leave.”

I opened my mouth. Stopped. Then I said, “I’d rather you didn’t.”

Her hand stayed around the glass. For a moment she only looked at me. “No plans to.”

I smiled down at the table. “And since you asked for clearer—”

I looked up. “I want to be here with you.”

“Oh.”

Her mouth lifted at one corner, but she didn’t turn it into a joke. I looked at the hand she’d left between us, the scars across the knuckles and a ring turned slightly sideways. I reached over and straightened it. Her fingers shifted beneath mine, and I could have taken my hand away. Instead I let it rest there until she turned hers palm-up.

Neither of us said anything about that. _If I say anything, it stops. So no. Absolutely not. Nobody speak._

---

My fourth drink arrived while we were negotiating a band. Hers had arrived with it, and I'd stopped being able to say who ordered.

“Just the two of us?” I asked.

“Why not?”

“We need a bass player.”

“Already making it difficult.”

“And someone has to sing.”

“You sing.”

I looked at her. “Privately.”

“Exclusive performances.”

“Bad performances.”

“Distinctive sound.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“Could be very punk.”

I laughed into my free hand. Her thumb moved once against the fingers resting in hers, and I looked down and lost the rest of the argument.

“We’d need a name,” she said.

“Oh, absolutely not.”

“You haven’t heard the options.”

“I know enough about band names to be afraid.”

“Fine. You do it.”

I considered her. “So our whole thing is you being loud while I quietly apologize?”

Her laugh broke across the booth. Someone near the back glanced over, and for once I didn’t turn to check whether they were annoyed. Voeren tipped her head back, shoulders shaking. I knew that laugh from across the mess hall. It sounded different when I was the reason for it. Louder, maybe. Looser. Her glass was half gone.

She looked at me. “What?”

“I like that.”

“What?”

I should have made it smaller. The sensible revision arrived late and I ignored it. “Making you do that.”

Her smile stayed, but she looked down at our hands. “Oh.”

For once I wasn’t the one who had to find somewhere else to look. I smiled and let myself keep doing it. _Hi. Yes. It’s me. I’m doing a thing. I like her. Please nobody tell me to stop._

The food had gone cold. My palms were tender, and my back had started to ache despite the booth. When I reached for the glass, I misjudged the distance and caught it with my fingertips, and had to steady it.

Voeren noticed. “You all right?”

“Yeah.”

“Actual yeah?”

I smiled. “Actual yeah. Just a little drunk.”

“A little?”

“Don’t sound so pleased.”

“I’m not pleased.”

“You look pleased.”

She glanced down at our hands. “Not about the drunk part.”

I had no answer for that. None I trusted to come out in the right order. She let me take the time. I looked at the drawing beside her elbow.

“I want to hear you properly,” I said.

Her fingers tightened. “All right.”

“With the guitar. Both of us.”

She watched me for a moment. “When?”

“When we have time.”

The words brought Monday back into the booth. Neither of us looked at it. “When we have time,” she agreed.

I wanted to ask for a day, something specific I could put on the far side of the assignment. Instead I turned my hand in hers and let our fingers settle properly. She looked down, and the corner of her mouth lifted. Then her gaze shifted past my shoulder, and her thumb stopped.

I was still deciding whether to say something when I noticed the rest of her had gone still too.

I turned.

A man stood at the end of the booth, broad in the chest, thick in the neck, with one hand braced against the seat beside me. Another large man stood behind him with a muscular woman. More people crowded the aisle: three other men, and another woman.

Seven. I counted them again, because the first count seemed unreasonable.

The man beside me leaned closer. “Why don’t you and that fuckin gorilla get the hell out of here, yeah?”

My shoulders tightened. It took me a second, an embarrassing second, to understand he wasn’t looking at me. He was looking past me, at her.

My hand was still in Voeren’s. She released it gently and set her own down beside her glass.

“What’s your problem?” I asked. The sentence had sounded sharper in my head. It came out thick around the edges. “We’re having drinks.”

“You’re not the problem.” His chin moved toward her. “Your company is.”

I looked at Voeren. Her face had settled into something I knew from briefing rooms: nothing offered, nothing given away. A minute ago she had been laughing.

“You can take her somewhere else,” he said. “Or the pair of you can fuck off.”

The other large man moved closer, and the woman beside him shifted into the remaining space. I looked toward the aisle.  Not enough room. Past the crowd I could see the counter, and the bartender had stopped drying anything. My knee was stiff under the table, the knife sheath a hard line against the bench at my right hip, and my hands hurt, and I had four drinks in me and discharge instructions in my pocket.

_Leave._

Voeren stood. She slid out on her side, stepped around the end of the table, and stopped at my shoulder, yet a little heavier than I'd expected, close enough to me that the man had to give ground. He gave it barely. Her hand settled on my shoulder.

“We can go,” she said quietly.

We could. We could find somewhere else, sit in the car, get food we weren’t already too distracted to eat. We had talked about playing together, and that could still happen.

I looked at the napkin beside her glass, the old harbor, the little roads I’d drawn badly because she kept making me laugh.

The man tapped the side of the booth. “Come on.”

Voeren’s fingers pressed once against my shoulder. I could have stood. I _should_ have stood.

Instead I looked at him. “Nah.”

Her hand went still.

He tipped his head. “Nah?”

I closed my fingers around the glass. There were seven of them. I knew that. I smirked anyway.

“**You fuckin deaf, guy? I said fuck off**.”
