
# Chapter 9: Unauthorized

> [!warning] Content Note 
> Medical pain and risk and military violence.

He didn't tell me his name.

I realized that later, somewhere between the second door and the third, when it occurred to me that I had followed a stranger in a gray jacket for several minutes without asking him one thing.

He walked quickly but didn't hurry, like a man who knew nobody would stop him. We went down the freight passage, through a fire door, and out across a gravel yard where the morning had come up thin and cold. The air smelled of diesel and wet concrete. The sky over the garrison wall had turned the pale color of weak tea. My watch said 0642. Somewhere to the north, a building I couldn't see was still standing, and I kept walking.

The research annex was a low gray block with no windows on the street side. A private stood at the door with a rifle across his chest. His eyes went to my sleeve, then to my face, and then to the man in front of me.

"Doctor," the private said.

"Private."

That was all. The door opened. Nobody looked at me twice. A sergeant with a bad knee, trailing a civilian into a building that did not appear on any list I'd seen, was exactly the kind of thing people agreed not to notice.

_Doctor._ I filed it away.

The corridor inside was long and clean and smelled of antiseptic. At the end, a door bore a small plate of silver letters: VEYR DYNAMICS — TECHNICAL LIAISON. He pressed his thumb to a reader. The lock gave a quiet click, and we went in.

There were two rooms. The first was an office, bare to the point of looking unlived in: a desk, a coffee maker with a brown ring of old residue, a calendar from the previous year. The second room was behind a heavy door, and when he opened it, the cold hit my face.

It was white, windowless, and humming. A long padded table stood in the center with restraints folded at the sides like seat belts. An IV stand and a bank of monitors waited at its head. Against one wall, a refrigerated cabinet ticked softly to itself. It looked like a hospital room that had been told to keep a secret.

He closed the door behind us, and the lock engaged.

"Please," he said. "Sit."

I didn't. "I already said I'd do it."

"You did." He folded his hands in front of him. "And I would like you to hear it anyway. I am not required to. I'd like to."

It was a strange thing to say, and I understood it was meant to sound decent. I sat on the edge of the table because my knee had started to shake.

"I'm a technical liaison for Veyr Dynamics," he said. "That's why I was in the room. I monitor the telemetry of Bastion recipients for the contractor, so nobody in the field has to. I'm also a physician, in the sense that matters. You may call me Doctor."

"Doctor."

"What I'm about to offer is not Bastion." He said it carefully, like a man reading a label. "It's a different formula. It has not been approved by anyone. It has been in cultures, in tissue, and in my own notes for a long time, and it has never been given to a person."

"Never."

"Never. You would be the first."

I turned that over. It was strange how little it moved me.

"There are three outcomes I can describe," he said. "It may do nothing. It may hurt you, badly, and do nothing. Or it may kill you. I can't give you odds, because there is no data. Anyone who offers you a number is lying."

"And if it works?"

"Then it's permanent. There's no reversing it, no taper, no second opinion." He paused. "The process will hurt. I won't tell you how much, because I don't know how much, and I'd rather not guess. Afterward you will be hungry and tired and sore. It will feel like your body being rewritten, because it will be."

I looked down at my hands. The bandage on my right palm had slipped. The skull ring sat on my left thumb with its crack running down the jaw, and my thumb had found the crack without being told.

"There's one more thing."

"Go on."

"There is no paperwork." He said it gently. "That's rather the point. You will sign nothing. If this works, the Colonel will have words for you, and I would like to be somewhere else when he does. If it doesn't work, nobody will have anything at all."

It was a very clear sentence, and I noticed what it didn't say. It didn't say what would happen to him.

"Why me?"

"Because there's no one else in that room who would say yes." He tilted his head. "That's true, but it isn't all of it."

"Then what is?"

"You're a pretty good fit for this."

I waited.

"I read everyone's profile, Sergeant. It's what I do. The garrison's liaison desk sends me every chart summary that crosses it, and I have read yours." He said it mildly, without any weight at all. "Something in it caught my eye."

"Caught it how?"

"I'd rather not say."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "If I'm right, you will find out on your own. If I'm wrong, it won't matter."

I should have asked what that meant. I understood exactly what it meant.

There was a list of things a person should do at a moment like this. I could see every item on it, the way you see exits in a burning building: ask for time, ask for his name, ask what it would cost, ask what he got out of it. I saw them all. I walked past every one.

"How long does it take?"

"Onset takes an hour, perhaps two. You'll be unconscious for part of it."

I checked my watch. 0652. The fire mission's window was 0930.

"Is that a deadline?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Then we should begin."

He rolled up his sleeves. I lay back on the table, and the pad gave beneath me with a sigh. The ceiling was a flat, shadowless white. I stared at it and tried to find a thought that wasn't a number.

_Hold on._ I had no idea who I was saying it to. _I'm coming._

---

"The straps are for your safety," the Doctor said. "You'll seize."

"Do it."

He did it carefully. Wrists, ankles, a broad band across my chest that pinned the ache in my ribs against the pad. I felt the cold edge of a tourniquet around my left arm and the quick, clean pinch of a needle. He taped the line. A second syringe waited on a tray, filled with something pale and amber, the color of weak tea.

"Count with me if you like."

I didn't. I looked at the ceiling.

He pressed the plunger.

For a moment, there was nothing. Then a cold ran up my arm, so sharp and clean that it felt like clean water. It climbed to my elbow, my shoulder, my throat.

Then it turned.

The heat arrived all at once, a white wave that began in the vein and spread outward through every nerve. My back arched against the straps. A sound came out of me that I didn't recognize. Every muscle in my body locked, one after another, as if someone had gone down a row of switches.

"Heart rate one-forty," the Doctor said, somewhere very far away. "One-sixty. Good."

_Good._

It wasn't pain. I'd known pain. I'd known it on a canal wall and in a bar and on a floor with a man's hands at my throat. This was something else. It was my own body, being taken apart by hands that weren't mine, and every piece of me was awake to feel it.

My teeth cracked together. I bit my tongue and tasted copper. The straps creaked. My heartbeat had stopped being a rhythm and become a drum someone was beating with both fists.

_I'm dying._

It came with a flat, strange calm, the way a number arrives at the end of a sum. I wasn't afraid. There wasn't room for it. There was only the fire, and the knowledge, very clear and very small, that nobody in this room was going to stop it.

And then she was there.

I hadn't asked for it. It didn't come as a thought. It came as a flood, sound and light and skin all at once, so fast that I couldn't hold on to any one piece.

_"HAH! Need help, Spade?"_

A laugh over a broken bulkhead, her hand closing on my forearm.

_"Not anyone."_

Her fingers, covering mine on the strap, and the blue light of the pier behind her.

_"I've got you."_

The weight of her hand on the stretcher. The warmth of it. The way the world had gone soft at the edges as I let go.

_"You can be both."_

A bathroom light and her mouth fighting a smile. My ears on fire.

_"Can I kiss you back?"_

A couch, a paused film, her palm against my cheek.

_"You nervous?"_

Her breath at my ear. The weight of the sticks in my hands.

_"Terrible taste."_

Her fingers settling a plastic ring onto my thumb. Her thumb moving once across my knuckle.

The fire went through me again and my vision whited out. I couldn't tell if I was screaming. I couldn't hear anything but the drum.

_"To stay?"_

_"Yeah."_

_"I can do that."_

Her smile, coming slowly, in my apartment, in the amber light.

It was the last thing I held.

I felt it the way you feel the last rung of a ladder. I didn't think _her_ or _because of her._ There was no thought at all, only the small, bright weight of a sentence she'd said, and under my left thumb, in the dark, the crack in the ring.

"There it is," the Doctor was saying, from very far off. His voice had changed. It was soft and quick and oddly warm, the way a man speaks over something he's been waiting a long time to see. "Come on. Come on. There you are."

The fire swallowed it.

---
The first thing I knew was the ceiling.

It was white and flat and shadowless, and it took me a long time to understand that I was looking at it and not at the inside of my own eyelids. My mouth tasted of copper and old coins. My throat felt as though I'd swallowed a handful of gravel and screamed it back up.

I tried to move my hand, and it moved.

The straps were gone. There were faint red lines across both wrists, like bracelets, and as I watched, they began to fade. I stared at them for a second without understanding what I was seeing.

A clock hung over the door. It read 0803.

_Forty minutes._ I'd been counting nothing, and forty minutes had gone.

"Welcome back, Sergeant."

The Doctor sat in a chair against the far wall, a tablet resting on his knee. He looked exactly as he had when I'd lain down. Not tired, not worried, only mildly attentive, like a man watching a kettle.

"Did it—"

"It was a success."

He said it quietly, and with the faintest smile. "As far as I can tell. I'd like to be careful with the word. The formula needs another hour to settle, perhaps a little longer. You'll feel it arriving in pieces. I'd like you to stay here while it does."

I'd stopped hearing him at _success._

I sat up.

I waited for the catch in my ribs, the sharp, familiar hitch that had followed every breath for a week. It didn't come. I took a deeper breath, and then a deeper one, and found the edge of my lungs exactly where it should have been, with nothing pulling there at all.

I swung my legs off the table. My knee bent. It bent all the way, without the grumble, without the little lock at the end. I rolled my neck, and the bruises along my throat, which had been a dull ache for days, didn't ache.

The dressing on my right palm had come half off. I peeled the rest away.

Under it, the skin was pink and new, smooth as the inside of a cheek.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange part. The injury had stopped being a thing that hurt. The pain had gone somewhere else, and it was everywhere: a deep, crawling, burning ache under my skin, as though a thousand small hands were working in the dark and every one of them was furious.

"That's the repair," the Doctor said. "It will hurt as long as it's happening. You should expect that."

"What does it do?"

"Speed, and recovery." He set the tablet aside. "Past that, I'd be guessing. It won't make you stronger. It won't make you harder to kill. You'll bleed the way you bled yesterday. You'll simply close faster." His eyes stayed on mine. "Don't mistake healing for armor."

"How fast?"

"I can tell you it isn't finished."

"I'm going."

"I'd rather you didn't."

"I know."

"You're my first," he said gently. "The first hour tells me a great deal. I would like to watch it."

"No."

I'd said it before I decided to. It came out flat and level, the voice I used at the console when a line went red. He studied me for a moment, then inclined his head.

"Then at least eat."

He stood and went to the cabinet. When he came back, he was holding three ration bars and a bottle of water that had been stirred cloudy with sugar. "Eat. You are about to burn through everything you have."

I took them. I didn't mean to be graceless. The first bar was gone in under a minute, and I hadn't tasted it. The second followed. I drank half the bottle in a gulp and it hit my stomach like cold iron, and I was hungrier than I had ever been in my life. It felt like an animal had woken under my ribs and was asking, politely and without any sympathy, for more.

I squeezed the wrapper of the third bar in my fist. It crinkled the way a wrapper should. I couldn't have crushed a can with that hand, and I never would. I looked at the thing I was holding and knew what the Doctor had meant, and was oddly relieved.

"If anyone asks," the Doctor said, as I reached the door, "I was never here."

"Understood."

"It will keep arriving," he added, almost kindly. "Don't trust the first thing it tells you."

I didn't ask what that meant. I opened the door and went out into the light.

---

It was 0811 when I reached the signals section.

The room was long and low and smelled of solder and dust. Spare handsets hung in rows on the wall, and a rack of long-range sets stood under a sheet. The key board by the door held eleven hooks. Nine had keys.

Nobody was at the desk. I could hear the hum of the operations room through the wall, and the sound of someone trying not to be heard.

I went to the locker.

I took the carbine from its rack, the section spare, a plain, matte-black thing that had never been fired in anger. I checked the chamber, checked the safety, and took three magazines from the shelf, which was every round the section was cleared to give me. I clipped the radio to my vest, added the med pouch and a folded gray drag sheet from the bottom shelf, and checked the knife at my hip with a quick touch.

_The drag sheet._ I hadn't decided to take it. My hand had done it on its own.

I stood there for a moment with it in my hands, a rolled gray thing with webbing loops. The last time I'd used it, I'd been counting steps to a pier.

I put it on my pack.

"Sergeant."

Pruitt stood in the door. He'd closed it quietly behind him, and his face had the look of a man in the middle of something he'd decided and didn't intend to explain.

"Specialist."

"Directorate approved the fire mission at 0758." He kept his voice low. "Window's 0930. It hasn't moved. They won't move it."

"I know."

"I know you do." He held out a small gray handset with a stubby antenna. "It's a tag receiver. It's tuned to AN2."

I took it. The little screen was dark.

"It's weak," Pruitt said. "She's intermittent. But it hasn't gone flat." He swallowed. "I thought you should have it."

I looked at him. He was young for the desk he sat at, and he was very tired, and I realized that he was handing me the only thing in the building that wasn't a lie.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." He was already stepping back. "I never saw you."

---

I made it to the door of the section.

The Colonel was standing on the other side of it.

He had two MPs with him, one on each shoulder, in dress belts, with their hands carefully near their sidearms. Behind them, in the freight corridor, Major Ito stood at the edge of the light with his tablet held against his chest.

Stroud looked at me for a long moment. His eyes went to my face, to the carbine, and to my palm.

"You look rested, Sergeant."

It was almost funny. _Rested and fit for duty._ I couldn't have said whether the joke was on him or on me.

"Sir."

"Where have you been?"

"Around."

"Stand down."

"No, sir."

"That's an order."

"I know, sir."

His expression did not change. It didn't need to. The MPs shifted their weight, and I understood how this would go if I let it: two hands on my arms, a quiet room, a chair. A very long afternoon. A column of smoke at 0930, and then nothing for anyone to do.

I kept my voice level. I didn't have the energy to do anything else.

"All that matters is the mission, right, sir?"

Stroud's eyes narrowed a fraction.

"That's what this is. That's what you told her. That's what the card's for." I looked at him. "Then I'll save our people, or I'll die. Either way, the objective's still the objective."

"That is _not_ what I meant."

"It's what you meant, sir."

Something moved in his jaw.

"The strike's at 0930," I said. "That's correct, isn't it? Then I have until then."

"You'll never reach the site."

"Then you won't have to pay for the fire mission twice."

It was a cruel thing to say, and I didn't regret it.

The MP on his left glanced at Stroud. The one on his right stared at a point just past my shoulder and said nothing at all.

"Sir," Major Ito said.

He'd stepped forward. His tablet was still against his chest, and his face was the color of paper, but his voice was steady.

"With respect. There are twelve people in that room, and every one of them has a screen on this hall. It's recorded. If either of these men lays hands on a sergeant in a corridor who has done nothing but walk, that's going to be a very long report."

Stroud turned his head.

For a moment, I thought he'd order it anyway. His whole body was a calculation. Then I watched something settle behind his eyes, like a figure sliding into a column, and I understood what I was to him: a variable that cost nothing, and one he'd already written off.

"Log it," he said. "Sergeant Spade departed without authorization at 0817."

"Sir," said Ito.

Stroud stepped aside.

He didn't look at me as I passed. The two MPs fell back against the wall like furniture. I walked down the freight corridor with the carbine on my shoulder and my pulse in my ears, and I did not look back.

"Sergeant."

I stopped.

"When you come back," Stroud said, behind me, in his flat, even voice. "If you do. You will answer for every minute of it."

I didn't turn.

"Yes, sir," I said.

I took the keys to the signals section's utility truck off the board. The log on the clipboard asked for a destination and a purpose. I wrote: _Salt Yards. Repeater service._

It wasn't a lie.

It wasn't the truth.

I signed my name and went out into the morning.

---
The gate guard read my log entry twice.

_Salt Yards. Repeater service._ He looked at me, at the carbine on the seat beside me, and at the section's gray utility truck, which had a cracked wing mirror and a dent in the tailgate from some other sergeant's bad day. Then he looked at the clipboard again.

"Departure's already logged, Sergeant," he said. "Colonel's office called ahead."

I didn't ask what they'd said. He raised the barrier.

I was through it before I understood what had been odd about his voice.

I'd heard him from the seat of the truck, with the window up and the engine running. He'd been standing eighteen meters away, and he'd spoken at the pitch of a man reading a form aloud. I had caught every word. Not only the words. The little dry click of his tongue against his teeth, the scratch of the pen on the clipboard. Somewhere behind him, a radio in the guardhouse was playing a song, and I could have named the song.

_Don't trust the first thing it tells you._

I didn't know yet which thing that was.

---

The road out of the garrison ran east along the canal, a straight gray strip between a drainage ditch and a wall of salt-stained fence. At 0824 the sun was a white disk behind low cloud. The light was flat and even and ugly. Dust sat in the air like gauze.

I ate the last ration bar with one hand and drove with the other.

The hunger hadn't gone. It had changed shape. It wasn't in my stomach anymore, but under it, deeper, a hollow, patient pull, like a tide going out. I'd eaten three bars and drunk a liter of sugared water, and I could have eaten the wrapper. When I swallowed, my throat clicked. My tongue felt too large.

My hands wouldn't stay still.

They weren't shaking exactly. They were _busy_. My fingers tapped the wheel in no rhythm I knew, and when I made them stop, the tap moved to my thigh. My left foot jittered against the floor. Every nerve under my skin felt like a held note.

I glanced at the dash. The white dashes in the middle of the road were passing at fifty-five kilometers an hour. They should have been a smear.

I could count them.

One, two, three, a long gap, four. I watched the paint slide under the hood, each dash as distinct as a rail tie, and the thought arrived with an odd, flat calm: _That's wrong._

_It's not wrong. You're tired._

I was tired. I'd slept in the shape of a question mark and then been unconscious on a steel table. I decided that was it, and I looked back at the road.

The heat came up my neck.

It always came the same way: a flush at the base of my skull, then a slow burn climbing the sides of my throat, and under it a deep, crawling ache, as though something was stitching my insides together with hot thread. I clenched my teeth. A bead of sweat ran down my temple and into my collar. My right palm itched like a nettle sting, and when I flexed it on the wheel, the new skin pulled tight and tender.

_It's the repair._ I breathed through it. _It's repairing. That's all it is._

It lasted forty seconds. Then it ebbed, and left me wrung out, with my pulse hammering in my ears.

I looked at my own heartbeat. It was fast. Too fast, a hundred and thirty, maybe more, and it wasn't going to slow down. I could feel it in the heels of my hands, in the hollow of my throat, in the little vein under my left eye.

I turned on the radio.

I had it set to Kestrel's net. For a while, there was only the low, even wash of a working channel. Then a voice I knew very well, a staffer's, flat and procedural, read out a string of numbers I'd have recognized in my sleep.

"—fire mission, grid as briefed, danger-close not requested. Directorate confirms. Time on target, zero-nine-three-zero."

"Copy, time on target zero-nine-three-zero," said another voice.

I turned it off.

It didn't help. I could still hear it. _Zero-nine-three-zero._ I looked at the clock on the dash. 0831.

_Seventy-nine minutes. No. Sixty-nine. Sixty-_

I forced my brain to do the subtraction slowly. _0931 minus 0831 is one hour. But it's 0930. So it's sixty minutes. No, it's fifty-nine. Stop._

My mind kept doing it anyway.

---

The tag receiver sat on the dash, face-up, wedged against the windshield with a roll of cable tape.

I looked at it every thirty seconds. I couldn't stop.

The screen showed one line of text and a number: AN2, then a space where the number should have been. For a long time, there was nothing at all. Then it blinked.

_52._

My breath stopped.

It was a heartbeat. It was her heartbeat, or a number meant to look like one. It was low, too low for someone who'd just fought through a corridor. It might have meant she was sedated, or hurt, or cold. It sat there for two seconds, and then the display went dark, and I realized that I'd braced my entire body against the wheel.

_Is it real?_

I'd never asked that question about a screen before. This morning I'd stared at two heartbeats on a board for an hour, as steady as a metronome, and they'd belonged to the dead. I'd been looking at a recording. I'd sat and watched it and said _steady's good._

I couldn't trust a number. That was what the morning had cost me.

The screen blinked again. _52._ Then darkness. Then, after a longer gap, _49._

It had changed.

The first one had been fixed, a metronome. This one was different. It was lower, then lower, and it wandered by a beat like a living thing. Something clenched in my chest and didn't let go.

_Alive. Maybe. Alive._

I took my eyes off it and put them back on the road.

---

By 0841, the canal had narrowed and the fence had given way to the dead bones of the complex: a gantry crane standing against the sky like a gallows, long rows of sheds with rusted roofs, a skeleton of piping. I knew every foot of it. I had never driven it.

I was suddenly, intensely aware of everything.

That was the only way I could describe it. It wasn't that the world had slowed. The world was exactly the speed it had always been. It was that I'd stopped filtering it. Some old editor in my head, the one that took in a landscape and told me which parts mattered, had gone off shift. Now the edge of every shed roof was sharp. Every crack in the road was a small, separate event. A bird took off from a pipe a hundred meters ahead, and I saw each wing-beat, and then I looked away because it was too much.

I smelled the canal. Not the generic smell of water. I smelled the brine in it, and the diesel sheen along the edge, and under both of them something sweet and dead that must have been a gull.

I smelled the truck. Hot rubber. Old coffee. The dust in the seat.

I smelled myself, and it was frightening: sweat, and under the sweat a sharp, clean smell like a struck match, and under that something that wasn't me at all.

I could hear my own blood.

_Stop it._

I tried to put it away. I tried to make myself small, the way I had when I was a child, until the noise was only noise. I breathed in for four, held for four, out for four, and my pulse didn't care.

_I haven't tested anything._

It landed without any drama. I'd walked out of a lab with a body I didn't know. I didn't know how fast I was, or how hard I could fall, or how long the regeneration would hold. I didn't know whether the hunger would hit again in ten minutes or ten seconds. I'd left a man in a white room who'd said _you're my first_, and I'd driven into the middle of a combat zone with a rifle I hadn't fired in a year and a pulse at a hundred and thirty-five.

_There isn't time._

The thought was very clear. There wasn't. I could stop the truck and jump off a wall. I could stop and sprint down the road and count the seconds. I'd have lost five minutes, and ten, and when I reached the annex there'd be a hole.

I set my jaw and kept my foot down.

My leg jumped.

It came out of nowhere, a hot spasm in my right calf, and the foot on the accelerator went down hard before my brain had finished telling it to. The truck lunged. The engine howled. I hauled the wheel straight and let off, heart pounding, and the truck slewed through a patch of loose gravel and settled in a long, shuddering glide.

I sat there for a moment with my hands locked on the wheel.

_That was me._

I hadn't meant to press that hard. I hadn't pressed hard at all, in my mind. The motion had left my body faster than the intention. It was as if my leg had gotten ahead of me.

_It's coming in pieces,_ the Doctor had said.

_Then I'll have to be careful with the pieces._

I flexed my fingers, one at a time. They obeyed. I eased my foot down gently, as if I were stepping on something alive, and the truck steadied.

0847.

Ahead, the road bent toward the canal, and beyond the bend, I could see the long, pale outline of the rail bridge, with the little gray box of Repeater Five bolted to its girder. A green light blinked on top of it, steady as a pulse. Mine. Something I'd built.

I looked at it for a moment longer than I should have.

Then the back of my neck went cold.

I couldn't have said why. There was nothing on the road. There was no sound but the engine and the tick of the cooling fan and my own breathing, and the wind in the long grass along the ditch. I told myself it was the serum. I told myself the whole day was going to feel like this.

I kept driving.

---
The road curved toward the canal, and the rail bridge came up on my left, long and pale and quiet.

I eased off the gas. There was a stretch of open road ahead, flat and straight for about a hundred and fifty meters, with the old pump station on the left, a squat block of gray concrete with a flat roof and a rusted pipe rack along one edge. The light fell flat on everything. The shadows were short and hard. Past it, the ditch ran down to the water, and beyond the water the road ran on, empty, toward the north end of the complex.

I told myself the cold on my neck was the serum.

It wasn't.

On the roof of the pump station, something moved.

It wasn't much. A shape lifting out of the line of the parapet, a hand's breadth at a time, as though it had been waiting to lift. I noticed it the way you notice a drop of water in a quiet room. Then my eyes fixed on it, and everything else fell away.

A man. Dark clothing. Black visor, gray gaiter, a shape across his shoulder as long as his body and twice as wide as a rifle.

The tube settled.

_It's a launcher._

I didn't think it. The knowledge arrived whole, from somewhere behind my ribs. I'd seen the shape in a briefing a year before, a fat, blunt cylinder with a flared mouth at the back, the sort of thing infantry carried when they needed something that could kill a vehicle. I'd never seen one pointed at me.

**It was pointed at me.**

I watched the muzzle swing the last few degrees and stop. And I understood the thing the Doctor hadn't told me and couldn't have: I understood stillness. Nothing in a fight stops moving by accident. A rifle drifts, a shoulder bobs, a body breathes. The tube had stopped. It had stopped because somebody had decided, and I could see where.

My pulse went through the roof.

It wasn't a feeling. It was an event, a single hammer blow in my chest that I felt in my teeth. Heat flooded my arms. My vision narrowed to a bright tunnel with the man at the end of it. And the sound around me, the engine, the wind, the tick of the fan, went distant and tinny, as though somebody had put a hand over the world.

_Now._

I was already moving.

I didn't decide to. My left hand hit the door latch while my right was still on the wheel, and the door swung out, and the cab opened into bright air. I felt the carbine's sling across my chest, where I'd put it at some point on the drive without noticing. I felt the dash under my knuckles as my hand tore the tag receiver off its tape.

The man on the roof fired.

There was a flat, hard _whump_, and a puff of gray smoke blew back from the rear of the tube. Something dark leapt out of the front.

_It looks slow._

That was the thought. It had no panic in it. It was a flat observation, like a reading from a gauge. The rocket left the tube and came down the road toward me, and I could see it, a blunt gray cone with a stubby fin at the back and a tiny wobble in its flight. It hung in the air like a thrown stone. I watched it cross ten meters and then ten more. A pale flicker lit at the tail as the second stage caught. The cab was still where it had been. The steering wheel was still in front of me. Everything was in its place, and the thing coming toward me wasn't hurrying at all.

_I have time._

It was the first thing the serum told me, and I believed it.

I pushed off the seat.

---

I meant to clear the ditch.

I'd looked at the near bank, three meters of salt grass and loose gravel, and thought _that's far enough._ It was a plan a person could make. I put my weight into the jump the way I'd have put it into any jump in my life, with a flex of the knees and a push of the hips.

My legs went off like a spring.

The cab dropped away. The world swung. I rose, and kept rising, and for one long, silent, astonished moment I was in the air with the ground a long way down, and the near bank went by under me, a blur of green and gray.

_That's too far._

The rocket hit the truck.

There was no sound at first. There was a flash, white and then orange, and a hand of pressure that closed on my back and threw me. I heard the roar a half second after it had already hit me, and by then I was spinning.

The air went out of me. I didn't feel the ground arrive. I felt a series of impacts, shoulder, hip, ribs, shoulder, and a tearing sound from the left side of my chest. Gravel scraped my cheek. My carbine's sling cut under my arm. I hit a concrete footing with my forearm, and I heard it break.

I rolled. I kept rolling. When I stopped, I was lying on my back in a bed of salt grass with the sky going slowly around, and a long, dark column of smoke climbing into it, and something burning that used to be a truck.

_Don't trust the first thing it tells you._

It had told me I had time.

I'd had less than I thought.

---

For a moment, I couldn't tell where I ended.

Everything hurt. It hurt in sheets, in layers. My left forearm lay at a wrong angle across my stomach, and the pain from it was white and total. My ribs on that side burned, and when I breathed in, something grated. There was blood on my face and in my mouth. I tried to lift my head and the sky swam.

Then the other thing began.

It started in my arm. A hot, grinding pull, the kind of feeling you get when a stuck drawer gives. Under the skin of my forearm, something moved. I felt the bone _shift_, a slow, nauseating, wet slide, and then a click that I felt in my teeth. The pain doubled. I made a sound that wasn't a word.

_Don't look._

I looked.

The arm was straightening. It didn't leap into place. It crept, a degree at a time, the way a tide creeps up a beach. The skin where the bone had pressed against it was a deep red, and while I watched it faded to pink, and then to the color of the rest of me. The ache in my ribs went from a grind to a crawl to a burn. Somewhere inside my chest, something else clicked into place.

It took thirty seconds. It felt like thirty minutes.

When it was over, I lay still, breathing, with my hand pressed flat against the dirt, and the ground under it was warm.

I'd broken an arm and cracked at least three ribs. I'd been on the ground for half a minute. And I was lying in the grass, alive, with everything where it belonged, and the only thing wrong with me was an ache like the worst day of my life, fading.

_That's real._

I sat up.

The truck was a shell. It sat in the middle of the road with its roof peeled back and black smoke boiling out of the cab, and the heat of it reached me across forty meters. The tailgate lay on its side in the ditch. There was a long gouge in the gravel where a piece of the chassis had skidded, ending exactly where I'd been sitting twelve seconds earlier.

I looked at the ground at my feet.

A long scar ran through the salt grass, dug out in a line of torn earth. At the near end it started where my boots had hit the far bank of the ditch, and that was nine meters from the road. At the far end, it ended in a hollow where my hip had struck the footing.

I'd been aiming for three.

I stared at the distance. I walked it, with my eyes, from the road to the hollow. _Nine, ten meters._ A person didn't jump ten meters from a standing start. A person didn't jump it out of a truck.

I didn't.

_I did._

Up on the pump station roof, the man with the launcher had lowered the tube.

I couldn't see his face. I could see the shape of him against the sky, standing very still with the empty launcher dangling from one hand, and I could see that he was looking at me. He wasn't looking the way a soldier looks at a target. He was looking the way you'd look at something that had come out of a wall.

He'd fired a rocket at a truck. He'd expected to see a fireball and nothing else.

I got to my feet.

My knee didn't complain. My ribs didn't catch. The tag receiver was in my left hand, so tightly that my fingers had left prints in the plastic, and I looked at its dark little screen and didn't dare turn it over.

_Fifty-two. Forty-nine._

I put it in my vest.

The man on the roof raised a rifle.

I watched his shoulders drop and his weight settle. I felt the old, new thing in my chest tighten, and my eyes found the muzzle without being told.

I wasn't fast enough to be safe.

I was fast enough to be alive. 

*Maybe, I'll be fast enough to save her.*
