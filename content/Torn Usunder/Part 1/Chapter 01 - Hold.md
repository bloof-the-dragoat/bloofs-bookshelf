# Chapter 1: Hold

> “You don’t have to be useful at breakfast.”
> — Mara Graymark

---
The tape goes on in three turns. Palm, knuckles, wrist.

Bloof has done it so many times the motion no longer needs them. The roll is gray and cheap, fraying at the edge, and beneath it, yesterday’s scabs split a little along the knuckles. They flex their fingers until the wrap sits right and let the sting be somebody else’s problem.

On the parapet, the phone reads 4:52.

Cold finds the gap between collar and neck and settles there. Frost beads along the ridges of their horns, and the steel stud in one ear aches with it. The rooftop tar is silver with frost, and the rail bites through their sleeves.

Four floors down, the street hasn’t woken yet. Along the east, a thin gray seam sits where the buildings stop. Across the gap, a mana-fiber sign idles through its overnight loop, pale blue letters with nothing to say.

A canvas bag hangs from a beam on a length of chain, ticking against its hook in the wind. A backpack slumps against the stairwell door, forgotten until it’s needed.

Bloof sets their feet. A long breath out. It fogs, then tears away. Their tail, gray and marked with dark teardrops along its curve, draws in against the back of one leg and goes still. Then, they move.

A low sweep, heel driving through frost. The momentum becomes a pivot, the pivot an imaginary trapped wrist, a hip-throw that never lands. The bag swings out of the dark. Bloof isn’t there when it swings back: half-step, turn of the hip, a forearm laid along the line of the swing, the way a hand finds a banister. The weight slides past on a long creak of chain. Their boots leave dark crescents on the white tar.

The right shoulder catches on the third pass. A hitch, a hot wire pulled tight from neck to blade. They finish the turn anyway, shortening the next one by an inch so it won’t show.

Between one form and the next, heat gathers in their palms, a low orange pulse beneath the tape. Their hands close. It is gone, the way a wick is pinched out.

A thread of singed cloth drifts up and fades.

Fire is easy to stop. Water does it. Ice does it. Wind, packed earth, a sealed door, a patient opponent who knows the right trick. Anything Bloof can burn, someone has already learned to put out.

So the rest has to be better. The feet. The hips. The timing. Everything that doesn’t need a flame to work.

On the parapet, the phone’s alarm chimes. Once. Twice.

Bloof does not look. They set up for the next form.

Palm. Knuckle. Wrist. Pivot. Set. Sweep. Pivot. Step. Turn. Strike.

Repeat.

The bag jumps on its chain with every hit, and every hit lands in the same hand-wide patch of canvas, worn pale as a thumbprint. Bloof’s weight rolls from heel to hip to shoulder to fist in a single line, with nothing left over. Only the shoulder complains, a bright catch at the end of each swing, and Bloof lets it.

The smell reaches them first: burnt cloth, sharp and a little sweet.

Under the tape, the orange has crept back. Bloof’s fist opens, and the glow dies as quickly as it came.

Five more strikes. Then a sixth. The alarm starts again.

Bloof finally picks up the phone.

**5:40.**

The alarm dies under their thumb.

They peel back a corner of the tape. It is scorched through at the palm, and under the frayed edge the knuckles have split again, dark and shining. A fresh wrap would take two minutes. They press the curled edge flat with a thumb and leave it.

The screen goes dark. For a moment it holds their reflection: black lower eyes with pale slit pupils, red crescents curved beneath them. A purple star sits between the brows. Above the lower eyes, a second pair of lids rests shut, as if half the face were asleep.

Bloof tilts the phone. The reflection slips away.

They take the jacket from the parapet, black and heavy. Spiked bands go over the tape on their wrists. A collar studded with steel goes around their throat, buckled by feel, its spikes cold to the touch. Then the jacket settles over everything, chains clinking. They drag the cuffs down over the bands and the old scars climbing their forearms, one sleeve, then the other, until nothing shows but their taped hands and the tips of their claws: silver, traced in black filigree.

They lift the backpack onto the left shoulder, turn toward the stairwell door, and tuck both hands into their sleeves.

Behind them, the bag keeps swinging, ticking against its hook.

---

Bloof leaves the roof and takes the stairs down to the third floor, one hand trailing the rail. The cold on their clothes is already fading, though the smell of singed cloth remains.

The door rune gives its soft chime under their palm, a clean note now, with none of the flat buzz it had last week. Bloof spent an hour on the coupling at two this morning and has not mentioned it. Inside, the unit is warm and smells of chamomile. Mara’s boots sit by the door, still damp, and her medical bag hangs on its hook with the strap twisted from being thrown there. Bloof steps around the boots out of habit and drops their backpack beside them.

Mara is already at the door when it closes, back half-turned, two fingers on the rune. She waits for the lock to settle, then moves on to the supply shelf, counting under her breath. She is tall and broad-shouldered, with the corded forearms of someone who lifts beastfolk for a living, her dusk-blue scrubs rumpled, sleeves shoved to the elbow. Cream fur shades to dove-gray across her back, a dark stripe running down her spine and tail. The tail hangs low. The rest of her is upright and brisk.

“There’s my early bird,” she says, without turning. “Suspicious.”

“I’m always up early.”

“Mm. That’s what makes it suspicious.”

Her ears swing toward them, and her nose twitches, just once. Her mouth tightens. She turns around with a warm, tired smile already on her face. Her eyes are amber, ringed with gray fur and shadowed from the double.

Bloof sits on the backless stool by the window. Their sleeves stay down, with only the taped hands showing at the cuffs. Their tail hangs loose behind the stool. They keep it still.

The light comes in low and pale across the counter, over an envelope lying by the glass. The corner reads “Association Assessment” in a clean, official font. Bloof’s eyes touch it and move on.

“Hands flat—” Mara rubs an eye. “Let me see, sweetheart.”

Bloof doesn’t answer. Mara crosses the kitchen, opens the freezer, and takes a flat pack of ice from the stack. She holds it in both hands for a moment, and a thin bloom of pale blue frost spreads across the cream fur of her forearm before it fades. She presses the pack into Bloof’s right palm and closes their taped fingers around it with her own.

The cold goes through the tape and hits the split skin. For a moment, it burns.

Mara looks at the cuff, then at Bloof’s face, and says nothing about either.

“Eat something,” she says instead. “Please? For me?”

“You eat. You just got in.”

“I’m eating.” Mara isn’t. She takes the seat across from them and rests her chin on her fist. The yawn comes out sideways. “Double shift. Somebody’s grandmother and a lot of stairs. Don’t ask.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good, because I’d cry. Drink your tea.”

Bloof reaches for the mug and finds it already on their left side, where Mara must have slid it while they weren’t looking. Their right shoulder pulls at the thought of reaching across, so they use the left. Mara watches the steam and not their arm.

The envelope sits in the light. Mara glances at it and then at Bloof, who meets her eyes for less than a second.

“That came yesterday,” she says. “It’s handled.”

“The assessment’s due on the first.”

“It’s handled, Bloof.”

“I put in the transfer.” It comes out too fast. Bloof realizes it too late. “It’s from the internship. They pay on time.”

Their left ear dips, just a little, and they feel it go.

Mara’s eyes stay on Bloof’s face. The smile she gives them is the one she uses on patients and on kitchen appliances.

“Aetherion pays on time, I know,” she says. “I’m not asking.”

She reaches across the counter and takes their left hand from around the mug, taped knuckles and all, and holds it a second.

“The bills are not your worry, sweetheart. That’s mine. You carry enough.”

“I can—”

Mara raises her other hand. It comes up slowly, where Bloof can see it, and stops a few inches from their horns.

“Incoming,” she says.

Bloof is already tilting their head. Her palm settles on top and moves once through the hair behind the star on their forehead. Their shoulders come down, the jacket chains sliding with them, and their lower eyes drift half shut before they remember to stop them.

The tail behind the stool wants to move. It wants to so badly. Bloof holds it flat against the floor and counts the tiles.

Mara’s ear flicks. A smile crosses her face and is gone.

“Careful,” she says. “You’ll hurt yourself being that stoic.”

“You really should eat,” Bloof says, mostly to the table. Their face feels hot.

“Yes, yes. Bossy.”

Mara gets up for the bread. Bloof drinks their tea. When she turns from the counter, her eyes land on the silver claw tip showing beside the softening ice pack.

“Is that one chipped?”

“Yeah. Right, ring.”

“Redoing them tonight?”

“After class, probably.”

“Long week?” she asks. “You’ve been slamming doors.”

“Exam. Big one.”

The tip of their tail presses against the stool leg. Mara glances down, then lets it go.

“Do the dark blue,” she says. “With the little lines. The ones from game night. Those were gorgeous.”

“Those took two hours.”

“They looked like it. I told everyone you did them. Everyone.”

“You told them?”

“I showed Teller at the station. He asked if you do commissions.”

Bloof’s mouth lifts at the corner before they can stop it. Behind them, their tail thumps once against the stool leg, then again, and Bloof clamps down on it a second too late.

Mara presses her lips together and makes a very elaborate show of buttering toast, the dark stripe on her tail giving one pleased swish before she catches it.

“I didn’t say anything,” she says to the toast.

“You’re doing it with your face.”

“My face is innocent.”

“Your face is so loud.”

“There it is,” Mara says softly, and doesn’t look up.

Bloof returns the ice pack to the freezer and collects their backpack. Mara takes a bite of toast before following them to the door. A cloth-wrapped lunch box hangs from the hook beside it.

She lifts it free and holds it out. “You’re thin.”

“I’m not.”

“Take it, kiddo.”

Bloof takes it. The warmth rests against their palms.

On the wall beside the hook hangs a photograph, slightly crooked. Mara stands in it, younger and cream-furred, with her arm around a child, smiling at the camera. The child stands next to her with their hands at their sides. Their eyes are wide and still.

A memory brushes past: a cool hand on their forehead during a fever.

Mara straightens the frame.

“I’m proud of you,” she says, in the same voice she used for the toast. “Go.”

“You should sleep.”

“I will. Eat the lunch.”

Bloof slides the box into the backpack and shoulders it. At the door, Mara says it once more, plainly.

“I love you.”

She has said it a thousand times, and it still turns Bloof’s face hot. They look at the floor.

“Sleep,” Bloof says. “Please… Mom.”

Mara goes still for half a second. Then she smiles, soft, like she was handed something she won’t say she noticed.

Bloof’s hands are already fists inside their sleeves. They close the door before the heat reaches their face.

---

The street has woken up since the roof.

By the time Bloof reaches the Marrow Street platform, the awnings are shedding frost. Their hands stay in their sleeves, backpack riding high on the left shoulder. The right one stays loose.

A noodle cart is unfolding itself at the corner, and its vendor props a resonance-crystal heater at her feet. The crystal glows a low amber and ticks as it warms. The beastkin in line drift toward it without seeming to.

Overhead, the mana-fiber signs have switched from their overnight loops to the morning ones. Pale blue letters give way to bright ones, advertising tram delays, tuition loans, and a broth that is “now with real marrow.” Beneath one of them, someone has tied a strip of red cloth around the lamppost, the kind beastfolk leave for the Beast god’s blessing on a long commute. Bloof’s eyes touch it, and they keep walking.

At the gate, a magi-tech turnstile hums against the leyline current. An attendant sits in a glass booth beside it, thumbing through a queue.

The boar ahead of Bloof, thick-necked and wearing a delivery vest, taps his card, gets a green chime, and is through in under a second.

Bloof taps theirs.

The card reader holds. One second. Two. The light on the turnstile runs a slow amber loop instead of flashing green, and Bloof keeps their face easy.

From their angle at the glass, the attendant’s screen is just visible.

**5-D · PROV.**

A line of smaller text sits beneath it: *Pending review.* The attendant’s eyes move over it without any particular interest, and then the light goes green and the bar clicks open.

“Go ahead,” the attendant says, already looking at the next card.

The tram slides in on a hum Bloof feels in their back teeth, the leyline rails singing under it. The car is half full. Bloof takes a standing spot beside the doors, one shoulder against the pole.

Two seats down, an office worker glances at the horns, the collar, the chains, and the tail, then at the horns again.

The commuter looks back at the phone. Bloof looks out the window.

A child on her father’s lap watches them over his shoulder, chin on his collar and eyes wide and unblinking. When the tram jolts, he tucks her closer and turns his shoulder to the aisle, gently, as though he were only getting comfortable.

Above the doors, the news ticker scrolls between ads:

*MINOR LEYLINE FLUCTUATION REPORTED IN EASTERN GRID · SERVICE UNAFFECTED · AUTHORITIES CITE ROUTINE VARIANCE.*

A commuter reads it, shrugs, and goes back to his coffee. The lights don’t so much as flicker.

Under Bloof’s left cuff, the tape has stuck itself to the split skin again. They leave it.

At the next stop, the car fills. A shopping bag brushes their knee. Someone asks to get past, and Bloof shifts closer to the doors.

They take the earbuds from their jacket pocket and put them in.

They don’t start any audio.

The tram hums on.

---

Concordia reveals itself in layers on the walk up from the tram stop. The Concord’s temple towers come first, pale spires catching the sun. Below them, the leyline lamps along the avenue are still lit, burning a soft white against the daylight. Then the street bends, and Alhathim Professional Arts rises on its terraces.

It is built of honey-colored stone that darkens where the rain finds it, with copper roofs gone green and high arched windows that throw long bars of light across the walks. Mana-fiber banners hang between the lecture halls, brightening and dimming as the current moves through them. Flowering trees line the main quad, their petals drifting down onto the paths in slow pink spirals.

At the center, a fountain carries a Beast god’s carved face. Water pours from the open mouth, and a ring of resonance crystals set into the basin keeps it warm enough that the early frost never touches it.

Students cross the grass in clusters. Law students argue over a shared tablet. Medics in practice scrubs carry crates of splint stock. A pair of combat-sciences students warm up by the bell tower, one hoisting a stone bench while the other times the hold. Somewhere past the fountain, a recruiter in a stiff gray coat is setting up a table beneath a banner for the spring placement fair.

Bloof slows at the edge of the quad. Beyond the fountain, students hurry between lectures with tablets under their arms. Here, at least, Bloof can be late for class like anyone else.

“Bloof!”

The shout comes from across the quad, delighted enough that three students turn to look. Bloof knows the voice before they find him. They pull out the earbuds and pocket them.

Pip is already running. He is a blur of warm yellow and faded green, with his satchel bouncing against his hip and one long cream ear flopping back with each stride. He skids to a stop an arm’s length away, grinning so wide it looks like it hurts. His ears are straight up.

“Hi! Hi, you’re here, good morning. How are you? How was the week? Did you finish the relay diagnostics? I finished mine. Mine caught fire a little. Not a lot.”

“A little,” Bloof says.

“A little!” Pip agrees, beaming like it’s good news.

Bloof falls into step beside him. Their hands loosen inside their sleeves.

“I’m fine,” Bloof says. “Tired. Exam.”

“Oh, the big one, right. Wait.” Pip stops. His ears go still, then flick forward. “Bloof. Did you eat?”

Bloof opens their mouth to answer and finds there is no answer to give. Tea. They drank the tea.

“I, uh, forgot.”

“You *forgot*?”

“There was a lot going on. I had my lunch—”

“That’s lunch.” Pip is already digging in the satchel. He comes out with a wrapped bundle, PERRIN scrawled across a bakery sticker in blocky marker, and peels back the paper to hold up half a croissant sandwich. “I got two. I always get two. Ah.”

“Pip—”

The sandwich meets Bloof’s muzzle before they finish the name. Their lower eyes go wide. The croissant shatters, buttery and warm, and then the rest arrives all at once: crisp smokebark, a soft egg, melted pepper jack, and the slow, smoky heat of chipotle mayo behind it.

It is the sandwich. It is *the* sandwich.

Bloof takes it from him. Another bite. Then a third. It is entirely possible that the sound they make is not a word.

When they come back to themself, Pip is watching with his hands clasped at his chest. His ears lean forward, and his small tail flicks behind him.

Bloof swallows. Their face feels warm.

*Oh, you ridiculous hare.*

They sigh, long and put-upon, and lift their left hand to pat him on the head, once, between the ears.

Pip’s ears flatten into the touch and his whole face lights up.

“You know I *have* to do that if you get me this,” Bloof says.

“That’s the plan,” Pip says, with total sincerity.

They continue along the north walk. Pip talks, and Bloof listens. The tram was late, the greenhouse sprinklers flooded a bench, someone’s study-group snack stash is missing, and it is entirely possible that it was Pip.

Halfway across the quad, his eyes drop to Bloof’s hands. The tape shows at the wrist, scorched and gray and frayed.

His ears go still.

“Bloof,” he says, quieter. “Your hands.”

“It looks worse than it is.”

“It looks like it hurts.”

“It’s training.” Bloof draws the cuff down. “I’m fine, Pip.”

Pip opens his mouth, then closes it.

“I’ve got salve,” he says. “If you want some. Just say.”

“I know.”

“Okay.”

At the fork by the science wing, he slows. Past the glass, a long greenhouse shows rows of misted benches under rune-lit panels.

“That’s me. Soil lab, then my minor section this afternoon.” He shifts his satchel. “I’m going to make your crystals clean up a swamp one day.”

“You’re welcome to them.”

“I’ll hold you to that!” His ears lift again. “Sparring’s after lunch, right? I’m going to get thrown into a wall.”

“Guess I’ll see you at sparring,” Bloof says.

“You say that like it’s reassuring.”

“It is.”

Pip laughs, bright and startled, and jogs backward two steps before he turns.

“Eat the *rest* of your croissant!”

Bloof watches him go, still tasting chipotle. Then they check the time, settle the strap on their left shoulder, and head for the Magi-Technical Engineering building.

---

The hardware lecture hall is a steep bowl of curved benches around a rune board the size of a wall. It smells of flux and warm copper, and the smell never fully leaves. Bloof takes a seat on the aisle, three rows from the top, where the exit is close and no one has to climb past them. They prop the slate so the taped hand barely shows at its edge.

At the front, the professor has set a palm-sized assembly on the demonstration bench: a clouded crystal seated in a brass cradle, with a ribbed vent plate bolted to one side.

“Absorption crystal,” she says. “Common in medical and workshop field kits. It takes in mana from a compatible source, holds it, and gives it back on request.”

She taps the plate.

“Here is a problem from a real recall. A medic’s absorber keeps cracking its crystal. The stored charge never passes sixty percent of capacity, and the crystal itself checks clean. What else do you test?”

Down the benches, guesses come in. A damaged socket. The regulator. The wrong housing material. The professor writes each on the board.

Bloof has been looking at the vent plate.

“The intake rate,” they say.

Heads turn. Bloof keeps their eyes on the assembly.

“Go on.”

“It heats up while it charges. If the vent can handle a steady intake but not a surge, the heat builds faster than it can shed it. The crystal could crack long before it’s full.”

“And the fix?”

“Throttle the intake as the temperature climbs. Let it take less when it’s hot, instead of finding out where it breaks.”

“Rate, not capacity,” the professor says, writing it on the board. “Copy that down. It will be on the exam.”

Bloof is already looking at their slate again. Kell, the otter beside them, leans over.

“That’s twice this term you’ve saved my lab grade,” she whispers.

“You still have to do the lab.”

“Fine. Coffee’s on me.”

“Tea.”

Kell grins. One corner of Bloof’s mouth lifts before they look back at the slate.

Then the lamps stutter. The rune board gutters, the crystal on the bench pales and steadies, and the floor’s low hum drops a note and returns. A murmur runs along the benches.

“Routine maintenance on the eastern grid,” the professor says, without turning. “Carry on.”

Bloof watches the nearest lamp until the light holds. One breath. Two.

Then they go back to the diagram.

---

By lunchtime, the commons is loud with trays and talk. Bloof sits at the end of a long table with Kell and a few others from the lecture and unwraps Mara’s cloth.

The box is no longer warm. Inside are pressed rice, glazed roots, a square of folded egg, and a slip of paper tucked under the lid.

*Vegetables are not optional.*

Bloof folds the note twice and puts it in a pocket.

Under the table, their thumb finds the tape on the right hand and presses the curled edge flat. The split beneath it complains. They draw the box closer with their left hand and begin to eat.

The table is arguing about the board.

Across the table, Tamsin, a goat from the lecture, looks up from a tablet.

“Fenmarsh jumped to thirty-first. Thirty-first! She’ll be top twenty by spring.”

“It’s an unofficial list,” Kell says. “The kid who updates it likes her.”

“Her mist is awful for fire users, though. A pyro kid last term couldn’t light a candle in it.”

“That’s what the mist is *for*.”

Tamsin looks over at Bloof. “You’re what, fiftieth? Sixtieth?”

“Something like that.”

*Fifty-seven. Not that I’m counting.*

The argument moves on, loud and cheerful and entirely unconcerned with whether Bloof joins. They listen to it the way they’d listen to rain, and eat.

Their phone buzzes against their thigh. Under the table edge, the screen shows Nyra’s name and nothing beneath it. Bloof silences the notification and turns the phone face down on their knee.

It can wait until after combat class.

The lunch is gone faster than they intended. Bloof wipes the box, folds Mara’s cloth around it, and slides it into the backpack. They check the time. They close the taped hand, open it, and roll the right shoulder once, slowly, until the catch comes.

The same catch from the roof before dawn.

---

The training hall sits at the end of a long colonnade, its doors propped open to the draft. Beyond them, the sound of a floor being cleared carries out into the afternoon.

At the threshold, Bloof lifts the bag clear of their hip. The movement pulls at the right shoulder. They lower it carefully and step inside.

The mats are half cleared. Students line the wall in clusters, and the air smells of rubber and old sweat. Orlan stands at the center with a slate, calling pairs in a flat, unhurried voice.

“Callum Vance. Doran Ashwick.”

A ripple of low talk.

“Aldous Merrick. Sera Reyes.”

Bloof finds a spot near the wall and sets the bag down. Their thumb works beneath a curled edge of tape.

“Bloovrn Graymark. Isolde Fenmarsh. First match.”

Bloof’s thumb stops.

Across the mats, Isolde looks up.

Thirty-first. Mist.

The conversation nearest Bloof thins. Isolde sets her water bottle beside her bag and straightens, her gaze finding theirs across the room.

Bloof presses the loose edge of the tape flat.

Fire is easy to stop.

They have been awake since before dawn working on the rest.
