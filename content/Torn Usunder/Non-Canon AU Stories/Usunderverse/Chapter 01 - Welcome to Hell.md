# Chapter 1 — Welcome to Hell

The mattress was soft, and that alone felt like a luxury.

Every part of Bloof hurt in its own distinct way. The ribs, where the gravity user had pressed the whole world down on them until something inside gave a wet, quiet crack. The forearm, wrapped in clean gauze and still throbbing underneath. The long ache down the spine that came from running **Overdrive** too hot for too long. The wounds were closing, though. They could feel the slow, itchy pull of the body doing its work.

They had not won. They had only gotten out. A high-level gravity user, a room that weighed four times what it should, and a retreat that was really a crawl. Seris had dragged them the last stretch.

_Rest,_ she’d said. Bazemir had said it, too, in the heavier, gentler way he had of saying things that were not up for debate.

Seris had stayed awake until their breathing eased. Now she slept beside them, curled on her side, one hand loose on the blanket between them, the way she only did when she thought nobody was watching.

_Just one minute,_ Bloof thought. _Then I’ll get up and check the door._

They closed their eyes.

The dark behind their eyelids went red.

It wasn’t a dream, not quite. No floor, no wall, no sky: only a void the color of the inside of a closed fist, warm and humming. Bloof hung in it without weight. They tried to move and couldn’t. They tried to breathe and found they already were, slow and deep, in time with something else.

Then something closed around them.

A hand. It had to be a hand. Enormous, the span of it wider than any room they’d ever stood in, every finger wrapped around some part of them at once. Not crushing. Not cruel. If it had been cruel, they would have known what to do with it.

It just _held_. Like something that had been reaching for a very long time and had at last caught what it wanted, and didn’t know its own strength.

Bloof opened their mouth to say _let go._

The hand pulled.

---

They woke on the ground.

The first thing wrong was the heat. The ground was warm, a living, uneven warmth, like a sleeping animal’s flank. The second was the missing warmth of Seris beside them.

Their eyes were open before they decided to open them. No lights they knew, no sky they knew, only a smear of low red where the horizon should be. The air tasted of scorched sugar and, underneath, something that had been dead a while.

For three full breaths, Bloof didn’t move.

Then, because it was what they knew how to do, they started working.

Inventory. Left forearm: torn open again by the landing, weeping through the gauze. Ribs on the right: bruised or cracked; breathing would settle it. Hands: shaking. Legs: working. Head: ringing.

_Overdrive does this. Don’t read anything into the hands._

Equipment next. Phone and tools were still in their pockets. Then they reached inward, checking the **Stillhold**: **Kaleidos**, **Vein and Marrow**, everything where they’d left it.

They let out a careful breath. Whatever had brought them here hadn’t left them empty-handed.

Exits: a narrow lane, brick on both sides, blocked at one end by a dark mound of debris. Open at the other, where the light was.

Light: red from above, neon flickering from somewhere ahead. Sound: a crowd. Distant music, none of it in the same key. Something that might have been screaming and might have been laughter.

They pinched a thread of **Dragonfire** between thumb and forefinger to see whether it would answer. It came at once, a little too easily, a clean bright bead of heat. But the air around it tasted of iron, thick and steeped, as if the mana had been left to stew. Their upper eyes, kept shut, ached with the effort of not opening onto it.

They killed the spark.

The phone lit when they pulled it free. Seris first. The call screen sat there and did nothing.

“Seris,” they said to the screen, quietly. “It’s me. Pick up. Come on.”

Nothing. They tried again. Then Bazemir. Nothing.

“Come on,” they said again, and that one cracked down the middle. They shut their mouth hard.

Mara’s name sat one swipe above Bazemir’s.

Their thumb stopped on it and stayed a moment too long. Then it moved on.

**Bridle** first. In through the nose, hold, out through the teeth. The old count, the one that had held them together for most of their life. The breath obeyed. The heat under their sternum didn’t; it sat there, warm and awake, not angry, just not settling the way it should after a fight.

The memory of the hand pressed against the inside of their chest.

They tried again. Same result.

_Shit. Where the FUCK am I! I was finally safe. I JUST—_

“No. Shut it out. Not now.”

Food. Water. Somewhere to sit that wasn’t a wall. Then they could think about the rest of it.

They limped toward the light.

A sign hung at the mouth of the lane, half its letters dead, buzzing a sickly pink over the street.

**WELCOME TO IMP CITY — SECOND CIRCLE**

Under it, someone had sprayed a word in red paint that Bloof was fairly sure was a slur, and below that, a drawing so anatomically specific that they looked away on reflex.

Then the street hit them all at once.

Sound first: a speaker on a pole shrieking an advert for a discount blood-orange bar, then cutting out mid-word. Music from three directions. Someone shouting, someone laughing, something wet dragging across stone.

Bloof stopped at the mouth of the lane.

Small, horned figures crowded the pavement, red skin and striped horns and tails flicking between passing legs. A towering shape ducked beneath an awning, its mouth opening sideways to answer someone Bloof couldn’t see. Farther down, a face turned toward them with too many eyes.

They searched for something familiar. Ears. Scales. A muzzle they could place. Each time they thought they recognized a feature, the rest of the creature came into view and ruined it.

_These aren’t beastfolk. There’s nothing like this in Concordia._

A cart rattled past close enough to brush their trousers, and Bloof recoiled before they could stop themself. The wheels looked carved from bone. Something caught between the crates scraped a dark trail over the stones.

“_Move your fucking tail._”

They pulled it in. The driver was already looking elsewhere.

Heat pressed beneath their collar. Their stomach turned, and for one disorienting moment the buildings seemed to lean over the street, all those lit windows and open mouths and signs they could read but couldn’t make sense of. Bloof looked back into the lane.

Empty. A wall. The place they’d woken.

No way back.

Their hand had drifted toward their equipment. They caught it there and forced the fingers open.

They knew how to cross a dangerous street. Watch the hands. Leave room to move. Don’t stare. Simple things, things they had done hurt, exhausted, terrified.

Their feet wouldn’t move.

_Seris, I don’t know what to do._

Someone shouldered past them. Pain flashed through their ribs, sharp enough to bring the pavement back into focus.

Bloof took one step. Then another, before they could stop again.

They kept close to the wall, shoulders held level by an effort that made their whole body ache, hands empty and visible at their sides. Their face settled into the expression they knew how to wear. Their eyes kept hunting the crowd for anything they recognized.

It took only a block for the staring to start.

“The fuck are _you_ supposed to be?”

An imp leaning in a doorway, bottle in hand, gave their horns a slow up-and-down. Bloof kept walking.

“Hey. Fire toad. I’m talking to you.”

They stepped around a cart blocking half the lane. Behind them, someone laughed, and someone else asked whether the horns came off and what it would cost to find out, then added a specific suggestion. Bloof’s ears flattened. Their tail snapped once against the back of their ankle before they pulled it tight.

The attention thickened as they went. A pair of demons in sharp suits broke off their conversation to watch them pass. A hellhound the size of a pony lifted its head from a gutter and sniffed. Someone whispered _dragon_, and the word skipped down the street like a coin across water. Another voice: _What’s with its fucking ears?_ None of it helpful. All of it unwanted.

The street opened onto a square where two demons in expensive coats were fucking against a fountain as if it were a hobby, and not one passerby slowed. On the far side, a demon with a split lip knelt on the pavement, arms raised to shield his head while a larger one swung a metal pipe at him. The crowd steered around the pair like a puddle.

Bloof’s foot moved. One step.

_Don’t. You’re hurt, you’re running on nothing, and you have no idea how strong that thing is. If you step in and go down, who helps either of you?_

They forced themself to stop. The pipe struck again. Bloof flinched, then made themself keep walking.

What turned their stomach wasn’t the violence so much as the boredom. Not one face in the square changed.

“Well, aren’t you a fucking _treat_.”

An imp stepped in beside them, close enough that his sleeve brushed their arm. Bloof shifted away. He followed, looking them over with an interest that made their skin tighten beneath their clothes.

“Those horns real?” His gaze dropped. “What else you got going on?”

Bloof stared at him half a second too long.

“I’m not interested.”

“You don’t even know what I’m offering.” He leaned closer, liquor and sweet cologne crowding out the smoky air. “Got a room right upstairs. Come on. Bet I could get that tail moving.”

“No. Thank you.”

They tried to pass. His hand settled on their hip.

Bloof recoiled so sharply that pain caught beneath their ribs. Their tail drew tight against their leg, and one hand closed over his wrist before they quite registered moving.

“_Take your hand off me._”

Their grip shook. They hated that he could feel it.

For a moment his grin faltered. He pulled free when they released him and raised both hands.

“Pit’s sake. Could’ve just said no.”

“_I did._”

Bloof walked before he could answer, gaze ahead, hands at their sides, though every brush of the crowd now made their shoulders tense.

_Seris would have heard me the first time._

Across the square, a faded poster peeled from a wall: a red imp with a wide, jagged grin and the expression of someone about to say something unforgivable. The ink had bled to pink. Someone had scratched an eye out.

Bloof fixed on its curling lower edge until they reached the curb.

There, a thin demon with a tray of skewers looked up, saw the horns, saw the scales, saw whatever it was in Bloof’s face, and abruptly stepped back with both hands open.

“Sorry. Apologies, I didn’t realize. Go ahead.”

“Go ahead with _what_?”

It came out too sharp, too loud. Heads turned.

The thin demon flinched. Bloof clamped their jaw shut so hard it hurt, set their eyes on the middle distance, and kept walking, face blank, shoulders square, while everything under their ribs shook.

Whatever they’d just been mistaken for, they hadn’t chosen it. And they had no idea how to make it stop.

They had been walking a long while before the smell of the stall reached them, ahead of the stall itself. Fried something. Bread. Meat, probably, though Bloof had stopped asking what kind.

Their stomach clenched so hard they had to stop and put a hand on a lamppost.

The vendor was a thick-armed imp with a ledger the size of a door. He looked at Bloof’s horns, then at their face, and his whole manner shifted from boredom to something oilier.

“Well. Aren’t _you_ a long way from the good districts. What’ll it be, my lord?”

_MY LORD?_

They chose not to comment. “Food.” Bloof pulled a crumpled fistful of bills from their jacket. “I can pay.”

The vendor looked at the money like it was a joke. “It’s the wrong green.”

“It’s cash.”

“It’s _paper._” He held up a single acid-green note from his own till, stamped with a smirking cartoon face and the number 100. “This is souls. That’s a picture of a dead president.”

Bloof set the bills down and tried again. “I can fix something. That register’s shorting out. Give me ten minutes and some bread.”

“I don’t want my register fixed.”

“A battery, then. A coupler. I’ve got components that are worth—”

“I don’t want your _trinkets,_ sweetheart.” The vendor leaned on the counter. His smile showed too many teeth. “Someone with your horns doesn’t pay in paper. Someone with your horns pays in a _favor._ Nothing heavy. Small. I’ll tell you when I need it.”

Bloof looked at the hand extended across the counter. Open palm. No paper, no seal, no limit.

They knew that shape. They had grown up around its cousins: terms nobody wrote down, debts nobody could measure. It wasn’t a deal. It was a hook.

“No.”

“You’ll be hungry again by dark.”

“I know I’ll be hungry again by dark.” Their voice had climbed without permission. “I’m not signing something I can’t read, I’m not—I’m not giving you an open favor, okay? I’ll deal with it.”

The last four words wore thin. They heard it. The vendor heard it.

The vendor’s smile didn’t change. It only got patient, which was worse.

Bloof turned. Four steps later they felt it: the quiet change in the crowd behind them, the pull of attention sliding off the food and onto the dark stain spreading down their sleeve.

They took a left. Then a right. The footsteps took the same turns.

Three of them, when Bloof risked a glance in a dark shop window. Big imps, not in a hurry, matching their pace with the lazy confidence of hunters who had all the time in the world. A wounded stranger. No retinue. No house. Nobody to ask questions afterward.

_Great. Hungry and followed. Maybe it’s a theme._

They cut through an alley, then a courtyard, then a gate that opened onto a street where the neon dimmed and the pavement turned from cracked to swept. The buildings here were older, taller, hedged with iron fences. Somewhere ahead, a clock tolled something that sounded like an hour.

Behind them, the footsteps slowed. Stopped at the corner.

Bloof didn’t look back. They kept their eyes on the road ahead, where a long wall ran the length of the block and a narrow service gate sat in it, and where, at last, the smell of bread came wafting over the stones like an apology.

---

The service gate opened beneath their hand, and the garden behind the wall was a surprise.

Rows of pots lined the path, each one bearing something green and wicked: pitcher plants as tall as a child, snapping blooms with jagged lip-teeth, a vine that curled lazily around its trellis like a sleeping snake. One flower, bloated and glistening, tilted its head as Bloof passed.

Bloof froze.

The flower opened its mouth, wide and wet, and held it there.

Bloof held their breath. Two seconds. Three. Then the flower closed with a soft, almost polite click and turned back to its trellis, and Bloof realized the whole garden had been watching them cross the path, and had decided, for reasons of its own, not to do anything about it.

_The house knows._

The path ended at a lit kitchen door. Bloof stopped there and listened. Nothing. They tried the handle. Unlocked.

The kitchen was warm, clean, and almost silent. Copper pans hung in a gleaming row. Bloof checked every corner before they let themself look at the food: the pantry, the cold room, the door to the hall. No staff, no guards, no sound but the soft tick of the stove.

A pot simmered on the back burner, steam smelling of onions and pepper, and on the counter, under a clean cloth, sat the source of the bread smell: a round loaf, still faintly warm. Bloof lifted the cloth and took the loaf. One. It was heavy and warm, and their hands didn’t want to stop at one.

They set a small magi-tech coupler on the counter, the good one, the one that had taken weeks to build.

“That’s for it,” they said to the empty kitchen, quietly, absurdly. “I’m sorry.”

Magic closed around their wrist like a hand.

Bloof went still.

The bread slid from their fingers. Pressure wrapped up their forearm, soft but absolute, and folded them gently over the counter with a force they could have fought if they’d had anything left to fight with.

They didn’t even try.

From where they lay pinned, they could see the cloth, the dent in the loaf, the coupler. Nothing else.

“I’m sorry. I’ll put it back. I’ll leave.”

“You took one loaf,” said a voice from the hall door behind them. “You left something that is clearly worth considerably more than a loaf. And you apologized to an empty room.”

“It wasn’t empty.”

“No. But you did not know that.”

The pressure on Bloof’s wrist eased, then let go. They didn’t move.

The demon in the doorway was tall and narrow and obviously unworried. Four red eyes. A heart-shaped white face. A small black beak. Feathers the gray-blue of a winter dusk. He wore a dressing gown that had seen better years, and his gloved hand still glowed faintly at the fingertips, the way a coal glows after the fire’s gone out.

An owl. A very large one. Looking at Bloof the way a doctor looks at a chart.

“You have kept your claws off me,” he said. “Even when I caught your wrist. And you left something beside the bread before you knew I was here.” His gaze returned to the component on the counter. “That tells me rather more than the apology.”

“I wasn’t going to hurt anyone.” It sounded like begging. Bloof shut their mouth.

“I believe you.” The red eyes flicked to the door. “The garden alerted me when you crossed the path. I thought it best to see what you wanted before allowing it any opinions.”

Bloof stared at him.

“That wound is not recent enough to ignore. Sit, please. Not because I am ordering it. Because you are visibly about to fall.”

“I’m fine. I can stand. I’m—”

Their knee buckled. The chair appeared under them as if it had always been there.

“I took your food,” Bloof said.

“You took _a_ food. Sit.”

Bloof sat. They put their hands under their thighs so he wouldn’t see them shake.

“May I ask your house?”

“I don’t have one.”

A pause. Then, mildly: “That is not the answer I expected. It is an interesting one.”

He crossed to the stove and began taking things out of the cold cupboard as though it were any ordinary evening. A chipped mug hung on a hook by the window, a little apart from the rest. A long coat hung beside the door, too broad in the shoulder for him, and he moved around it without looking, the way you move around furniture you have stopped seeing.

“There are three of them on my street,” he said, ladling soup into a bowl. “Ruffians. They have been trailing you since the courtyard. They will not cross my ward line. They have more sense than courage, thank the Pit.”

“You knew they were there.”

“I know most things that happen within a hundred paces of my door. It is a hobby.” He set the bowl in front of Bloof, then bread, then a glass of water, in that order. Last came a spoon, laid within easy reach of their hand. His own hand withdrew unhurriedly, leaving the next move to them. “Eat.”

Bloof stared at the soup for a long moment.

“This is a trap,” they said.

“It is soup.”

“Nothing’s free here.” Their voice was starting to fray at the edges. “A man offered me a meal for a favor. Open-ended. ‘I’ll tell you when.’ I know how that works. If I eat this, then you—”

“Nothing is owed.”

He said it plainly, without any particular flourish, which made it land harder.

“I mean that in the most literal sense,” he went on. “No favor. No name. No signature. You may eat this, and sleep if you like, and I shall ask for nothing, because I do not want anything from you that you have not freely chosen to give.” The red eyes softened. “I suspect someone has taught you that kindness is a debt in disguise. I am sorry for that. It is not true in this kitchen.”

Bloof’s throat tightened awkwardly.

They ate.

It took about three bites for their hands to stop shaking. About six for them to realize they’d been wolfing the bowl like something feral, and slow down.

Stolas lifted the ladle. Bloof hesitated, then nudged the bowl toward him.

“Thank you,” they said, after the second bowl, and it didn’t come out stoic at all. It came out small. “I don’t—I don’t normally do this. Break into—I’m not someone who—”

“I believe you.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No. But I have been wrong about stranger things.” He settled into the chair across from them. “What do I call you?”

“Bloof.”

“Bloof.” Stolas repeated the name with a small, genuine smile. “I am Stolas. A prince of the Ars Goetia.”

Bloof’s gaze flicked up from the bowl.

“I uh, don’t know what that is.”

“Then Stolas will do.” He folded his hands on the table. “It is a pleasure to meet you.”

He said it slowly, tilting his head a few degrees and pausing before the next sentence, as though he had all the time in the world and intended to spend a little of it on whoever sat in front of him.

Something in Bloof’s chest stopped.

Their tail, which had been flat against the chair leg, twitched once, hard. Their ears lifted. The spoon halted halfway to their mouth.

Stolas noticed. Of course he noticed. Those four red eyes missed nothing.

“Have I said something amiss?”

“No.” It came too fast. Bloof set the spoon down with great care. “No. Sorry. You just—” They stopped, started again. “Sorry. You remind me of someone from home.”

Stolas’s eyes softened, but he didn’t pounce on it.

“Someone you were fond of, I hope.”

“...Yeah.”

“Then I shall try not to ruin the comparison.”

Bloof let out a laugh that cracked halfway through. They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes for exactly one second, then lowered it and went back to the soup, face composed again, ears still not quite down.

Stolas let the silence be a silence. He poured a cup of tea he did not drink and watched the window until the steam thinned.

“Where am I?”

The bowl was empty. The bread was gone. Bloof’s hands, folded on the table, were nearly steady.

“Hell,” said Stolas.

“No, I mean—what city? What district?”

“Imp City. In the Pride Ring of Hell.”

Bloof waited for something in his expression to change.

Nothing did.

“You mean that literally.”

“I do, yes.”

The kitchen went quiet.

“No,” Bloof said. “Hell is—” They stopped, searching his face again. “It’s something from *stories*.”

Stolas tilted his head. “Stories?”

“Fantasy. Places villains get banished to. Monsters, fire, the dead.” Their fingers tightened around each other. “Different books had different versions. It wasn’t somewhere you could actually—”

They looked toward the window. Red light lay across the sill.

“It isn’t a real place. Where I’m from.”

Stolas set his cup down.

“I see. I am afraid it is here.”

Bloof looked back at their hands. They had almost stopped shaking. Food had helped. Water had helped. Neither had made the room any easier to understand.

**Hell**.

A word they’d read safely in bed, with another chapter left and somewhere familiar waiting when they closed the book. Now it was an address.

Their gaze lifted to Stolas.

“You said the dead.”

“You mentioned them, yes. But—”

“Am I dead?” The question came barely above a whisper.

“I do not believe so,” Stolas said. “Being here does not, by itself, mean that you died.”

Bloof drew a breath. It caught against their injured ribs.

“But you don’t know.”

“Not for certain,” he admitted. “I will not pretend otherwise.”

“I _need_ to go back.” The words came faster than they meant. “I need to—there’s two of them. I don’t know how long I’ve been gone. Seris was right next to me, she was _right there_, I closed my eyes for one minute, one minute, and I—”

They stopped. Their jaw worked.

“Sorry,” they said. “Forget it.”

“I would rather not,” said Stolas, gently.

He took a notepad from a drawer, and a pen with a long feathered tip. “Say the names again. Slowly.”

“Seris.” Bloof swallowed. “Seris Daelune. And Bazemir. Bazemir Keraunth.”

The pen paused on the second name. Only for a moment. Stolas did not ask. He wrote it more slowly than the first.

“Beastfolk,” Bloof added, hoarse. “They’re both beastfolk. Like me. It’s what we call ourselves.”

“Beastfolk.” Stolas tried the word like a new wine. “Charming. We have imps and hellborn and sinners, none of whom have ever once agreed on what to call anyone.” He set the pen down. “Do you know how you arrived? What you last remember?”

“I was in bed. I fell asleep.” Bloof’s throat was tight. “There was a… dream. A red place. Something grabbed me.”

For the first time, Stolas’s face went very still.

“Something,” he said.

“I couldn’t see it. It didn’t feel like it wanted to hurt me.” They shook their head. “It felt like it was _holding_ me.”

Stolas was quiet for a long moment. The kettle ticked on the stove.

“That,” he said at last, “is a question for a different night, and I do not propose to answer it tonight. But I shall write it down.” And he did.

“Can you open a portal?” Bloof asked. “You did something to my wrist before I even saw you. You’re a mage. You’ve got a _book_ or—”

“I do.” He lifted one gloved hand. A circle of faint gold light bloomed in the air above the table, wide as a dinner plate, and through it Bloof saw a rooftop, a smear of red sky, a chimney with a pigeon sleeping on it. A window onto somewhere in the same city. “I can open a door to anywhere I have been. A ring. A district. The mortal realm. Anywhere I know.”

The circle shimmered. Bloof’s heart leaped.

“But the door must lead _somewhere,_” Stolas said, quietly. “I cannot step into a place I have never seen. Not to a world I cannot name. If I tried, I would be pointing at nothing, and I do not intend to throw you into the nothing.”

The circle collapsed.

“Yours is not one of them,” Stolas said. “Not yet.”

_Not yet._

It was the first thing anyone had said all day that sounded like a plan.

Bloof pressed the heel of their hand against their sternum, where the tide wouldn’t settle, and breathed. Four in. Hold. Four out. Their eyes burned. They kept their face perfectly composed.

“There is a thing I have to ask you,” Stolas said, after a time, in that same careful tone. “I do not wish to offend. You are a dragon.”

“I’m a dragonborn. And a goat.” A pause. “Some goat.”

“I see.” Something very nearly amused moved at the edge of his beak. “Dragons are exceedingly rare in Hell. I could count the ones I know on one hand, and the last one of any consequence I was introduced to had a throne and a very poor sense of humor. So when a stranger with horns and scales and a rather noble bearing climbs through my kitchen window—”

“It was a door.”

“—door, my apologies, I assumed you were someone’s heir. Or someone’s inconvenient relative.” His gaze flicked briefly to their ears. “Though the fluffy ears did give me pause. Rather endearing.”

Bloof’s ears lifted before they could stop them.

“They’re… the goat part.”

“Ah. That explains it.”

Bloof glanced down at the empty bowl. “Thank you?”

“You are welcome.” Stolas returned his attention to the notepad, apparently content to leave them with that. “In any case, I shall not trouble you about your family.”

“I’m not an heir. There isn’t a house waiting to claim me. I’m just—” They stopped. “I’m trying to get home.”

Stolas’s hands stilled.

“All right,” he said, more quietly. “Then I shall leave it there.”

He stood. The chair scraped. He folded the page with their names in it and set it on a shelf by the window, where the red light fell across it, next to a heavy book that looked like it had opinions.

“You will stay here tonight.”

“I can’t—”

“You are injured, starving, and on a street that appears to have taken an interest in you. I have three empty rooms and a ward on every door. You may close yours. You may lock it. I shall knock before I open it, if I must open it at all.”

_You may close it._

It was such a small thing. A door they could shut.

Something in Bloof’s chest came very close to breaking.

“Okay,” they said. “Thanks.”

“Of course.”

He led them up a staircase, past a hallway of closed doors and one very old portrait that someone had draped with a gray cloth, into a bedroom with a narrow bed, a basin, and a window that looked out on the red-lit rooftops of a city that never seemed to sleep.

“Water is in the basin,” he said from the door. “Food in the morning. If you need anything, there is a bell by the bed. It is rather loud.”

“Is it enchanted?”

“It is _dramatic,_” said Stolas, with dignity, and left.

---

The room had a lock. They checked it twice anyway.

When they unwrapped their forearm, they stopped.

The cut was closing. Not scabbed over. Closing, the edges drawing together, pink and clean, hours ahead of where it ought to be. They flexed their fingers. Less pain than before.

_Cool. Super normal._

They cleaned the wound and wrapped it again.

Then they sat on the edge of the bed with their back straight, because slouching felt like admitting something.

**Bridle**. In through the nose. Hold. Out through the teeth.

Their breath did what it was told.

The heat beneath their sternum remained.

From downstairs came the faint clink of crockery. Bloof listened until the house went quiet, and for a long while after.

Folded hands. A patient pause before a sentence. Their name spoken with a care that had caught them unprepared.

They looked at the empty space beside them.

“I’m fine,” they said quietly.

The second word wavered. They didn’t repeat it.

They lay down carefully, easing the pressure on their ribs. Beyond the curtains, a vehicle passed. Somewhere in the house, a floorboard creaked.

They lay there until the house’s small sounds blurred together. Their eyes closed before they meant them to.

Their breathing lengthened. Their shoulders loosened.

Beneath their ribs, the warmth remained.

> [!lore] Character Note 
> The term **Bridle** refers to Bloof's first form of **Wrath**. It is a calm, controlled, and intentionally limited use of Wrath, which gives minor physical enhancements (speed, strength, regen), enhanced fire abilities (both pyromancy and Dragonfire/Azurefire), as well as improved endurance. There is low immediate risk, but emotional suppression accumulates pressure.

> **Overdrive** refers to Bloof's second form of Wrath. It has stronger physicality, amplified strikes, and even higher firepower. Its attacks also have a form of durability negation. However, extended use can lead to tunnel vision, overheating, reduced restraint, and eventual emotional burnout, leading to a hollow-like state.
> 
> The term **Stillhold** refers to a pocket dimension available to Bloof using **Wrath**. It is a small, contained area in which they can store items, such as their greatsword/scythe, **Kaleidos**, as well as their gauntlets, **Vein and Marrow**.
